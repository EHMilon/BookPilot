There's a question that haunts the second half of life: *I climbed the first mountain — career, status, achievement — so why does the view from the top feel empty?* David Brooks spent his career as one of America's most prominent public intellectuals chronicling the first mountain — ambition, résumé-building, the transactional game of success. Then life cracked open. His marriage ended. He found himself asking what he'd spent decades avoiding. *The Second Mountain* is what came out of that crack — not a sequel to his earlier work, but a quiet repudiation of it.

Brooks's earlier *The Road to Character* was about individual virtue. This book is about the structure that makes virtue possible: commitment. Deep, costly, permanent commitment to people, causes, and communities. The first mountain teaches you to build a self. The second mountain teaches you to give it away.

---

Brooks opens with a distinction that structures the entire book. The first mountain is the mountain of ego. You climb it to establish yourself — career, reputation, accomplishments, a self you can present to the world. The values of the first mountain are the ones our culture celebrates: independence, achievement, self-expression.

The second mountain is the mountain of relationship and commitment. You climb it not to succeed but to belong. The values shift from independence to interdependence, from achieving to serving, from being known to truly knowing others.

The counterintuitive claim: you can't skip the first mountain. Brooks tried to write a book about the second mountain without having climbed the first, and he admits it would have been hollow. The person who denies their own ambition hasn't transcended ego — they've just repressed it. You have to have a self before you can give it away.

**The Trap of Perpetual First-Mountain-Climbing:** Brooks is sharp on this. Our culture doesn't just tolerate first-mountain values — it rewards them endlessly. There is always another promotion, another deal, another accolade. You can spend your entire life on the first mountain and die "successful" by every external measure. The trap is that the first mountain's rewards diminish with repetition. The same achievement that thrilled you at 25 feels hollow at 45. But the structure of your life keeps pushing you up the same slope.

> *"The first mountain is about building your identity and separating from your parents. The second mountain is about dissolving your identity and uniting with others."*

Brooks argues that a meaningful life is built on four mountains of commitment, each corresponding to a fundamental human need:

**1. Vocation — A committed life includes meaningful work.** Not any work, and not just "doing what you love." Brooks means work that serves others and connects you to a purpose larger than your paycheck. He profiles people who turned down prestigious jobs to serve communities, build institutions, or pursue craft for its own sake. The shift is from a "career" (what you do for yourself) to a "vocation" (what you do for the world).

**2. Marriage — A committed life includes a profound partnership.** Brooks is surprisingly traditional here, arguing for an understanding of marriage as a covenant rather than a contract. Contracts are conditional — "I'll stay as long as my needs are met." Covenants are unconditional — "I'll stay because I promised." The difference, Brooks argues, is the difference between a relationship based on exchange and one based on total gift.

**3. Philosophy and Faith — A committed life includes a moral framework.** Brooks traces the history of moral thought from the "moral ecology" of traditional communities to the radical individualism of modern culture. His argument is that we've liberated ourselves from oppressive moral structures and replaced them with nothing. The result is not freedom but drift.

**4. Community — A committed life includes belonging to a place and a people.** This is the chapter that feels most urgent. Brooks argues that we've systematically destroyed the institutions of local community — churches, clubs, neighborhood associations — and replaced them with online networks that simulate but don't deliver belonging. The result is the epidemic of loneliness that's been well-documented elsewhere, but Brooks connects it to the loss of *moral* community — places where you're known, held accountable, and supported.

> *"Freedom isn't the absence of commitments. Freedom is the right to choose the commitments that will bind you for life."*

The most emotionally raw section of the book is Brooks's description of the valley between the two mountains. He calls it "the wilderness" — a period of dislocation, failure, or loss that shatters your first-mountain identity. For Brooks, it was divorce. For others, it's illness, career failure, a child's suffering, or just the accumulated weight of emptiness.

The wilderness is essential. You can't walk around it. If you try, you'll find yourself climbing the first mountain again, just with more determination. The only way to reach the second mountain is to *let go* of the first mountain's definition of success. And letting go requires being broken first.

Brooks describes his own wilderness with a vulnerability that's rare in this genre. He doesn't frame it as a triumphant transformation — "I failed, then I learned, now I'm better." He describes the confusion, the shame, the sense of being untethered. He spent months just sitting in his apartment, reading, not knowing who he was anymore.

**The story that stopped me cold:** Brooks tells the story of a man named Art who ran a homeless shelter in New York. Art had been a successful lawyer who lost everything — marriage, career, money — and ended up living in his car. He found his way to a church that ran a shelter, started volunteering, and eventually became its director. When Brooks asked him what changed, Art said: "I stopped trying to be somebody and started trying to be of use." That sentence — "trying to be of use" — is the second mountain in six words.

> *"To be of use — that's the whole thing. Not to be admired. Not to be successful. To be useful."*

Brooks devotes a section of the book to what he calls "weavers" — people who spend their lives building the social fabric. Not famous people. Not powerful people. People who start community gardens, lead local Scout troops, organize neighborhood watch programs, run small religious congregations.

He argues that these people are the real heroes of society, and we systematically overlook them. We celebrate the disruptors and the innovators. We ignore the people holding communities together. The weaver's work is invisible, unglamorous, and essential.

Brooks profiles a woman named Donna who runs a community center in a poor neighborhood in Washington D.C. She doesn't have a grand strategy or a TED talk. She just shows up, every day, for decades, and knows everyone by name. When a kid is in trouble, Donna is the one they call — not the government program, not the school system. Because Donna is *committed*, not just employed.

Brooks writes like a columnist — ideas are sharp, but development is sometimes shallow. The second mountain is a beautiful metaphor, and the four commitments structure is useful, but the book reads more like a collection of essays than a unified argument. Chapters on marriage and faith feel disconnected from each other. The through-line is emotional rather than logical.

The book is also written from a position of obvious privilege. Brooks's "wilderness" was a divorce that, while painful, happened to a wealthy, famous, white man with an extensive support network. He's aware of this but doesn't fully grapple with it. The second mountain is easier to climb when you've already conquered the first.

More critically, the book's prescriptions are vague. The diagnosis is powerful — we're lonely, uncommitted, adrift. But the solution is "make deeper commitments" — which is true but not actionable. *How* do you make deeper commitments when your entire life is structured around flexibility and exit options? The book offers more diagnosis than cure.

If you're in your 30s or 40s and starting to feel that the things you've been chasing aren't delivering what you expected, this book will name that feeling better than almost anything else. If you're younger, the first mountain may still feel compelling — and that's fine. Come back to it when the view from the top starts to disappoint. Skip it if you're looking for step-by-step guidance. This is a book of orientation, not instruction.

That phrase — "to be of use." I keep coming back to it. It's so much humbler than "find your passion" or "pursue your purpose." Being of use doesn't require grand ambition. It requires showing up. It requires staying. It requires the willingness to be ordinary in service of something important. That reframe has changed how I think about my own work. Not "what can I achieve?" but "how can I be useful?"

And the weavers. I started noticing them after reading this book. The person who organizes the office potluck. The neighbor who checks on the elderly woman next door. The person who volunteers at the local library. These people aren't achieving anything that would look good on a résumé. They're holding the world together.

1. **The commitment audit.** List the five most important commitments in your life. Next to each, write: contract (conditional, based on what I get) or covenant (unconditional, based on who I am). Be honest. Identify one contract you could move toward covenant.

2. **Find a weaver.** Identify one person in your community who quietly holds things together — not a famous leader, just someone who shows up. Thank them. Ask how you can help.

3. **The wilderness check.** Are you avoiding a valley? Is there an area of your life where you know the first-mountain frame isn't working, but you're still grinding? Name it on paper. You don't have to solve it today. But stop pretending it's not there.
