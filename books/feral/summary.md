Here's a thought experiment that will change how you see the British countryside — and possibly every landscape you've ever walked through. What if the rolling green hills, the neat hedgerows, the sheep-dotted pastures that we think of as "the English countryside" are not a natural landscape at all, but a desert? An ecological wasteland, stripped of virtually every large animal that once lived there, maintained by grazing and burning and culling, and utterly bankrupt compared to what was there before humans arrived. What if the countryside we love is a ghost?

That's George Monbiot's argument in *Feral*, and it's one of the most radical environmental books of the century so far. Monbiot is a journalist, an activist, and one of Britain's most controversial environmental thinkers — he's been called an eco-terrorist (he's not) and a romantic fantasist (he might be, but that's not the insult his critics think it is). *Feral* is his manifesto for what he calls "rewilding": the large-scale restoration of ecosystems to a state where natural processes — predation, flooding, forest regeneration — are allowed to function without human management. It's a book about bringing the wild back, and it's as much a philosophical argument as an ecological one.

> "We have created a world that is safe for humans and dangerous for everything else. That's not a triumph. That's a tragedy."

The book's most powerful chapter is about **trophic cascades** — the chain reaction that occurs when a top predator is reintroduced to an ecosystem. Monbiot uses the famous Yellowstone wolf reintroduction as his anchor. In 1995, wolves were released into Yellowstone National Park after a 70-year absence. The result was not just a wolf recovery — it was a landscape transformation. The wolves killed elk, which had been overgrazing the park's willows and aspens. With fewer elk, the trees recovered. With more trees, songbirds returned. Beaver dams followed (beavers need willow). The dams created new wetlands. The wetlands attracted amphibians, insects, fish. The rivers changed course — literally — because the regenerated riverbanks held the soil, reducing erosion. All of this from a single species: a trophic cascade that rippled through the entire ecosystem.

Monbiot's point is that we have no idea what we're missing. The British landscape has been so thoroughly managed for so long (10,000 years of human habitation) that we've forgotten what a fully functioning ecosystem looks like. We think a hillside covered in heather is natural. It's not — it's a grazing landscape maintained by sheep, who eat tree seedlings, preventing forest regeneration. The "natural" state of most of Britain would be temperate rainforest, with bears, wolves, lynx, wild boar, beavers, and aurochs (the wild ancestor of cattle). We've removed them all and then called the result "the countryside."

**The Trap** is the idea that conservation means preserving what we have. Monbiot calls this "the fallacy of the baseline" — we assume that the state of nature we grew up with (or saw in photographs from the 19th century) is the "natural" state, and our job is to protect it. But that baseline is arbitrary. It's a snapshot of a landscape that was already deeply degraded. True conservation, Monbiot argues, isn't about preserving the present. It's about restoring the past — or at least creating the conditions for a future that nature builds itself.

> "Conservation should not be a looking glass into a lost world. It should be an engine for creating a new one."

Monbiot explores **rewilding projects around the world** with the enthusiasm of a man who has found his cause. The Oostvaardersplassen in the Netherlands — a reclaimed polder where wild horses, deer, and cattle roam without any human intervention, dying of starvation in winter if that's what nature chooses. The Knepp estate in West Sussex, where a conventional farm was transformed into a rewilding project and saw an explosion of biodiversity — nightingales, turtle doves, purple emperor butterflies, species that had vanished from the surrounding countryside. The Iberian lynx reintroduction in Spain. The beaver reintroductions in Scotland. Each project is different, but they share a common principle: stop managing, stop intervening, let nature figure it out.

The **marine rewilding** section is the most surprising part of the book. Monbiot describes the destruction of the UK's seabed by industrial trawling — a practice that drags heavy nets across the ocean floor, crushing everything in their path. The result is an underwater desert. But in a few places where trawling has been banned — like the Lyme Bay marine reserve — the recovery has been astonishing within just a few years. Pink sea fans, spider crabs, scallops, fish species that hadn't been seen in decades. The ocean wants to be wild, Monbiot argues. It's waiting for us to stop.

One of the book's most personal sections is about Monbiot's own relationship with wildness. He describes canoeing alone through the Scottish wilderness, feeling a fear that he realizes is a kind of atrophied instinct — the fear of the large, toothy, potentially dangerous world that no longer exists in Britain. He describes the sensation of walking through a landscape that could contain a bear, which is different from walking through a landscape that definitely doesn't. The difference is *aliveness*. Even the *possibility* of danger makes the landscape more vivid. He argues that this is one of the unseen costs of ecological simplification: we've made the world safer and lost the capacity for wonder.

> "The opposite of wonder is not cynicism. It's safety."

> "A wild world is not just a nice thing to have. It is a fundamental part of who we are. We cannot flourish in a world that has been stripped of the non-human."

This is the book's moral argument — not utilitarian, but existential.

One of Monbiot's most affecting chapters is called **"The Loss of Wildness"** — a personal essay in which he describes the extinction of his own capacity for awe. He grew up in rural Oxfordshire, spent his childhood exploring woods and streams, and gradually realized that what he thought was nature was really a managed landscape. The loss he describes is not just ecological but psychological: he's lost the capacity to be surprised by a landscape, to feel a thrill of danger, to encounter something truly Other. Rewilding, for Monbiot, is not just about restoring ecosystems. It's about restoring that capacity for wonder. "We need wildness," he writes, "not because it's useful, but because it's the source of our deepest joy." The chapter gives the book its emotional weight — it's not just an environmental argument, it's a confession of what we've sacrificed.

**Criticism:** Monbiot is a polemicist, and *Feral* shows the strengths and weaknesses of that mode. The book is compelling as a call to action, but it's less convincing as a plan. Monbiot acknowledges that rewilding requires land — lots of it — in a crowded island that's already using most of its territory for agriculture. He's vague about the political and economic transitions required to make rewilding mainstream. The book's emotional core is also vulnerable to the accusation of "shifting baseline nostalgia" — Monbiot points out that our baseline is wrong, but his own baseline (temperate rainforest with wolves and bears) is also a choice, not an objective standard. Some ecologists have argued that his vision is as arbitrary as the one he criticizes. And the book's tone occasionally tips into a kind of eco-masculine energy — the wild is dangerous, the wild is powerful, the wild is something we should fear. It's a very male vision of nature, and it sits uneasily alongside other traditions of nature writing (like Nan Shepherd's *The Living Mountain*) that find wildness in gentler registers.

**What stuck with me** is the trophic cascade concept applied to human psychology. If you restore predators, the entire ecosystem reorganizes. Monbiot believes the same is true for human culture: if you restore a relationship with the wild, everything else — our politics, our economy, our sense of self — reorganizes around it. The idea is almost too big to hold, but it's the book's deepest argument: that our disconnection from wildness is not just an ecological problem but a spiritual one, and that the solution to both is the same.
