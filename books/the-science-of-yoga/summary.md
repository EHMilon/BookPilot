The first time William J. Broad — two-time Pulitzer winner at *The New York Times* — takes a yoga class, he's covering it as a science reporter. He spends the session in the back of the room, taking notes, skeptical in the way only a journalist who's seen too many pseudoscience claims can be. But then the teacher says something that stays lodged in his brain for years: *Yoga can kill you.* Not "yoga can harm you." Kill you. That sentence becomes the seed of *The Science of Yoga*, a book that does something almost no one in the yoga world had done before: it takes the claims of the $80-billion-a-year yoga industry and runs them through the cold, indifferent machinery of scientific inquiry. The results are uncomfortable, surprising, and sometimes genuinely beautiful.

Broad's thesis is simple and provocative. Yoga has been sold as an ancient science whose benefits have been validated by centuries of practice. But when you actually look at the data, much of what yoga practitioners believe is unsupported, exaggerated, or outright wrong. And conversely, some of yoga's most profound benefits were discovered entirely by accident — hiding in studies that were designed to study something else entirely.

The single most destabilizing idea in the book is this: most of what modern yoga teaches about the history of the practice is fiction. Broad traces the roots of contemporary postural yoga to a surprisingly recent source — the early 20th century, when Indian reformers like Krishnamacharya and his students (including B.K.S. Iyengar and K. Pattabhi Jois) systematically merged British calisthenics, Scandinavian gymnastics, and a handful of traditional Indian postures into something they could present as an ancient tradition. The deeper truth: yoga's famous poses — downward dog, headstand, the lotus — have almost no documented history before the 1700s, and many were invented wholesale in the 20th century.

The Trap here is reactionary dismissal. When you learn that "5,000 years of tradition" is largely a marketing invention, it's easy to conclude the whole thing is a scam. Broad doesn't go there. He argues that understanding yoga's real history — messy, syncretic, and recent — actually makes it *more* interesting, not less. The practice isn't less valuable because it was assembled in Mysore in the 1930s. It's just less mystical.

This is the section that made Broad's book controversial. He dedicates significant space to the risks of yoga, particularly the risk of stroke from certain neck positions in headstand, shoulder stand, and even simple forward bends. The mechanism is plausible: extreme neck flexion can damage the vertebral arteries, leading to dissection and stroke in young, otherwise healthy practitioners. Broad cites a study from *Neurology* that found 14% of strokes in young adults were linked to yoga or chiropractic manipulation.

He also examines yoga's impact on fertility (some evidence suggests intense practice reduces testosterone in men and disrupts menstrual cycles in women), and the risk of overstretching (loose ligaments can lead to joint instability, especially in hypermobile women who are drawn to yoga). These aren't reasons to stop practicing — they're reasons to practice intelligently, with awareness of individual risk factors.

One of the more uncomfortable chapters investigates yoga's relationship with sexuality. Broad examines the traditional claim that celibacy (brahmacharya) enhances spiritual power, then cross-references it with modern studies showing that intense yoga practice can lower testosterone in men and disrupt menstrual cycles in women. The ancient yogis, he suggests, were not wrong — sexual abstinence combined with intense physical practice does have physiological effects. But the claim that this is spiritually beneficial is a value judgment, not a scientific finding. Broad handles this with typical journalistic distance, but the implication is provocative: some of yoga's most cherished spiritual doctrines may be products of their culture's relationship to sex, not universal truths.

And now, the turn. Because Broad isn't a hit piece writer. He's a science journalist, and the science on yoga's benefits is genuinely compelling when you separate it from the hype.

The most important discovery in the book, for my money, is about the parasympathetic nervous system. Broad walks through the research showing that slow, deep breathing — the kind central to pranayama — directly activates the vagus nerve, the body's brake pedal. This isn't mystical. It's measurable. Heart rate variability increases. Cortisol drops. Inflammation markers decrease. The same effect that meditation claims is confirmed here through breathing alone, with no belief required.

Then there's the unexpected: yoga's impact on athletic performance. Broad profiles studies showing that yogic breathing techniques (particularly "breath of fire" and "alternate nostril breathing") improved lung capacity in elite athletes more than traditional cardio training. Swimmers who practiced pranayama reduced their race times. The mechanism seems to be improved carbon dioxide tolerance — teaching the body to stay calm under the suffocating sensation of oxygen debt.

The book's most delightful story involves a researcher named Herbert Benson, who in the 1970s studied practitioners of Transcendental Meditation and discovered what he called the "relaxation response" — a physiological state opposite to the fight-or-flight response. Benson was a Harvard cardiologist, not a yoga teacher. He stumbled onto one of yoga's core claims (that the body can be trained to relax at will) and proved it with blood pressure cuffs and EEG machines. Broad uses Benson's story to make a larger point: the most important discoveries about yoga came from scientists who were skeptical of yoga. The believers just assumed the benefits without testing them.

The book's biggest weakness is that Broad treats science with too much reverence. He acknowledges that yoga research is often poorly designed — small sample sizes, no control groups, publication bias toward positive results — but he doesn't always follow the implications of that critique far enough. Some of the studies he cites as evidence are exactly the kind of weak studies he warns about earlier. The structure also loses steam in the middle chapters, where the investigation becomes a catalog of studies rather than a narrative. And Broad's tone, while admirably detached, sometimes reads as if he's writing about a lab specimen rather than a practice that millions of people experience as genuinely transformative.

*The Science of Yoga* also has a blind spot around the psychological and spiritual dimensions of the practice. Broad is clearly uncomfortable with anything that can't be measured, which means he misses the whole category of benefit that people describe as "I just feel better when I practice" — the kind of subjective improvement that science can't capture but that remains the primary reason most people actually do yoga.
