Imagine a man who never says no. Who anticipates everyone's needs before they ask. Who apologizes when he hasn't done anything wrong. Who is terrified of conflict, of disapproval, of being seen as selfish. On the surface, he's a dream — accommodating, generous, agreeable. Underneath, he's resentful, exhausted, and secretly furious that his niceness hasn't earned him the love, respect, or sex he was promised. This is the Nice Guy, and Robert Glover wrote this book to name him, diagnose him, and — mercifully — propose a cure.

Glover is a therapist who spent decades treating men who came in with depression, anxiety, relationship problems, and sexual dysfunction. What they all shared was a pattern: they were "nice" in a way that wasn't genuine. Their niceness was a *strategy* — a covert contract to get their needs met without taking the risk of asking directly. Glover doesn't pull punches. He calls this a pathology. And the first step is admitting that being nice is not a virtue. It's a coping mechanism.

Glover defines the Nice Guy Syndrome as a belief system rooted in a core fear: *"If I am my authentic self, people won't accept me."* This fear drives every Nice Guy behavior:

- Seeking approval from everyone, especially authority figures and women
- Avoiding conflict at any cost
- Hiding their own needs, opinions, and flaws
- Trying to fix other people's problems
- Doing more than their share and resenting it
- Having difficulty setting boundaries
- Being disconnected from their anger

The Nice Guy isn't really nice. He's *performing* niceness. And the performance is exhausting.

*"The Nice Guy's most profound belief is that he must become what he thinks others want him to be in order to be loved, accepted, and safe."*

**The Trap** of reading this is thinking "well I'm not that bad." The Nice Guy defense is so deeply ingrained that most Nice Guys don't recognize themselves. They genuinely believe they're just good people who are unfairly mistreated. The giveaway? If you find yourself thinking about how much you do for others and how little you get back, that resentment is the signal.

This is the single most useful concept in the book. A covert contract is an unspoken agreement: "If I do X for you, you'll do Y for me." The Nice Guy does something nice — listens to his wife vent, covers a coworker's shift, helps a friend move — and expects something in return: appreciation, sex, respect, reciprocation. But he never *communicates* the expectation. So when it's not met, he feels cheated. The other person doesn't even know a deal was struck.

Covert contracts poison relationships because they create a ledger of unspoken debts. The Nice Guy keeps score. Everyone else thinks the game is over.

*"A covert contract is an agreement in which one party — the Nice Guy — agrees to hold up his end of the bargain without ever telling the other person what the bargain is."*

The solution is brutal in its simplicity: either ask for what you want, or don't expect anything in return. Stop doing nice things you don't actually want to do. If you're helping because you want credit, you're not helping — you're investing.

Glover traces the Nice Guy syndrome to childhood. Most Nice Guys had parents (usually mothers) who withheld affection or approval unless the boy performed correctly. "Be a good boy." "Don't make a fuss." "What will people think?" The boy learns that love is conditional on behavior. He learns to suppress his authentic self and present a sanitized version.

As an adult, this manifests as a desperate need for approval from others — especially women. The Nice Guy polls the room constantly. He asks permission for everything. He can't make a decision without checking with someone. His self-worth is on a drip-feed from external validation, which means he's always in deficit.

The antidote is what Glover calls "giving up the need for approval." This doesn't mean being an asshole. It means accepting that *you cannot control what people think of you*. Some people will disapprove no matter what you do. Let them. The goal is not to be liked. The goal is to be authentic, and let the chips fall.

Nice Guys are disconnected from their anger. They've learned that anger is bad, that angry men are scary, that showing anger will get them rejected. So they suppress it. But suppressed anger doesn't disappear — it leaks. It comes out as passive-aggression, sarcasm, silent treatment, fatigue, or depression.

Glover insists that anger is not the problem. Suppressed anger is. The healthy man can feel anger, express it cleanly (without attacking), and let it pass. Anger is a signal — it tells you when a boundary has been crossed, when you've been disrespected, when something matters to you.

*"The goal is not to get rid of your anger. The goal is to use your anger as information and to express it in a way that doesn't harm others."*

The practice: When you feel angry, pause. Don't suppress it and don't explode. Ask: "What boundary has been crossed? What do I need?" Then communicate that. Cleanly. Directly. Without apology.

This is the chapter that made the book controversial and the chapter that men need most. Glover argues that Nice Guys struggle in the bedroom not because they're bad lovers but because they're *too focused on being good lovers*. They're trying to perform. They're checking: "Is she enjoying this? Am I doing it right?" They can't relax into their own pleasure because they're too busy managing her experience.

The result: sex becomes work. The Nice Guy does to get done to him. He gives pleasure to earn pleasure. He can't receive without first performing.

Glover's prescription: stop trying to be a good lover. Instead, be a *present* lover. Focus on your own pleasure. Communicate what you want. Don't fake enthusiasm. The paradox is that when a man stops trying to please and instead shows up authentically, women find him *more* attractive. Because genuine desire is hot. Performative niceness is not.

Glover doesn't offer a cure — he offers a recovery model, like AA. The Nice Guy syndrome is a deeply ingrained pattern that will re-emerge under stress. The goal is not perfection but awareness. The acronym he suggests for the recovery attitude: **STOP** being nice — Skill, Truth, Openness, Playfulness.

- **Skill** — develop real competence at something. Stop trying to be loved. Start being *useful*.
- **Truth** — tell the truth, especially when it's uncomfortable. Stop editing yourself.
- **Openness** — let people see you. Your flaws, your needs, your boundaries. Vulnerability is attractive.
- **Playfulness** — take yourself less seriously. Stop treating life as a performance review.

The central truth of this book is that the Nice Guy is not really nice — he's *fearful*. His niceness is a strategy for avoiding rejection and disapproval. It doesn't work. It produces resentment, emotional distance, and unfulfilling relationships. The alternative is not to become a jerk but to become a *whole* man — one who can be both kind and direct, generous and self-protective, loving and honest.

The covert contract concept changed how I see every interaction that leaves me feeling resentful. Anytime I catch myself thinking "I did X for them and they didn't Y back," I know I made a covert contract. The fix isn't to confront them — it's to stop making secret deals. If I want something, I ask. If I don't have the courage to ask, I do the thing without expectation. The resentment is never their fault. It's mine, for not being honest about what I wanted.

The book is repetitive — Glover makes the same point about covert contracts about fifteen times. The writing is functional, not beautiful. Some of the clinical framing feels dated (the chapter on mothers borders on blaming single mothers). The book also leans into a heteronormative, marriage-centric view that may not resonate with single men or men in non-traditional relationships. And the "just stop being nice" framing, while catchy, oversimplifies — the real work is about unlearning childhood survival strategies, not just flipping a switch.
