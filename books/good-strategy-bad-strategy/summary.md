Most of what passes for strategy isn't. It's a wish list. A budget. A set of aspirational goals dressed up in PowerPoint. "We will grow revenue 20% year over year." "We will become the market leader in our space." "We will be the most innovative company in our industry." None of these are strategies. They're intentions. And they're useless.

Richard Rumelt has spent decades teaching strategy at UCLA and advising organizations from startups to the Pentagon. He's seen bad strategy everywhere — in governments, in Fortune 500 companies, in nonprofits. He defines it precisely: a strategy is a **coherent set of actions** designed to overcome a specific challenge. If you can't name the challenge, you don't have a strategy.

> "Good strategy is not just 'what' you are trying to do. It is the 'how' and the 'why' — a set of actions that address the critical challenge facing the organization."

Most organizations confuse strategy with ambition. A list of goals is not a strategy. A budget is not a strategy. A mission statement is not a strategy. Real strategy is uncomfortable because it requires *choice* — choosing to do some things and, just as importantly, choosing not to do others. Ambition is safe. Choice requires courage.

Rumelt identifies the **kernel of strategy** — three essential components that every good strategy must have:

1. **A diagnosis** — what's the real challenge?
2. **A guiding policy** — what's our approach to overcoming it?
3. **Coherent actions** — what will we actually do?

Without all three, you don't have a strategy. You have a wish.

He tells the story of **President John F. Kennedy's** response to the Cuban Missile Crisis as a model of good strategy. The challenge wasn't "the existence of Soviet missiles in Cuba" — that was the situation. The real challenge was: "How do we remove the missiles without triggering a nuclear war or appearing weak to the Soviets?" The diagnosis shaped everything. The guiding policy: a naval quarantine — not an invasion, not a bombing, not diplomacy alone. The coherent actions: specific ships, specific rules of engagement, specific communications with the Kremlin. Every part of the strategy flowed from a clear understanding of the *actual* challenge.

Teams spend most of their time on the "actions" part — what are we going to do? — without doing the hard work of diagnosis. They skip straight to solutions because diagnosis requires admitting uncomfortable truths: the problem might be us, our assumptions might be wrong, our previous strategy might have failed for reasons we don't want to face. Good diagnosis requires brutal honesty.

Rumelt devotes a significant portion of the book to exposing **bad strategy** and why it's so common. Bad strategy has four hallmarks:

Empty words that sound strategic but say nothing. "Our strategy is to be customer-centric, innovation-driven, and people-focused." Translation: we don't have a strategy.

If you can't name the obstacle you're trying to overcome, your strategy is hollow. Rumelt points to a major US bank in the 2000s that announced it would "become the best financial services company." The problem? They weren't the best at anything — they were a middling player in a crowded market. They were competing against Bank of America, Citigroup, and Wells Fargo. What was the specific challenge? They never said. They just set a goal and called it strategy.

"We will grow 15% this year." Okay, how? What's the approach? What are you going to stop doing? What trade-offs are you making? Without those answers, you're not strategizing — you're hoping.

Objectives that can't be achieved because they're impossible, contradictory, or disconnected from resources. A small company can't simultaneously "dominate three new markets" and "achieve 50% margins" on a startup budget. That's not strategy. That's fantasy.

> "Bad strategy is not just the absence of good strategy. It is a specific type of thinking — a misdiagnosis of the problem, a failure to choose, or a retreat into fluff."

Organizations reward bad strategy because it doesn't make anyone uncomfortable. Setting a big goal makes everyone feel energized. Admitting that you need to choose between two good options makes people frustrated. A CEO who says "we're going to grow 20%" gets applause. A CEO who says "we're going to exit three product lines to focus on the one where we can win" gets resistance. Bad strategy is a path of least resistance.

Rumelt introduces the concept of **proximate objectives** — goals that are close enough to be achievable but ambitious enough to matter. He uses the example of a high-tech startup that realized its real challenge wasn't building a better product — it was reaching profitability before running out of cash. The guiding policy: stop trying to conquer the entire market and focus on the one vertical where they had the strongest competitive advantage. The actions: fire the sales team targeting enterprise accounts, focus marketing on the specific industry where they had existing traction, cut non-core features from the product roadmap.

The mistake most organizations make: they pick objectives that are too far away. "We want to be the leading platform in our space." That's not an objective — it's a dream. An objective is: "We want to get 500 active paying customers in the healthcare vertical within 12 months, and we'll do it by focusing on clinics with 10-50 employees in the northeastern US."

Another powerful concept: **leverage**. Good strategy concentrates resources at the point of maximum opportunity. Rumelt tells the story of **the 1975 Battle of Dien Bien Phu** from the French perspective — not to glorify war, but to show the opposite: the French dispersed their forces in a way that made them vulnerable, while the Vietnamese concentrated theirs at the decisive point. Business version: a small software company had limited engineering resources. Their competition was spreading 50 engineers across five product lines. The small company put all 15 engineers into one product. They couldn't compete across the board, but they could build a better product in one niche. That's leverage — finding where a concentrated effort creates disproportionate advantage.

Most organizations respond to competition by trying to do *everything*. "They have feature X? We need feature X too." This is death by parity. Real strategy means deciding what *not* to do. If everything is a priority, nothing is. If you can't name what you're giving up, you haven't made a strategic choice.

> "The kernel of a strategy contains three elements: a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent action."

Rumelt also discusses the role of **design** in strategy. A good strategy is like a good architectural design — it's a coherent system where all the parts fit together. The marketing approach supports the product strategy, which supports the distribution strategy, which supports the growth strategy. They're not independent decisions. They're parts of a whole.

He contrasts this with the typical approach: marketing decides on a message, product decides on features, sales decides on targets, and nobody asks whether these decisions reinforce or undermine each other. The result is a messy, incoherent set of activities that may all be individually reasonable but collectively don't add up to an advantage.

> "Strategy is about being different. It means deliberately choosing a different set of activities to deliver a unique mix of value."

**The idea I couldn't shake** was Rumelt's insistence that you can't have a good strategy without a clear diagnosis of the problem. I realized that most of my own "strategic plans" were solution-first: I started with what I wanted to do, then looked for problems that matched my solution. Rumelt's framework forces you to reverse the process. Start with the problem. Diagnose it honestly. Then — and only then — design a response. It sounds obvious, but it's the opposite of how most organizations operate. We love our solutions. We hate admitting we're not sure what the problem really is.

The book is dense and academic in places. Rumelt is a professor, and it shows. The concepts are brilliant, but the writing can be arid — it lacks the narrative energy of Sinek or the relatable fables of Lencioni. Readers looking for a quick, inspirational read will struggle.

The examples are also heavily tilted toward military history and large corporations. A small business owner or startup founder might find it hard to map the lessons of the Cuban Missile Crisis to their decision about whether to launch a new product line. More small-scale, modern examples would help.

And there's a tension at the heart of the book: Rumelt argues that good strategy is rare and difficult, but he also insists that it's "a craft, not a science." If it's not teachable as a methodology, how much can a book really help?
