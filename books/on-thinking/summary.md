*On Thinking* is not one voice but a conversation — a collection of thinkers across disciplines who all circle the same question: what does it actually *mean* to think? Not problem-solving. Not calculating. Not the idle chatter of the mind. But the genuine, effortful, sometimes painful act of engaging with an idea until it reveals something you didn't put there yourself. The book is less a unified argument and more a mosaic, and that's precisely the point — because thinking, these authors collectively argue, is not a single faculty but a family of activities that resist tidy definition.

The volume draws from philosophy (Heidegger, Wittgenstein, Arendt), psychology (Vygotsky, Piaget), neuroscience, and even contemplative traditions. The throughline is the insistence that most of what we call thinking is actually something else: pattern-matching, emotional reaction, social compliance, or the mere retrieval of stored opinions. Real thinking, the book suggests, is rarer and stranger than we admit.

The most bracing section of the book comes from the philosophers who distinguish thinking from *calculation*. Heidegger's concept of "meditative thinking" (*Besinnung*) versus "calculative thinking" (*Rechnen*) is the axis the book turns on. Calculative thinking is what we do all day: figuring out the fastest route, optimizing a workflow, planning a conversation. It's useful. It's also not what Heidegger (or this book) means by thinking.

Genuine thinking, in this framework, is what happens when you encounter something that *resists* your usual mental tools — a problem that doesn't yield to optimization, a question that can't be answered by gathering more data. "Calculative thinking races from one possibility to the next," the book notes, paraphrasing Heidegger. "Meditative thinking lingers. It does not seek closure. It dwells with the question."

The Trap is obvious: we've built a culture that worships calculative thinking and has almost no space for the meditative kind. Productivity culture frames lingering as laziness. Speed is competence. decisiveness is intelligence. The book quietly suggests this is not just a loss of depth — it's a loss of the capacity to think about anything that truly matters, because the things that truly matter (death, meaning, justice, love) don't yield to optimization.

Arendt's presence looms over the collection. Her concept of the "banality of evil" is reframed here not as a claim about evil people but about the *absence of thinking*. Arendt's Eichmann wasn't a monster; he was someone who stopped thinking, who adopted the language and categories of his system without examination. The book's contributors push this further: thoughtlessness, they argue, is the default state. Thinking is the exception.

This lands differently after 2020. The book's exploration of how propaganda works — not by convincing people of lies but by making thinking unnecessary — feels uncomfortably current. When a slogan replaces a sentence, when an identity replaces an argument, you're not thinking. You're performing membership. "The opposite of thinking is not ignorance," one contributor writes. "It is certainty."

The scientists in the collection offer a different angle. They're interested in what the brain actually does during what we experience as "thought." The default mode network — the brain's resting state — is surprisingly active during creative insight. The more you "try" to think, the less original the result. This is the incubation effect, known since Wallas's 1926 model of creativity, but the neuroimaging makes it visceral: the brain's most creative moments happen when you stop trying.

The book connects this to the *diffuse mode* of thinking popularized by Oakley's *A Mind for Numbers* — but goes deeper. The implication is that our educational and professional systems are structurally anti-thinking. They demand the focused mode, the quick answer, the visible output. The diffuse mode, where real insight lives, is treated as a luxury or a distraction. "We have built a world," the book concludes, "in which the conditions for genuine thought are almost entirely absent."

The book was written before the current AI boom, but its diagnosis of thoughtlessness finds its most urgent application in our present moment. The algorithms that curate our information don't just distract us — they replace thinking with *processing*. They optimize for engagement, not truth. And engagement comes from the brain's pattern-matching system, which prefers the familiar over the true.

Several contributors address what they call "the outsourcing of thought" — the gradual transfer of cognitive labor to systems that are faster but shallower. When a GPS replaces your sense of direction, you don't just lose the ability to navigate; you lose the *cognitive maps* that spatial reasoning builds. When a recommendation engine replaces your curiosity, you don't just lose discovery; you lose the muscle of wonder.

The book's argument is subtle here. It's not Luddite — it doesn't say technology is bad for thinking. It says that every tool carries a cognitive price, and we rarely account for it. "Every convenience is a loss of practice," one contributor writes. "The question is not whether the convenience is worth it. The question is: what are you no longer practicing?"

The most practical section for me was the treatment of Vygotsky's work on *inner speech*. Thinking, Vygotsky argued, is internalized conversation. We learn to think by learning to talk, and our inner voice is a condensed, private version of social dialogue. The implication is that the quality of your thinking is directly related to the quality of the conversations you've internalized. If you surround yourself with shallow discourse, your inner speech will be shallow. If you read complex, careful thinkers, your inner voice gains texture and precision.

This reframes the value of reading and conversation entirely. You're not just acquiring information — you're populating your inner speech with better conversation partners. "Show me who you read," the book says in spirit, "and I'll show you how you think."

*On Thinking* suffers from the classic anthology problem: uneven quality and no single voice to carry you through. Some essays are brilliant (the Arendt section, the Vygotsky treatment). Others are impenetrably academic — the kind of writing that seems designed to exclude rather than illuminate. The Heidegger sections, in particular, require more familiarity with his terminology than most readers will have.

There's also a frustrating lack of *practical* guidance. The book is excellent at diagnosing why we don't think well and what genuine thinking looks like. It's almost silent on how to actually do it. The meditative thinking that Heidegger valorizes — how do you cultivate it? The book gestures at "slowing down" and "creating space" but offers nothing as concrete as a practice.

And the collection is overwhelmingly male and Western. Out of twenty-something contributors, I counted maybe two women and zero non-Western philosophers. Thinking traditions from Zen Buddhism (which has been studying the nature of thought for 1,500 years), Advaita Vedanta, or African ubuntu philosophy are absent. The book presents itself as universal while drawing from an astonishingly narrow well.

The idea I keep returning to: *thinking is not a natural act*. It requires conditions — safety, time, silence, and the right kind of friction — that modern life systematically eliminates. If you feel like you can't think deeply anymore, it might not be your fault. It might be your environment.

I also can't shake the Vygotsky insight about inner speech. Since reading this book, I've been more conscious of who I'm letting into my head. Which authors, which voices, which arguments. Because that inner conversation is where my thinking happens, and I'd rather populate it with people who are worth arguing with than with algorithms optimized for my outrage.

Anyone who feels like their thinking has gotten shallower and wants to understand why. Philosophy students will find familiar names in unfamiliar contexts. Skeptics of "deep work" culture will find validation and challenge. Skip it if you want a practical how-to guide, if academic prose makes your eyes glaze over, or if you're looking for thinking techniques rather than a diagnosis of why thinking is hard.
