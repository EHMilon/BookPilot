Massimo Pigliucci walked into Epictetus's classroom twenty centuries late and decided to enroll anyway. **How to Be a Stoic** is the syllabus — a 21st-century scientist-philosopher working through the ancient Stoic curriculum as if it were a live correspondence course, complete with homework, failures, and genuine observation of what works and what doesn't. Pigliucci came to Stoicism the way many modern people do: burned out on anxiety, interested in resilience, but unwilling to buy into anything that required metaphysical faith or unexamined optimism.

The book is structured as a letter to Pigliucci's imaginary friend "Epictetus" — a conceit that could be pretentious but instead becomes intimate and disarmingly honest. Pigliucci is a professor of philosophy at CUNY, a biologist by training, and a secular skeptic. He's not selling you a cure. He's showing you his practice, warts and all, and inviting you to try it yourself. The result is one of the most level-headed introductions to Stoic practice available — not because it breaks new ground, but because it treats the philosophy as something you *do*, not something you believe.

**The first idea is that Stoicism begins with the dichotomy of control — and most people get it wrong.** Pigliucci is careful where other popularizers are sloppy. Epictetus's *Enchiridion* opens with the claim that some things are up to us (judgment, choice, desire, aversion) and others are not (property, reputation, health, even our own bodies). The Trap is that beginners interpret this as passive resignation — "I can't control whether I get sick, so I'll stop going to the doctor." That's not Stoicism. The point isn't that you stop acting. The point is that you stop emotionally investing in outcomes you can't guarantee. You go to the doctor *and* you accept that you might still get sick. You apply for the job *and* you accept that someone else might get it. The discipline is in the comma — holding effort and acceptance together without either one canceling the other. Pigliucci puts it simply: "It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters." It's a line from Epictetus that has been quoted to death, but Pigliucci earns his use of it by showing just how hard it actually is to live by.

**The second idea is that you can't skip the emotional training.** Many people come to Stoicism drawn to its calm, its rationality, its refusal to be rattled. Pigliucci warns that this is the wrong approach. Stoicism isn't about suppressing emotions. It's about training your automatic responses so that your emotions align with reality *before* they fire. The Stoics used a series of exercises — *askēsis* — to reshape their emotional habits. Pigliucci describes his own attempts at evening journaling, morning reminders, and negative visualization. He describes failing at them, picking them up again, finding what sticks. "We are not born with the ability to use reason at all times," he writes, and the admission is quietly radical. You have to practice. The Trap is that modern readers pick up a Stoic book, understand the logic, and think they've accomplished something. Understanding is not transformation. The book becomes useful only when you close it and do the work.

**The third idea is the application of Stoicism to modern anxiety.** This is where Pigliucci's background as a scientist becomes most useful. He translates Stoic techniques into terms a secular reader can engage with. The Stoic concept of "impressions" — the initial thought that hits you before you can stop it — maps directly onto cognitive behavioral therapy's concept of automatic thoughts. The Stoic practice of "suspending judgment" — not assenting to an impression until you've examined it — is the ancient version of cognitive restructuring. Pigliucci describes how he uses this for social anxiety, for public speaking nerves, for the fear of not being good enough. The lines that hit hardest come from his own experience: "The idea is not to avoid all passions, but to learn to evaluate them and, if possible, redirect them." You don't try to stop feeling anxious. You examine the judgment underneath the anxiety and ask: is this true?

**The fourth idea is that Stoicism has a social dimension often overlooked by modern readers.** Pigliucci devotes significant space to the idea of *oikeiosis* — the Stoic concept of concentric circles of concern. You start with yourself, then your family, then your community, then all of humanity. The goal is not to abandon the inner circles for the outer ones but to widen your sense of what matters. A Stoic doesn't retreat from the world. They engage with it, but from a position of inner stability. Pigliucci calls this "being a warrior, not a monk." You show up. You do your duty. But you don't attach your happiness to the results. The Trap is choosing the monk path when the hard thing is the warrior path. If Stoicism becomes an excuse to withdraw from relationships, from politics, from the mess of being with other people, you're using it wrong.

**The fifth idea is the role of cosmopolitanism — being a citizen of the universe.** Pigliucci takes the Stoic notion of *cosmopolis* seriously. In a world of increasing tribalism, the Stoic claim that every human being shares a spark of the divine Logos — and is therefore worthy of respect and justice — is quietly radical. This isn't about being nice to everyone. It's about a fundamental reorientation of identity. You're not primarily an American, a Bengali, a Christian, a man. You're a human being, connected to every other human being by the capacity for reason. Pigliucci leans on Marcus Aurelius here: "What is not good for the hive is not good for the bee." The Trap is that cosmopolitanism can sound like empty universalism — "we're all one" — while ignoring real differences in power, culture, and circumstance. Pigliucci is aware of this and argues that Stoicism doesn't erase difference. It positions our shared humanity as the baseline from which we negotiate everything else.

**The sixth idea, and the one Pigliucci returns to most personally, is that Stoicism is a philosophy of continuous failure.** This is the most honest part of the book — and the most valuable. Pigliucci doesn't present himself as a Stoic sage. He presents himself as someone who tries and falls short, over and over. He gets angry in traffic. He worries about his children. He fails at negative visualization. And this, he argues, is the entire point: "Stoicism is not about being perfect. It's about making progress." The Trap is the perfectionist's trap — you start, you stumble, and you conclude the philosophy doesn't work. But the Stoics never promised instant transformation. They promised a direction. Seneca said it plainly: "What progress have I made? I am beginning to be my own friend." If you can be your own friend in failure, you've already won.

Pigliucci's book has been praised for its clarity and honesty, but it's not without weak spots. The letter-to-Epictetus format, while charming, occasionally feels forced — the device wears thin in the second half. The book is more of a survey than a deep dive; readers looking for original scholarship or novel interpretations will be disappointed. Pigliucci is a synthesizer, not an innovator, and **How to Be a Stoic** essentially covers the same ground as Irvine's **A Guide to the Good Life** with a different framing and a slightly more philosophically rigorous approach. Some readers will prefer Irvine's tighter structure; others will prefer Pigliucci's warm, confessional tone. The book also sidesteps difficult metaphysical questions — the nature of the divine Logos, determinism versus free will — that the original Stoics took seriously. This makes it accessible but somewhat incomplete as a picture of what Stoicism actually was for its original practitioners.

**The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book was the notion that Stoicism is a verb, not a noun.** You don't *have* Stoicism. You *do* it. And you do it imperfectly, every day, for the rest of your life. That's not a flaw in the philosophy. It's the design. Pigliucci doesn't offer a destination or a doctrine. He offers a practice — and the willingness to fail at it publicly. And somehow, that makes it more believable than any book that promises a five-step plan to permanent happiness.

**This book is for** curious skeptics who want a practical, no-nonsense introduction to Stoicism from someone who shares their secular worldview. It's for people who have tried cognitive behavioral therapy and want to understand its philosophical roots. It's not for readers looking for profound metaphysical insight or a rigorous scholarly treatment of ancient texts.

First, start a Stoic journal. Each evening, write down one moment you responded well and one moment you didn't. Don't judge — just observe. Second, tomorrow morning, write your "preview of the day" — imagine the difficult people you'll encounter and rehearse your response. Third, before a stressful event, pause and ask: "What is up to me here?" Commit to that and release the rest.
