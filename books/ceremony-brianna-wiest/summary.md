You've probably read the poems somewhere — a screenshot on Instagram, a friend's story, a Pinterest board. *Ceremony* is the book those poems came from. But the experience of reading it as a whole is different from encountering it in fragments. It's not just a collection of verses about healing and self-love. It's a liturgy, in the truest sense — a set of words designed to be returned to, spoken aloud, and woven into the fabric of how you live.

Brianna Wiest wrote *Ceremony* after years of writing prose that resonated deeply with people who were emerging from difficult periods. She noticed that the moments people shared most weren't her explanations or frameworks — they were the short, potent passages that seemed to capture something inexpressible. *Ceremony* is a response to that observation: a book of prose-poetry meant to serve as a companion through the rituals of becoming yourself, written in language that lodges in the bones, not just the brain.

The most important thing to understand about *Ceremony* is that it's not trying to be clever. It's not playing with form, subverting expectations, or deconstructing anything. It's doing something older and more vulnerable: it's telling you the truth in simple words and hoping that matters.

Wiest divides the book into stages that mirror the emotional arc of healing — from falling apart, through the slow work of putting things back together, to the tentative arrival at something like peace. The poems and passages are short, many of them fitting on a single page. But they're dense in a way that rewards rereading. A line that hits as comforting on Monday might cut as a challenge on Friday.

> "You cannot heal what you do not feel."

This appears early in the book and serves as a kind of thesis. Wiest is not writing about bypassing pain or transcending difficulty. She's writing about the specific courage required to sit with what hurts until it transforms. The ceremony isn't about making the pain disappear — it's about honoring it enough to let it move through you. This is a radical idea in a culture that sells us quick fixes and emotional numbing: the shortest path through grief is not around it, not over it, but directly and messily through it.

The book gives specific names to emotional states and transitions, treating them as ceremonies worthy of attention. There are poems about letting go, about choosing yourself, about the grief of outgrowing people you still love. There are passages about the quiet after a difficult decision, the loneliness of growth, the terror of being seen.

What makes this work is that Wiest doesn't pretend any of these moments are easy. The ceremony of choosing yourself is not triumphant in her hands — it's aching and full of doubt. She describes the morning after a hard choice: the way the room looks the same but you feel different, the strange emptiness that follows a decision you know was right but still hurts.

> "The ceremony of choosing yourself is not a celebration. It is a funeral. You are burying the person you thought you had to be."

This is the book's signature move: it takes the glossy language of self-love and self-care and turns it back into something that hurts. Choosing yourself is not a TikTok affirmation. It's a grieving process. You lose the version of your life you thought you'd have, the approval of people who wanted you to stay small, the comfortable story of who you were. Wiest gives these losses their proper weight. She doesn't rush you through the grief. She sits with you in it.

**The Trap:** The ceremony framework can make every emotional state feel like it needs to be named, honored, and processed — which is a recipe for spending all your energy naming your feelings instead of living your life. Wiest is aware of this and pushes back against it in quieter passages: the goal is not to endlessly ritualize, but to find the few ceremonies that actually free you. Not everything needs a poem. Some things just need to be felt and released.

One of the book's strongest themes is the rejection of linear healing. Wiest is adamant that recovery — from heartbreak, trauma, bad patterns — does not move from Point A to Point B in a straight line. It circles back. You think you've healed something, and then a trigger brings you right back to the feeling you thought you'd left behind.

But the spiral is not a failure. Each time you circle back, you return at a slightly higher level, with slightly more awareness, slightly more capacity. The same emotion that devastated you two years ago is now just painful. And next time it will be a memory. Wiest uses the image of a spiral staircase: you're moving upward, but you keep passing the same view from a different height. The only way up is to accept the repetition.

> "You are not starting over. You are starting again from a place of deeper knowing."

This reframe is quietly revolutionary. Most of us judge ourselves harshly when old wounds reopen. We think we've failed at healing, that we're broken in some fundamental way. Wiest's spiral model gives us permission to feel the feeling without the meta-judgment on top of it. You're not regressing. You're revisiting, which is completely different. The revisiting is how you integrate — how you move the lesson from your head into your bones.

Several of the book's most powerful passages are about love — specifically, the way our capacity to love ourselves determines what we can receive from others. This is territory Wiest has covered in her prose work, but in *Ceremony* it takes on a different quality. Starker. More irreducible.

One passage describes the strange inertia of being loved by someone when you can't love yourself: their love hits a wall. It has nowhere to go. You absorb it like a desert absorbs rain — it sinks into the sand and disappears without changing anything. The ceremony of self-acceptance isn't about being able to look in the mirror and say nice things. It's about becoming porous enough for someone else's love to actually reach you.

Another passage speaks to the fear of being fully seen — the terror that if someone knows all of you, they'll leave. Wiest's answer is disarmingly gentle: let them see. Let them decide. The ones who stay after seeing everything are the ones worth keeping.

> "The person you're becoming needs you to let go of the person you were."

This line, repeated in different forms throughout the book, is the thrumming bass note beneath everything. You cannot become while clutching. You cannot arrive while holding on. The ceremony of release is the most important one you'll ever perform, and you'll perform it many times — because every new stage of growth requires releasing the previous one.

One of the book's most understated contributions is its treatment of what comes *after* the ceremony. After the letting go, after the difficult choice, after the grief — what then? Wiest writes about the strange stillness that follows genuine emotional work. Not happiness, not relief, but a kind of quiet. The storm has passed. The water is settling. You're not fixed, but you're different.

These are the passages that stick longest, because they describe something most self-help ignores: the anticlimax of growth. You do the work, you go through the ceremony, and what you get is not euphoria but a slightly clearer view of your life. Wiest honors this anticlimax. She doesn't try to make it more dramatic than it is.

*Ceremony* will not work for everyone, and it's important to be honest about why. The prose-poetry format can feel repetitive after fifty pages — the same cadence, the same syntax, the same kinds of sentences doing the same kinds of work. Some readers will find the style one-note, like a song that only has one chord. There's also a risk that the book's emotional vocabulary becomes a kind of catechesis — you learn to describe your experience in Wiest's language rather than your own, which can be helpful or can be a different kind of cage. And the emphasis on emotional depth occasionally tips into valuing pain over action; you could read this book *instead* of doing the work, treating the experience of reading as the ceremony itself, and miss the entire point.
