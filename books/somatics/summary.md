I started reading *Somatics* lying on my back, because the irony of reading a book about reawakening the body while sitting in a chair wasn't lost on me. Thomas Hanna, a philosophy professor turned movement teacher, wrote this book in 1988, and it's one of those rare texts that feels both decades ahead of its time and urgently needed right now. It's the foundational text of the somatic movement — the idea that you can reclaim your body's natural intelligence by learning to *sense* it again.

Hanna begins with a simple observation: as we age, we don't just lose muscle mass or flexibility. We lose *sensory awareness* of parts of our body. The back tightens, the shoulders round, the hips lock, and eventually those areas go numb — not physically numb, but *sensorially* numb. You can't feel your lower back anymore. You don't know what your pelvis is doing. Your body has become a series of blind spots.

This is not a natural consequence of aging, Hanna argues. It's a learned response — a gradual, unconscious adaptation to stress and trauma that he calls the "Red Light Reflex" and the "Green Light Reflex."

The **Red Light Reflex** is the startle response gone chronic. When you're in danger, your body does three things: pull the head back and down, round the shoulders forward, and curl the torso into a protective C-shape. This is useful when the danger is real and temporary. The problem is that modern life keeps the red light on — work stress, financial pressure, constant alertness — and the body never returns to neutral. You end up with a permanently curled, contracted posture that Hanna calls the "startle reflex" habituated.

The **Green Light Reflex** is the opposite pattern: the body's readiness for action. Head up, shoulders back, chest out, back arched — the posture of a sprinter in the blocks. This is activated by chronic striving, ambition, or the feeling that you always need to be doing, achieving, reaching. Over time, this creates its own set of problems: lower back hyperextension, tight hip flexors, shallow breathing.

**The Trap:** Most people identify with one of these patterns and think the answer is to do the opposite. If you're a sloucher (red light), you try to force your shoulders back (green light). But Hanna says this doesn't work — you're just replacing one compensatory pattern with another. The real solution is not to *force* a different posture but to *sense* the pattern, bring it into awareness, and let the nervous system release it naturally.

Hanna's method is disarmingly simple. Each chapter targets a specific body area — the neck, the shoulders, the lower back, the hips, the feet — and provides a sequence of slow, gentle movements designed to wake up the sensory feedback that's gone dormant. The movements are tiny. You're not stretching. You're not strengthening. You're *sensing*.

There's one for the lower back that changed my life. Lying on your back, knees bent, feet flat on the floor. Slowly, millimeter by millimeter, arch your lower back so it lifts off the floor — just a sliver — then slowly flatten it back down. The movement is so small you wonder if it's doing anything. Then you repeat it on one side. And then you stand up, and one side of your lower back feels *alive* and the other side feels like a brick. That's the moment Hanna is after. The contrast between a sensed muscle and a numb one.

He calls this process "somatic learning" — learning that's registered in the sensory-motor system, not just the brain. You can't think your way out of chronic back pain. You can't stretch your way out of it either. You have to re-educate the nervous system by giving it new, subtle information that it can integrate over time.

**The Trap of "No Pain, No Gain":** Hanna is explicit that the somatic approach is the opposite of the fitness industry's ethos. If it hurts, you're doing it wrong. Pain triggers the startle reflex, which contracts the muscles further — exactly what you're trying to release. The movements should be so gentle that they're almost boring. The progress is measured not in inches of flexibility gained but in *quality of sensation*.

There's a whole chapter on the "Mummy" — a degenerative pattern where the entire front of the body contracts, pulling the head forward, rounding the shoulders, and shortening the torso. Hanna says this is the most common aging pattern in the modern world, and he attributes it directly to chair culture. Sitting for hours shortens the front line of the body. The Mummy is what happens when that shortening becomes permanent. His fix is a 10-minute daily sequence that systematically addresses each contracted area.

What makes *Somatics* different from every other movement book I've read is its emphasis on the *brain's* role in the body's problems. Hanna was influenced by the work of Moshe Feldenkrais, and the book carries that lineage forward: the idea that movement issues are really *learning* issues. Your brain has learned a pattern — slouching, bracing, holding — and it keeps running that pattern automatically. To change it, you don't need to force the body; you need to teach the brain a new option. And you do that through microscopic, attentive movement that the brain can actually register as new information.

Hanna calls this "sensory-motor amnesia" — the brain's gradual forgetting of how to sense and control certain muscles. It's not that the muscles are damaged. They're perfectly capable. But the brain has stopped talking to them. The connection has gone dormant. The somatic exercises are not about strengthening or stretching; they're about re-establishing the communication line. Once the brain can sense the muscle again, the muscle remembers how to relax, coordinate, and move freely.

This reframing was a revelation for me. I had spent years trying to "fix" my tight hips with stretching. I would hold a pigeon pose for two minutes, feel a deep stretch, and stand up — tight again within an hour. Hanna explains why: stretching is a reflex. When you stretch, the muscle briefly yields, then the nervous system tightens it back to protect against what it perceived as a threat. Somatic movement doesn't trigger that reflex because the movement is so small and slow that the nervous system never registers it as a stretch. The release is real and lasting because it comes from the nervous system, not the connective tissue.

One of Hanna's boldest claims is that much of what we call "aging" is actually sensory-motor amnesia — the gradual forgetting of how to move. He points to cultures where elderly people maintain extraordinary mobility and posture well into their 80s and 90s, and argues it's not genetics or diet that preserves them — it's that they never stopped moving in varied, conscious ways. "We do not grow old because we age," he writes. "We grow old because we stop moving. And we stop moving because we have forgotten how."

There's also a chapter on the relationship between somatics and chronic pain that I found unsettlingly accurate. Hanna describes patients who have been told by doctors that their pain is "in their head" — as if that means it's imaginary. Hanna says it's the opposite. The pain is absolutely real. But its origin is in the brain's learned pattern of muscle contraction, not in structural damage to the spine or joints. He calls this "somatic pain" — pain generated by the brain-muscle system itself. The treatment is not surgery, not drugs, not even massage. It's re-education.

**Honest Criticism:** The book is dense and can feel dated. Hanna's writing is academic in places — he coined the term "somatics" and he's determined to define it thoroughly. The exercises are described in text, not photos, which makes them hard to follow without a teacher or video. I had to read some sections three times before I understood what he was asking me to do.

The book also leans heavily on Hanna's personal authority. He doesn't cite much research — the book is mostly his observations and conclusions from decades of work. Some of his claims (like the idea that the Red Light/Green Light reflexes explain most chronic pain) feel hypothesis-level rather than evidence-level. A reader with a skeptical bent will want to verify some of this elsewhere.

And the biggest practical issue: the daily practice requires 15–20 minutes of focused, slow movement. Most people won't do it. Most people will try it twice, get bored, and go back to stretching or foam rolling. The book doesn't solve for that.
