Try this thought experiment: imagine waking up in 1978 and being told that a country of nearly a billion people, emerging from a decade of cultural devastation, would within forty years become the world's second-largest economy, build the largest high-speed rail network on earth, lift 800 million people out of poverty, and be locked in a technological cold war with the United States. Most people at the time would have laughed. *China in the 21st Century* gathers multiple perspectives to attempt what no single book can fully do: understand the rise of a civilization-state that operates on completely different assumptions than the Western liberal order it is now challenging.

The book faces an almost impossible task. China today is simultaneously a Communist Party dictatorship and a hyper-capitalist economy, a civilization five thousand years old and a nation that has reinvented itself four times in the last century, a partner in global trade and a rival in global power. To understand it requires at least four frameworks: historical (where did China come from?), political (how does the Party maintain control?), economic (how did growth happen?), and geopolitical (what does China want?).

China is too big and too strange to fit one story. The "China threat" narrative misses the cooperation. The "China opportunity" narrative misses the repression. The "China is just another great power" narrative misses the civilizational ambition. Anyone who claims to have China figured out hasn't been paying attention.

> "China's development is not a threat to anyone. It is an opportunity for the world."

This sentence — repeated in various forms by Chinese leaders from Hu Jintao to Xi Jinping — captures the official narrative. China's rise is peaceful, cooperative, and mutually beneficial. The Belt and Road Initiative is infrastructure and investment, not empire-building. The South China Sea bases are defensive, not aggressive. The digital surveillance is for security and convenience, not control. Every policy comes wrapped in the language of win-win cooperation. The book's contributors spend considerable time arguing over whether this is genuine, naive, or propaganda. The answer is probably all three.

The book opens with the tension that defines modern China: the Party's need for control versus the economy's need for freedom. Since Deng Xiaoping's reforms in the late 1970s, China has operated on a contradictory logic — economic liberalization paired with political authoritarianism. The assumption was that growth would eventually lead to political opening. It didn't. Instead, China created something new: a system that delivers prosperity without democracy, stability without freedom, and nationalism without liberalism.

The concept of the **China Dream** — Xi Jinping's signature ideological project — gets significant attention. It's easy to dismiss as propaganda, and it is propaganda, but it's also a genuine expression of China's post-colonial ambition: to restore the centrality that China had for most of recorded history. The "century of humiliation" (1839–1949, when foreign powers carved up China) is not ancient history in China — it's living memory, taught in every school, referenced in every diplomatic conflict. The China Dream is the promise that the humiliation is over and that China will never be weak again.

> "China has awakened, and it is a sleeping giant that will shake the world."

This quote — often misattributed to Napoleon, actually from a French diplomat — captures the anxiety that runs through the Western portions of this book. China's rise is not just an economic story. It's an emotional story. For the West, China represents an alternative — a model of development that doesn't require liberal democracy, doesn't prioritize individual rights, and doesn't defer to Western leadership. The existence of this alternative is threatening regardless of what China actually does.

Most analysis of China starts from the assumption that China wants what the West has — democracy, markets, individual rights. What if China doesn't? What if China is building something genuinely different — a state-capitalist, authoritarian, civilization-based model that competes on its own terms? The question isn't "when will China become like us?" but "what happens if it never does?"

The book's most valuable contribution might be its treatment of China's internal challenges — the ones that don't make Western headlines. The demographic crisis: China's population is aging faster than any large country in history, and the one-child policy means there aren't enough young people to support the elderly. The environmental crisis: China is the world's largest carbon emitter, and its air and water pollution have created public health catastrophes. The debt crisis: local government debt is staggering, and the property bubble that drove China's growth for two decades is deflating. The surveillance state: China has built the most sophisticated system of social control in human history, from facial recognition to social credit scores to mass detention of Uyghurs in Xinjiang.

These are not minor problems. They are existential threats that the Party manages through a combination of propaganda, repression, and growth. As long as the economy grows, the Party maintains legitimacy. If growth stops, the contradictions become unbearable.

> "The Communist Party of China has always represented the fundamental interests of the broadest majority of the Chinese people."

This is the official line, repeated endlessly. The book's contributors offer a range of responses: some take it seriously as an expression of genuine popular support (Chinese approval ratings for the Party are genuinely high), others dismiss it as necessary ideological cover for a one-party state. The truth is probably more complicated than either position. The Chinese people are not simply oppressed or simply supportive. They are living in a system that delivers clear benefits (security, rising living standards, national pride) at the cost of clear limitations (restricted speech, no political choice, state surveillance).

Western media tends to present China as either a dystopian nightmare or a developmental miracle. The reality is both and neither. China's 800 million people lifted from poverty is the greatest economic achievement in human history. China's detention of a million Uyghurs is a crime against humanity. Both statements are true. The inability to hold both simultaneously is a failure of moral imagination.

The book's geopolitical sections are the most contentious. China's ambitions in the South China Sea, the Belt and Road Initiative, the technological challenge to American dominance through companies like Huawei and TikTok — these are not separate issues. They are expressions of a single strategic goal: to reshape the international order so that it reflects China's interests and values, not just the West's. The Belt and Road Initiative, often framed as a debt-trap diplomacy scheme, is more accurately described as China building the infrastructure for a world in which it is the center. Roads, ports, railways, and digital networks that bypass Western-controlled shipping lanes and financial systems.

The Taiwan question is presented with the starkness it demands: China considers Taiwan a renegade province and has never renounced the use of force to reunify. The One China policy — that there is one China and Taiwan is part of it — is accepted by most countries, including the US, de facto if not de jure. But the intensity of cross-strait tensions has increased dramatically, and the book's contributors are divided on whether China's eventual unification with Taiwan will be peaceful or violent.

> "We are not afraid of zero-sum game," said a Chinese diplomat quoted in one of the chapters. "China does not seek war, but we are not afraid of it."

This captures the mood shift in Chinese foreign policy over the last decade. From Deng's "hide your strength, bide your time" to Xi's "national rejuvenation" and assertive diplomacy. The old China wanted to be left alone to develop. The new China wants to shape the world according to its vision.

Framing China as an enemy is as misleading as framing it as a partner. China is a competitor — on trade, on technology, on influence. But it's also a necessary collaborator — on climate change, on pandemic response, on nuclear non-proliferation. The US-China relationship is not a cold war. It's something stranger and less stable: two interdependent giants who distrust each other but can't afford to separate.

The idea I couldn't shake is the concept of *tianxia* (天下) — "all under heaven" — that runs through Chinese geopolitical thinking. It's an ancient idea: the world is a single space that should be harmonized under a virtuous center. The modern CCP has revived this concept to justify its global ambitions. But what's fascinating is that *tianxia* is not about conquest in the Western sense. It's about building a hierarchy that others accept because it serves their interests. China doesn't want to defeat the West. It wants to create a world in which the West is no longer the center, and in which China's model — state-led development, social stability over individual freedom, collective over individual — is seen as equally valid, maybe superior. The ambition is breathtaking. The assumption that everyone will accept it is unrealistic.

This is a composite book, not a single argument, and it shows. The quality varies widely between chapters. Some are sharp analysis; others are filler. The book has a structural problem: it tries to cover everything — economy, politics, military, technology, diplomacy, environment — and covers most things shallowly. There's also a bias problem that varies by contributor. Some chapters are obviously pro-CCP propaganda dressed up as analysis. Others are Western alarmism dressed up as expertise. A reader who knows nothing about China would come away confused about what's actually true. And the rapid pace of change in China means any book published more than two years ago is already dated. China in the 21st century moves too fast for book-length analysis to keep up.
