**Scaling Up** by Verne Harnish

You've got twenty employees, maybe fifty, and everything feels like it's on fire. You can't remember the last time you had an uninterrupted hour. The decisions you used to make in a minute now take weeks, and nobody — including you — is quite sure who's supposed to do what. That's the exact moment Verne Harnish's *Scaling Up* wants to catch you. Harnish, who built the global entrepreneur network Gazelles and spent decades watching companies hit the same wall at the same revenue thresholds, wrote this as a field manual for the messy middle — the phase between scrappy startup and genuinely functional company. His argument is simple and relentless: growth doesn't have to mean chaos. Most companies fail not because they run out of money or market, but because they run out of organizational coherence. The book gives you a grid, a rhythm, and a vocabulary for the fight.

The core architecture is the **Four Decisions** framework — People, Strategy, Execution, and Cash — and Harnish insists every scaling company needs to get all four right simultaneously. Neglect one, and the others rot.

**The One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP).** This is Harnish's signature tool and the beating heart of the book. The idea is absurdly compressed: fit your entire strategy on one page. Not because strategy is simple — but because if you can't boil it down to a single view, nobody in your company will actually remember it. The OPSP includes your core values, purpose (why you exist beyond profit), BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal — a term Harnish borrows from Jim Collins), sandbox (where you play and where you don't), and a handful of annual and quarterly priorities. Most companies treat this as a poster exercise — write it once, frame it, hang it in the break room. Harnish's point is the opposite: the OPSP lives in weekly meetings, quarterly reviews, and daily standups. If nobody is arguing about what goes in that BHAG box, you're doing it wrong.

**People — the right seats on the right bus.** Harnish takes Jim Collins's "first who, then what" and turns it into a practical tool called the **Function Accountability Chart (FACe)** . Not an org chart — a function chart. The question isn't "who reports to whom" but "who carries the final responsibility for what." Every critical function in the company has one name next to it — one person who can't pass the buck. Harnish's insight is that growing companies fail on people not because they hire badly, but because they don't clarify accountability fast enough as the org multiplies. The corollary: **Process Accountability Chart (PACe)** — the key processes each function owns, documented at the level of "an intelligent person can follow them." Founders keep wearing every hat because they're the best at everything. That's a feature at five people and a fatal bug at fifty. You need a process for getting yourself out of your own way.

**Strategy — the sandbox and the seven strata.** Harnish pushes for disciplined focus: what is your sandbox — the specific market, geography, and customer segment you dominate? And equally important: what is *not* in your sandbox? The **seven strata of strategy** run from massive global trends down to your unique differentiators. Most companies skip the top layers — the big macroeconomic and industry shifts — and dive straight into tactics. Harnish wants you to look at where the world is going and plant your flag in the path of the wave. The most common strategic mistake in scaling companies is trying to be everything to everyone. Focus doesn't mean small; it means deep. A company that tries to serve every customer segment ends up serving none well.

**Execution — the rhythm of the rock.** Harnish borrows the **Rock, Pebble, Sand** metaphor from Stephen Covey: big priorities are rocks (put them in first), the daily grind is sand (it fills the gaps). Execution discipline means protecting the rocks from being buried by sand. The mechanism is the **Weekly Meeting Rhythm** — daily huddles (15 minutes, no chairs), weekly team meetings (60-90 minutes, same agenda format every week), quarterly offsites, and annual strategic reviews. A company that doesn't meet with the right cadence isn't executing; it's reacting. The weekly meeting has a fixed format: start with the good news (5 min), review the scoreboard (10 min), review the rocks (20 min), and then the customer and people conversations. Same agenda, same order, every week. The predictability is the point — it frees up mental energy for the content instead of the process. Leaders fill every meeting with emergency garbage. The meeting rhythm only works if you protect the rocks — if every weekly meeting becomes a fire drill, you've just added a meeting, not built a discipline.

**Cash — the daily dashboard.** Harnish is obsessed with cash, not profit, and for good reason: profitable companies go bankrupt all the time because they run out of cash while waiting for receivables. His **Cash Acceleration Strategies (CASh)** include the obvious (invoice faster, get deposits) and the counterintuitive (raise prices, fire your worst customers). The **Power of One** exercise asks: what happens to profit if you increase price by 1%, cut COGS by 1%, reduce overhead by 1%, and increase volume by 1%? The compound effect is usually a 20-50% profit swing. Founders think about the P&L like homeowners — "are we in the black?" — but scaling companies need to think about cash like fighter pilots: where is it now, where will it be in 90 days, and what happens if the engine stalls.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing *Scaling Up* was the **FACe** — the function accountability chart. I've seen so many teams where everyone vaguely thinks someone else is handling something. Harnish makes visible what's usually invisible: the gap between "we should do this" and "a specific person owns this." The other idea that sticks is the **meeting rhythm**. It sounds banal — meetings? — but in practice, a company that meets the same way every week, with the same format, the same priorities visible, and the same accountability, builds muscle memory. The work doesn't feel chaotic because the container is stable.

*Scaling Up* is long, and it's dense in the way a reference manual is dense — you won't read it cover to cover, and Harnish doesn't expect you to. Some of the tools (the OPSP, the FACe) are genuinely brilliant; others feel like corporate wallpaper repackaged. The book can also be exhausting in its intensity — Harnish writes like a man who's been mainlining espresso for thirty years, and the tone never lets up. The **Rockefeller Habits** (the predecessor to this book, also by Harnish) covered much of the same ground, and *Scaling Up* sometimes reads as an expanded edition rather than a distinct work. For non-US readers, some of the financial and legal examples won't map cleanly. And while Harnish talks about culture, the book is, in the end, about systems, not soul — if your company's problem is a rotten culture, no OPSP will fix it.

CEOs and founders of companies between 10 and 250 employees who feel like they're drowning in operational chaos. Also works for functional leaders (VP of Ops, CFO) who need a framework to get the CEO on the same page. Solopreneurs, pre-revenue startups, and anyone who thinks their company's problems are too unique for frameworks. They're not.

1. Spend two hours drafting your own One-Page Strategic Plan — core values, purpose, BHAG, and your top three quarterly priorities. Show it to one colleague. Ask: "Is this what we're actually doing?"
2. Identify the top three functions in your company where accountability is fuzzy. Write one name next to each. Send the list to those three people and ask: "Are you clear that you own this?"
3. Create your Cash Conversion Cycle number — the days between paying for something and getting paid for it. If it's over 45 days, pick one Cash Acceleration Strategy (raise prices, require deposits, or invoice on delivery) and implement it this week.
4. Block a 15-minute daily huddle with your core team for the next 14 days. Same time, same place, no chairs, no phones. The only agenda: what's urgent, what's stuck, and are we hitting the rocks?
