The hardest truth about loving a child who is going to die is this: you do it anyway. You don't do it because you're brave or noble or because you believe in a higher purpose. You do it because the alternative — protecting yourself, holding back — would be a different kind of death, one that happens while you're still breathing.

*Finding Chika* is Mitch Albom's account of Chika Jeune, a little girl from Haiti who came into his life through the orphanage he helped found (the Have Faith Haiti Mission), and who left it three years later, at age seven, from a brain tumor. It's not an easy book to read, and it was not an easy book to write. Albom spent the year after Chika's death trying to make sense of it, and the result is part memoir, part elegy, part direct address to a child who is no longer there — because that's what Chika became to him, even though she was never legally his.

---

Chika entered Albom's life in 2013, during one of his visits to the Port-au-Prince orphanage. She was a tiny girl with a huge personality — bossy, hilarious, fiercely independent, fond of telling adults what to do. Albom describes her as "a flashlight in a dark room." She wasn't sick then, not yet. She was just a kid who had lost her mother in childbirth and whose father couldn't afford to raise her. For Chika, the orphanage wasn't a tragedy. It was home. She had friends, a routine, and adults who adored her. One of the book's most tender sections describes the rituals of orphanage life — the meals, the games, the way the children competed for attention with the desperation of kids who had already learned that love could disappear.

Then came the diagnosis. During a routine visit to a Miami dentist, the dentist noticed her left eye was swollen. Probably an infection. But the swelling didn't go away, and eventually tests revealed a diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG) — an aggressive brain tumor with a zero percent survival rate. The same tumor that has taken dozens of children in the headlines. Albom and his wife Janine were faced with a choice: send Chika back to Haiti with painkillers, or fight a battle they would inevitably lose.

They chose to fight. The book is the story of that fight.

What makes *Finding Chika* different from other illness memoirs is Albom's willingness to show his own emotional process in all its ugliness. He admits to bargaining with God. He admits to researching miracle cures in the dead of night, knowing they were false hope. He admits to dragging Chika to appointment after appointment, watching her suffer through treatments that would ultimately fail. He admits to the rage he felt watching other families with healthy children. He admits that part of him wanted to run.

*I wanted to save her. But I also wanted to save myself from the pain of losing her. And the two desires were at war.*

The book alternates between the chronology of Chika's illness and Albom's reflections after her death, written as direct addresses to her. These sections are the most powerful because they're the least polished. He tells Chika about the days after she died — the way he couldn't throw away her toothbrush, the way he kept seeing her in the corner of his eye, the way the silence in the house became a physical weight he carried from room to room. He tells her about the ordinary moments he misses most: not the birthday parties or the trips to the beach, but the way she'd grab his hand in the grocery store, the way she'd demand a second scoop of ice cream with the imperiousness of a tiny queen, the way she'd fall asleep with her head on his chest during flights.

*I thought love was forever. I still do. But loving someone forever doesn't stop the missing.*

Albom's theology — God as mystery rather than answer — is consistent with his other books, but here it feels earned rather than literary. He doesn't offer explanations for why Chika died. He doesn't claim she's in a better place, or that her suffering had meaning. What he offers is something rawer: that love does not end with death, but it changes form, and learning to live with that change is the work of grief. There's a section where he writes about visiting a psychic medium — something he never would have done before — because he was so desperate to hear from Chika that he was willing to suspend every rational belief he held. The medium says things that seem to come from Chika, and Albom doesn't know whether to believe her or not. He leaves the question open. And that openness, that refusal to force the story into a neat shape, is what gives the book its honesty.

One of the most unexpected and beautiful sections is about the Haitian culture of death. Albom describes visiting Chika's mother's grave in Haiti and learning about local traditions — the wailing women, the days of mourning, the way death is treated as a visible communal wound rather than a private medical event. He contrasts this with the clean, clinical American approach to dying — the hospice bed, the hospital room, the euphemistic language of "passing" and "letting go." He's not making a judgment, but he's clearly learned something from Haiti about how to hold death: that it's meant to be witnessed, not hidden.

The chapter on the day Chika died is handled with extraordinary restraint. Albom condenses the worst day of his life into a few pages. There's no dramatic scene, no last words. Just a child slipping away, a father holding her, a mother whispering that it's okay to go. The ordinariness of it is the horror. Death doesn't announce itself. It just arrives, and the world keeps turning, and you're left holding a body that no longer contains the person you loved.

**The Trap** is the "what a blessing" framing — the idea that Chika was a gift who taught invaluable lessons about love and loss. Albom anticipates this and actively resists it. He says explicitly that he would rather have Chika alive than have learned any lesson. The book is not a gratitude narrative. It's a grief narrative, and Albom refuses to prettify it. He doesn't want your sympathy. He doesn't want to inspire you. He wants you to know that his daughter existed, and that she mattered, and that loving her was worth every bit of the pain.

*If I had to do it over again, I would do it the same way. But that doesn't mean I'm grateful for what happened. I'm grateful for what she was.*

The book's weakness is that it sometimes strains for literary effect. Albom's signature style — short sentences, repeated phrases, italicized emphasis — works in small doses but can feel manufactured across a full book. The back-and-forth structure (then vs. now) is effective but occasionally feels gimmicky, as if he's using a novelistic device to manage grief that can't actually be managed. And there's a persistent tension between the public figure writing about grief and the private man experiencing it — Albom can't stop being a storyteller, even when the story is his own, and sometimes you wish he'd just let the feeling sit without trying to shape it into a lesson.

Still, this is the most honest thing Albom has written. It lacks the polished neatness of *Tuesdays with Morrie* or *The Five People You Meet in Heaven*, and that raggedness is exactly what makes it work. Grief is not neat. Neither is this book.
