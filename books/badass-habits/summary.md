Jen Sincero is not the first person you'd expect to write a book about habits. Her previous work was all about the big, dramatic shifts — the soul excavation, the belief overhaul, the leap into the unknown. Habits, by contrast, are small and boring and require the kind of disciplined consistency that doesn't exactly fit Sincero's "wake up and slay" brand. Which is exactly why *Badass Habits* works. It's Sincero doing the unglamorous work of showing that even the most dramatic transformation comes down to what you do on a Tuesday afternoon when no one is watching.

The book's premise is simple but potent: your habits are not just things you do — they're who you are. Every repeated action is a vote for a particular identity. Sincero builds on James Clear's identity-based habit framework but filters it through her own voice, which is less clinical and more confrontational. Where Clear might say "decide the type of person you want to be," Sincero says: "Decide who you want to be, and then start acting like that person even when you don't feel like it, *especially* when you don't feel like it." The emphasis on action over feeling is the book's spine, and Sincero returns to it relentlessly. Your feelings are not reasons — they're weather. You don't cancel your life because it's raining.

What makes *Badass Habits* different from the dozen other excellent habit books on the market is Sincero's willingness to talk about the messy psychological underground that keeps us stuck. She spends significant time on what she calls "the gremlins" — the voices in your head that tell you change is too hard, that you'll fail, that you don't deserve it. Most habit books assume you're coming to the table with neutral or positive motivation. Sincero assumes you're coming to the table with a lifetime of baggage, and she addresses it directly. "Your gremlin isn't trying to hurt you," she writes. "It's trying to protect you from the humiliation of trying and failing. It's just doing a really bad job of it." This psychological framing sets her book apart — she's not just teaching techniques, she's helping you befriend the parts of yourself that resist change.

**The Trap:** The most common habit trap is what Sincero calls "the starter's loop." You get excited about a new habit, you make elaborate plans, you buy the equipment, you tell everyone your goals — and then you crash somewhere between day 3 and day 10. The starter's loop is seductive because the beginning feels good. It's all possibility, no friction. The planning itself provides a dopamine hit that makes you feel like you've already achieved something. Sincero's solution is brutal but effective: stop telling people what you're going to do. Start doing it in silence. The energy you spend announcing your intentions is energy you won't have for executing them. The starter's loop is the reason most New Year's resolutions die by February — all announcement, no follow-through.

Sincero divides the book into four clear sections: identifying your badass self (the identity you're building toward), hacking your environment (making good habits easy and bad habits hard), slaying your gremlins (the psychological work), and scaling up (maintaining momentum and dealing with inevitable stumbles). The environmental hacking section is where Sincero is at her most practical and useful. She has a background in coaching high-performance individuals, and it shows in the tactical specificity of her advice: put your workout clothes next to your bed, not in the drawer. Delete social media from your phone rather than relying on willpower. Create a "habit contract" with a friend who has permission to ask if you've done it. These are not new insights, but Sincero presents them with such energy and humor that they land like fresh discoveries.

The book's most valuable chapter is on what Sincero calls "the forgiveness practice." She argues that the #1 reason people abandon habits is perfectionism — one missed day triggers shame, which triggers more missed days, which triggers full abandonment. Her solution is radical: expect to miss days. Build in the expectation of imperfection. When you miss, forgive yourself immediately and completely, and get back on track the next day. "Miss two days and you're building a new habit of not doing the thing," she warns. "Never miss three." This three-day rule is practical, memorable, and genuinely useful. It gives you a clear boundary: a single slip is fine, but by the third missed day, you're not slipping anymore — you're quitting.

The voice is pure Sincero — profane, direct, and studded with the kind of one-liners that make you laugh and cringe simultaneously because they're aimed directly at you. The writing is conversational to the point of rambling, which works for some chapters and drags in others. Fans of Sincero's previous books will love it; newcomers might find it takes some adjustment.

**The Shift:** What changes when you get good at habits is internal. You stop seeing yourself as someone who tries things and fails and start seeing yourself as someone who decides and executes. Sincero describes this as moving from a "hopeful beginner" identity to a "committed practicer" identity. The difference is everything. Hopeful beginners are at the mercy of their motivation. Committed practicers show up regardless. The shift doesn't happen overnight — it happens one small decision at a time, each one reinforcing the new identity until it becomes automatic.

Sincero adds a twist to the classic habit advice: if a habit takes less than two minutes, do it immediately, not later. Don't put it on your to-do list. Don't schedule it. Do it now. This bypasses the entire decision-making apparatus of your brain and prevents the gremlin from finding reasons to delay. The two-minute rule turns out to be one of the most powerful habit-building tools available because it eliminates the gap between intention and action. When the gap shrinks, resistance has no room to grow. This is why the best habit advice is also the simplest: if you can do it in two minutes or less, do not defer it. Do it now.

**Honest Criticism:** The book is too long for its ideas. Sincero's conversational, digressive style works better in shorter formats. By page 200, you've absorbed the core ideas. The gremlin metaphor, while initially effective, becomes tedious through overuse. For readers already deep into habit science — who have read Clear, Duhigg, and McGonigal — much of this will feel like a more entertaining version of familiar territory. Sincero's unique contribution is the psychological excavation, but she doesn't go deep enough to satisfy readers who want the full therapeutic approach. And the book assumes a level of privilege: her advice about hiring coaches, buying equipment, and restructuring your environment costs money. For someone working multiple jobs or caring for dependents, "hack your environment" means something very different.
