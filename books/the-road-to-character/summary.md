Adam I is the résumé self. He constructs, achieves, competes, and collects. He's the version of you that shows up at a cocktail party with a practiced answer to "So what do you do?" Adam II is the eulogy self. He's the version of you your closest friends would describe at your funeral — the one who was kind, who showed up, who had a quiet integrity that had nothing to do with your LinkedIn profile. David Brooks opens *The Road to Character* with this distinction drawn from Rabbi Joseph Soloveitchik's *The Lonely Man of Faith*, and he doesn't hide which side he thinks we've been neglecting. He wrote the book, he admits, to save his own soul — because he'd spent decades perfecting Adam I and barely attending to Adam II.

The book is not a how-to guide. There are no worksheets or habits to track. Instead, Brooks profiles eight historical figures — people who built deep character — and examines what they did that we don't. It's a quiet book in an age of loud ones. It demands the patience to sit with other people's lives and see what they reveal about your own.

---

Brooks distills what he learned from his subjects into what he calls the "Humility Code" — a set of principles that stand in direct opposition to modern culture's values. These aren't rules he invented. They're patterns he observed in people who lived with genuine character:

**1. We don't have the power to build ourselves.** The modern self-help gospel says you can construct your identity from scratch. Brooks's subjects believed the opposite: you discover who you are by surrendering to something larger. The self is found, not made.

**2. Character is built in struggle.** There is no shortcut that avoids difficulty. Every person Brooks profiles went through something that broke them — and they were rebuilt differently because of it. The comfortable life does not produce character.

**3. Pride is the central vice.** Brooks traces this through every biography. The people who failed morally — and many of his subjects had spectacular moral failures — failed because they overestimated their own virtue. Humility is not self-deprecation. It's honest self-appraisal.

**4. We need moral ecologies.** Character isn't built in isolation. Brooks's subjects were embedded in communities, traditions, and relationships that held them accountable. The modern individual, responsible only to themselves, is vulnerable to self-deception.

**5. The best people are often the most broken.** This is the book's most counterintuitive claim, and Brooks presses it hard. The people with the deepest character weren't those who avoided failure. They were those who were humbled by it and had to rebuild.

> *"The greatest people are not those who tower over others, but those who sink to the level of the common humanity."*

Brooks profiles eight figures, and each one illustrates a different aspect of character:

**Frances Perkins** — the first woman in a US presidential cabinet. Perkins wasn't naturally powerful. She was painfully shy, socially awkward. But she had a fierce commitment to justice that drove her through every obstacle. Her character came not from confidence but from *conviction*. She believed in something more than herself.

**Dwight Eisenhower** — Brooks's treatment of Eisenhower is one of the best in the book. Eisenhower wasn't brilliant, not in the flashy sense. He was patient, disciplined, and ferociously self-controlled. He practiced what Brooks calls "the art of the controlled fire" — managing his anger so precisely that even his closest aides never saw him lose his temper.

**Dorothy Day** — the radical journalist who founded the Catholic Worker movement. Day's conversion to Catholicism was not a comfort — it was a demand. She spent the rest of her life living among the poor, in voluntary poverty, serving people who could never repay her. Her character was forged in the daily, unglamorous work of service.

**George Marshall** — the general who ran the Allied war effort and later designed the Marshall Plan. Marshall was famous for his lack of ego. He refused to write memoirs. He declined honors. When Churchill called him "the architect of victory," Marshall was embarrassed. His character was built on the principle of *self-effacement* — the belief that the mission mattered, not his role in it.

The other profiles — Samuel Johnson, A. Philip Randolph, Bayard Rustin, and the writer and activist Augustine of Hippo — round out a gallery of people who built character the hard way.

> *"The people who have character don't think about having character. They think about doing the right thing."*

The thread running through all the profiles is that these people were fighting battles we don't even acknowledge. They were fighting against their own egos, their own pride, their own tendency toward self-deception. And they had explicit systems for doing so.

Eisenhower kept a diary where he recorded every time he felt angry — and every time he expressed that anger. Not to vent. To *measure* his self-control. Dorothy Day practiced regular confession, not because she was sinning more than anyone else, but because she needed the discipline of naming her failures aloud. Marshall refused to read newspaper columns about himself, because he knew that praise would feed the ego he was trying to starve.

**The Trap of Self-Esteem Culture:** This is where Brooks is at his most provocative. He argues that the modern emphasis on self-esteem and self-affirmation is actually corrosive to character. "If you tell people they're wonderful from the time they're children," he writes, "they may have high self-esteem, but they'll have trouble facing their own failures." The people with deep character didn't think they were wonderful. They thought they were flawed beings in need of constant improvement.

**A story that stopped me cold:** Brooks recounts a scene from Eisenhower's boyhood. Young Dwight got into a violent fistfight with another boy and came home bleeding. His mother bandaged his wounds — then gave him a lecture about the sin of anger that he remembered for the rest of his life. She was not sympathetic. She was not gentle. She was *moral*. She held him to a standard. And Eisenhower credited that moment, more than any other, with teaching him that his impulses needed discipline.

I reread that story several times. It is the opposite of modern parenting. And it produced one of the most disciplined leaders in American history.

> *"The road to character is the road of struggle against the self."*

Brooks quotes Kant's old line about "the crooked timber of humanity" — that nothing straight was ever built from it. The point is that human beings are fundamentally flawed. Character doesn't come from denying this, but from accepting it and building structures — internal and external — that compensate for it.

This is the book's most important insight: the people with the deepest character were not optimists about human nature. They were realists. They knew their own capacity for self-deception, selfishness, and pride. And they built their lives accordingly — with accountability structures, spiritual disciplines, and moral communities.

The modern alternative is the belief that people are basically good and that problems come from external systems. Brooks sees this as naive. The real battle, he argues, is internal — and it never ends.

The portraits are uneven. Some — Eisenhower, Marshall, Perkins — are richly drawn. Others, particularly Samuel Johnson and Augustine, feel rushed. Brooks is clearly most comfortable with political and military figures, and the literary and religious portraits suffer in comparison.

The book also has a nostalgic tone that can wear thin. Brooks is essentially arguing that we used to know how to build character and we've forgotten. There's truth in this, but the golden-age framing ignores the ways that earlier eras were genuinely oppressive. The "moral ecology" Brooks celebrates included systematic racism, sexism, and religious intolerance. The question isn't just "what did we lose?" but "what can we reclaim without the baggage?"

And the book has no real prescription. It identifies the problem — we've neglected Adam II — and shows what Adam II looked like in exceptional lives. But it doesn't tell you, specifically, what to do. The implication is that you should read these biographies and figure it out yourself. That's a defensible stance, but it leaves the reader with inspiration and no instruction.

If you've felt the quiet pull toward being a better person without quite knowing what that means, this book will give you a vocabulary and a gallery of examples. It's a book to read slowly, one chapter at a time, letting each biography sit. Skip it if you want action steps. Read it if you want company.

The distinction between résumé virtues and eulogy virtues has lodged itself permanently in my mental landscape. I catch myself testing every decision against it: "Which self am I serving right now?" It's an uncomfortable question. Most of the time, the answer is Adam I. But just asking it makes Adam II a little stronger.

And Eisenhower's anger journal. I started one after reading this. Not because I'm particularly angry, but because I realized I had never systematically paid attention to my own emotional patterns. That's the kind of small, unglamorous practice that builds character over decades. Most people will never see it. It doesn't go on a résumé. But Eisenhower did it for years, and it made him a different kind of man.

1. **The two selves audit.** Take a piece of paper. Draw a line down the middle. Left column: the résumé virtues you're cultivating. Right column: the eulogy virtues. Compare the time and energy you're investing in each. Be honest.

2. **Read one biography.** Brooks's method is the book's real curriculum — learning from other lives. Pick one person known for depth of character and read a good biography (not a Wikipedia page). Eisenhower, Dorothy Day, and Frances Perkins are Brooks's best profiles.

3. **Name your weakness.** In the tradition of Brooks's subjects, identify one character flaw you know you have — not a career gap, a character gap. Say it out loud to someone you trust. The act of naming is the first step of discipline.
