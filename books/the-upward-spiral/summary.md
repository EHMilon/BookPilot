Depression is a downward spiral. Your mood drops, so you withdraw. Withdrawal makes you feel worse, so you withdraw more. Each step down makes the next one more likely. Alex Korb, a UCLA neuroscientist, read hundreds of studies on this well-documented phenomenon. Then he asked a different question: if spiraling down is a physical process in the brain, can you *reverse* the direction?

*The Upward Spiral* is his answer. It's not a memoir, not a self-help guide in the traditional sense. It's a neuroscience handbook for building momentum in the opposite direction. Each chapter covers one brain mechanism that contributes to depression and one concrete action that disrupts it. The message: you don't need a breakthrough. You need a nudge — a single positive step that triggers a chain reaction.

> "Depression isn't just a cognitive or emotional problem — it's a physical problem with the brain's circuitry. And you can change that circuitry with the right behaviors."

The book's organizing idea is **positive feedback loops**, the opposite of depressive spirals. Your brain has circuits for mood, motivation, sleep, and social connection. These circuits don't exist in isolation — they influence each other. Improving your sleep improves your mood. Improving your mood improves your social motivation. Social connection reduces inflammation, which improves mood further. One small improvement ripples outward.

Korb's first and most surprising intervention: **make a decision**. Any decision. Neuroscience research shows that the act of making a choice — even an inconsequential one — activates the prefrontal cortex's reward circuits. Indecision, the hallmark of depression, creates a feedback loop of paralysis. You can't decide, so nothing happens. Nothing happens, so you feel helpless. You feel helpless, so you can't decide.

Korb explains: making a decision changes your perception of control. You go from "things happen to me" to "I make things happen." This shift activates the striatum, a brain region involved in motivation and reward processing. The content of the decision matters less than the *act* of deciding. Choose what to eat for dinner. Pick a movie. Set a time to go for a walk. The choice itself is medicine.

> "Making decisions includes creating intentions and setting goals — all of which are proven to help you change your brain. Doing something is better than doing nothing, even if it's the wrong thing."

Depressed people think they need to make the *right* decision before they can move forward. This is paralysis by analysis. Korb says the best decision is the one you make. Wrong decisions can be corrected. No decisions keep you stuck. The goal is movement, not perfection.

Exercise is next. This is the least surprising intervention in any mental health book, but Korb provides the neuroscience that makes it unavoidable. Exercise increases dopamine (motivation), serotonin (mood), norepinephrine (energy and focus), and BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which promotes the growth of new neurons). It reduces cortisol (stress) and inflammation (linked to depression). If a drug did all of this, it would be the bestselling medication in history.

But here's the key insight from Korb's research: **you don't need much**. A 10-minute walk is enough to trigger the neurochemical cascade. The problem isn't that exercise is ineffective — it's that depressed people think they need a full workout routine. They don't. They need to get their heart rate up for a few minutes. Anything beyond that is bonus.

> "Exercise changes the brain in ways that are more powerful than any antidepressant, yet it's free and has no side effects. The only problem is actually doing it."

Korb introduces the concept of **behavioral activation**, a therapeutic approach where you schedule pleasant activities regardless of how you feel. The depressed brain says "I don't feel like it, so I won't." Behavioral activation says "do it anyway, and motivation will follow." This sounds like toxic positivity, but the neuroscience supports it: dopamine is released *during* goal-directed behavior, not before. You don't need to feel motivated to start. You need to start to feel motivated.

People wait for motivation to strike before taking action. Korb says motivation is a *result* of action, not a prerequisite. The sequence is: act → dopamine → feel better → act more. Not: feel better → act. If you're waiting for the motivation fairy, you'll wait forever.

Korb explores social connection with equal depth. Depression makes you isolate yourself. Isolation makes your depression worse. It's a classic downward spiral. Korb's counterintuitive advice: **social connection doesn't require deep conversation**. Even minimal, low-stakes social contact — saying hello to a barista, brief eye contact with a passerby, standing near others — triggers oxytocin and reduces cortisol. The brain's social circuitry responds to *any* positive social signal, not just intimate conversations.

This is liberating for someone too exhausted for deep friendship maintenance. You don't have to pour your heart out. You just need to be near people in a low-pressure setting. A coffee shop. A park bench. A group fitness class where nobody talks. The proximity itself helps.

The chapter on **gratitude** makes similar neuroscience claims: counting blessings activates the prefrontal cortex and increases dopamine. But Korb doesn't just say "be grateful." He explains *why* it's hard for a depressed brain. The depressed brain has a negativity bias — it notices what's wrong, not what's right. Gratitude isn't toxic positivity; it's a deliberate counterweight to a brain that's tilted toward the negative. Writing down three good things each day physically strengthens the neural pathways for positive attention.

> "The brain is not designed to be happy. It's designed to survive. Gratitude is a hack that tricks the survival system into recognizing what's already working."

People use gratitude as a way to bypass their real pain — "I should be grateful, so I shouldn't feel sad." That's not gratitude, it's suppression. Korb's version is different: acknowledge the pain *and* acknowledge the good. Both can be true. The practice isn't erasing the negative — it's making sure the positive gets airtime too.

The honest weakness of *The Upward Spiral*: it's too clean. Each chapter offers one intervention and one brain mechanism, which makes for easy reading but flattens the complexity of depression. Real depression is messy. The same intervention doesn't work every time. The book's bullet-point neuroscience can feel like a checklist for a car repair when what you're experiencing is more like a flood. Patches won't hold.

There's also the problem of accessibility. Korb assumes you're functional enough to take a walk, make a decision, and write down three gratitudes. Severe depression — the kind where you can't shower, can't eat, can't remember what gratitude feels like — isn't addressed. The book's interventions assume a baseline that not everyone has.

The social connection chapter is also oddly thin on structural factors. If you're isolated because of social anxiety, poverty, or living in a remote area, "stand near people" is not a solution. The book doesn't bridge the gap between neuroscience advice and real-world barriers.

Pick one tiny upward spiral today. A single decision you've been avoiding — make it right now, even if it's the wrong one. Then take a 10-minute walk — no fitness gear required, no destination needed. Before bed, write down one thing that went okay today. Not great. Not life-changing. Just okay. These three actions — decision, movement, recognition — might be enough to tilt the spiral one degree in the right direction. Tomorrow, that degree might become two. The direction matters more than the speed.
