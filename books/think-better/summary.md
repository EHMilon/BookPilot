Most thinking books teach you to think *more* — more logically, more critically, more systematically. Tim Hurson's *Think Better* teaches you to think *differently*. It's a book about productive thinking, which Hurson defines as the ability to generate ideas that are both novel and useful. Not just creative. Not just logical. Both.

The premise is simple: most of us have been trained to think in one gear. We analyze problems, find the "right" answer, and move on. But the most important problems don't have a single right answer. They have multiple possible answers, and the first one you find is rarely the best. Productive thinking is the discipline of generating better options before you commit.

---

Hurson starts by distinguishing two modes. **Critical thinking** evaluates ideas — it judges, filters, and selects. **Productive thinking** generates ideas — it creates, combines, and expands. Both are necessary. But most of us are trained only in critical thinking. We can tear an idea apart before it's fully formed.

*"Critical thinking without productive thinking is like having a high-powered microscope but no samples to look at. You can analyze everything perfectly — but you have nothing to analyze."*

**The Trap:** Schools reward critical thinking. Tests ask you to find the correct answer. But in the real world, the hardest part isn't choosing between options — it's generating options worth choosing. If you only have one mediocre idea, the best critical analysis in the world won't save you.

Hurson's core contribution is a six-step process called the **Productive Thinking Model**:

**Step 1: What's Going On?** — Before you solve anything, understand the situation. What's the problem? What's the context? What assumptions are you making? Most people skip this step and jump straight to solutions.

**Step 2: What's Success?** — Define what a good outcome looks like. Not vaguely ("I want to be happy") but specifically. What would success feel like, look like, measure like? Hurson calls this the **future pull** — a clear vision of success pulls you forward.

**Step 3: What's the Question?** — The quality of your answers is limited by the quality of your questions. Hurson advocates for **question-storming**: generating as many questions about the problem as possible before trying to answer them. A good question reframes the entire problem.

**Step 4: Generate Ideas** — Now you brainstorm. But not randomly. Hurson provides specific techniques: **forced connections** (combine unrelated concepts), **challenge assumptions** (what if the opposite were true?), **SCAMPER** (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other uses, Eliminate, Reverse).

**Step 5: Forge the Solution** — Evaluate and refine your ideas. This is where critical thinking comes back in. Which ideas are feasible? Which are worth developing? Hurson uses a **PPCo** framework: Pluses, Potentials, Concerns. For each idea, name what's good, what's possible, and what's concerning.

**Step 6: Align Resources** — Implementation. Who needs to be involved? What obstacles exist? What's the timeline? An idea that never leaves your head isn't a solution.

*"Most creativity fails not because the idea was bad, but because the implementation was never planned."*

**The Trap:** People get stuck on Step 4 and never proceed. They love brainstorming but hate the hard work of evaluation and implementation. Or they skip to Step 6 without doing the earlier work — they start implementing before they've understood the problem.

This is Hurson's most distinctive technique. Instead of brainstorming solutions, you brainstorm questions. What do we need to know? What's the real problem? What haven't we considered? The rule: no answers allowed during a question-storm. Only questions.

The effect is freeing. When you can't offer solutions, you're forced to explore the problem more deeply. And it's often in the exploration — in the reframing — that the real insight emerges.

*"A problem well-stated is a problem half-solved. But most problems are half-stated, poorly framed, and resting on unexamined assumptions."*

**The Trap:** We're answer addicts. When someone presents a problem, we immediately start generating solutions. It makes us feel competent. But premature solutions are almost always superficial. Question-storming forces you to sit in the discomfort of not-knowing longer. That's where the good stuff lives.

Hurson's most creative technique is **forced connections**. Take two unrelated concepts and force them together. How is my problem like a garden? Like a bicycle? Like a symphony? The absurdity is the point — by forcing connections that don't naturally exist, your brain has to make new neural pathways.

*"Creativity is not about having original ideas. It's about making original connections between existing ideas. The more diverse your input, the more connections you can make."*

The business world has internalized this: the most innovative companies hire people from different fields. But individuals can do the same. Read outside your domain. Travel. Talk to people in different industries. The raw material for creative connections is diversity of experience.

**The Trap:** Most creative techniques fail because people don't push far enough. They try one forced connection, it feels silly, and they give up. The breakthrough usually comes at connection number 15 or 20. Quantity generates quality.

The book dedicates significant space to what Hurson calls the **internal censor** — the voice in your head that judges ideas before they're fully formed. "That's stupid." "That won't work." "That's been tried before." This voice is useful in evaluation mode, but deadly in generation mode.

Hurson's solution is **deferred judgment**: separate idea generation from idea evaluation. During brainstorming, any idea is allowed. No criticism. No "yes, but." Every idea is a stepping stone. During evaluation, bring the censor back. But don't let it speak during generation.

*"The internal censor is a nightclub bouncer who throws people out before they've had a chance to dance. It doesn't know which ideas are good — it just knows which ones are safe."*

---

*Think Better* argues that productive thinking is a skill, not a gift. It can be learned, practiced, and improved. The key insight: creativity isn't about waiting for inspiration — it's about having a systematic process that generates ideas on demand. The Productive Thinking Model gives you a structure: understand the problem, define success, ask better questions, generate options, evaluate, and implement. None of these steps is optional. Most people skip the first two and the last one.

Question-storming. I've used it dozens of times since reading this book. The simple act of saying "no answers, only questions" changes the entire dynamic of a problem-solving conversation. People stop trying to look smart and start actually exploring. The best question almost always produces a better solution than any initial answer.

The second thing that lodged itself in my thinking: the concept of deferred judgment and how rarely we practice it. We're so quick to evaluate — "that won't work," "we tried that," "that's too expensive" — that most ideas die before they've had a chance to breathe. The discipline of separating generation from evaluation is simple in theory, almost impossible in practice, especially in meetings where people feel pressure to demonstrate their intelligence by finding flaws. The few teams I've seen enforce this separation — no criticism during brainstorming, no exceptions — produce ideas that are not just more numerous but genuinely more creative. The quality emerges from the quantity, but only if the censor is kept silent long enough.

The book is structured like a textbook — useful but not always engaging. Hurson's examples lean heavily on corporate brainstorming sessions, which can feel dated and narrow. The six-step framework is comprehensive but can feel mechanical — problems in real life don't always cooperate with a linear process. And the book doesn't adequately address what happens when your creative solution is rejected by an organization that doesn't want creativity. Implementation isn't just about resources — it's about politics, culture, and power.
