**The Obesity Code** by Dr. Jason Fung

You've heard it a thousand times: obesity is caused by eating too much and moving too little. Calories in, calories out. Eat less, move more. It's simple physics. And if it were true, the obesity epidemic would have reversed years ago, because we've been telling people to eat less and move more for five decades, and the rates have only climbed. Dr. Jason Fung arrives at this paradox with the same surgical precision he brought to diabetes: our model of obesity is wrong, and the wrong model produces the wrong treatments. *The Obesity Code* argues that obesity is not an energy-balance disorder. It's a hormonal disorder — specifically, a disorder of insulin. And you can't fix a hormonal problem with willpower.

**The carbohydrate-insulin hypothesis.** Fung's central claim: insulin is the master regulator of fat storage. When insulin is high, the body stores fat. When insulin is low, the body burns fat. This is basic endocrinology. The problem in modern life is that we eat foods that keep insulin elevated all day long — refined carbohydrates, sugar, frequent meals, and snacks. The result is that the body's "set point" for weight — the weight your body naturally defends — gets pushed upward. Conventional weight-loss advice — eat less, exercise more — doesn't lower insulin. In fact, caloric restriction often increases the stress hormone cortisol, which can actually raise insulin and make the body *more* efficient at storing fat. This is why most diets fail. It's not that people lack willpower. It's that the hormonal environment is working against them.

**The failure of calorie counting.** Fung dismantles the calorie model with both logic and evidence. If a calorie were a calorie, then all calories would be metabolized the same way. But they're not. Protein and carbohydrate and fat trigger completely different hormonal responses. Fructose is processed differently from glucose. The type of calorie matters as much as the number. More damningly, the "calories in" side is not independent of the "calories out" side — when you eat less, your metabolism slows down to compensate. The body is not a passive calorie counter. It's an active regulator that adjusts energy expenditure based on energy availability. Weight-loss apps track calories as if they were a bank account. The body doesn't work that way. Cutting 500 calories from your diet doesn't mean you'll lose 500 calories of fat. Your body will reduce its energy expenditure to compensate.

**Refined carbohydrates: the real enemy.** The foods that spike insulin most dramatically are refined carbohydrates and sugar. White bread, pasta, rice, breakfast cereals, pastries, soda, juice — these are not "empty calories" that add to a daily total. They are hormonal signals that tell the body to store energy as fat. Fung points out that the rise in obesity correlates almost perfectly with the rise in refined carbohydrate consumption, not with total calories. "Low-fat" foods, which were promoted as healthy, are often loaded with sugar and refined carbs to compensate for the lack of taste. The low-fat era didn't just not solve obesity — it may have been a primary cause.

**The role of fasting.** The most powerful tool Fung offers is **intermittent fasting**. If high insulin is the cause of obesity, then the treatment is to give the body periods of low insulin — periods when you don't eat. Fasting is not starvation; it's giving the body time to process what it has already stored. Fung explains the difference: starvation is forced and prolonged; fasting is voluntary and time-limited. During a fast, insulin drops, the body switches to burning stored fat, and the metabolic machinery restores itself. Fung cites studies showing that intermittent fasting, independent of calorie restriction, improves insulin sensitivity and promotes fat loss. The type of fast matters too — 16-hour daily fasts (the 16:8 protocol) work well for most people, while 24-hour or longer fasts may be more effective for breaking through weight loss plateaus. We've been taught that breakfast is essential, that eating small frequent meals keeps metabolism high, and that fasting puts the body into "starvation mode." Fung shows the research behind each of these claims and finds them either weakly supported or directly contradicted by evidence. The "snacking culture" of the last thirty years — eating six times a day — may be one of the worst dietary changes we've ever made.

**Sleep, stress, and the obesity epidemic.** Fung extends the hormonal argument beyond diet. Cortisol, the stress hormone, also raises insulin. Chronic sleep deprivation raises cortisol and lowers the hormones that signal fullness (leptin and GLP-1). The modern trifecta — too much sugar, too little sleep, too much stress — creates a hormonal environment that actively promotes weight gain. This is why obesity correlates with poverty, shift work, and urban life. We treat the "obesity epidemic" as a problem of individual choices. Fung argues it's a problem of the environment we've built. Telling an overworked, sleep-deprived, stressed person to "eat less and move more" is like telling a drowning person to swim harder.

**Set point theory.** Fung introduces the concept of the body's **set point** for weight — the weight range that your body defends through hormonal regulation. When you lose weight, your body fights back: it increases hunger hormones (ghrelin), decreases fullness hormones (leptin), and slows metabolism. This is not a bug; it's a survival mechanism. Most diets fail because they trigger the body's defense systems. The only way to change the set point is to change the hormonal environment, specifically by lowering insulin for sustained periods. This is the cruelest truth in the book. It means that "a little bit of weight loss" is often counterproductive — your body will fight to regain it, plus more. Lasting weight change requires not a diet but a hormonal reset.

The idea I couldn't shake after reading *The Obesity Code* is the **hormonal model of obesity**. I'd absorbed the calorie-counting model like everyone else, and watching Fung dismantle it with evidence was unsettling in the best way. The second idea that stuck is the **set point** concept — knowing that my body has defenses against weight loss that have nothing to do with discipline changed how I think about anyone struggling with weight. The problem is not weakness. The problem is biology fighting against the environment.

Same issues as *The Diabetes Code* — Fung is a polemicist, not a neutral presenter. He overstates the certainty of the carbohydrate-insulin hypothesis and understates the role of genetics, gut microbiome, and other factors. The randomized controlled trial evidence for intermittent fasting is growing but still relatively young. Fung also doesn't engage seriously with edge cases — people with eating disorders, pregnant women, or those with certain medical conditions need very different approaches. The book is repetitive; after the first few chapters, you've internalized the main argument, and the rest is evidence stacking. And while Fung is great at diagnosis, his prescriptions are simpler than the problem deserves: fasting and low-carb work for many but not for all.

Anyone who has struggled with weight and felt that "eat less, move more" doesn't work. Also useful for doctors and dietitians who want to understand a fundamental rethinking of how we approach obesity. People with a history of eating disorders — fasting can be triggering. Also, anyone who is satisfied with the current dietary paradigm and doesn't want it challenged. If you're looking for a gentle, moderate approach to weight loss, this book is not gentle or moderate.

**Why this book matters more now than when it was written.** The failure rates of conventional dieting and weight-loss programs continue to confirm Fung's thesis. As GLP-1 agonists like Ozempic become blockbuster drugs, The Obesity Code's argument that medication treats symptoms, not root causes, is more relevant than ever. Fung would say the new generation of weight-loss drugs is just a more sophisticated version of the same mistake: suppressing appetite instead of fixing the hormonal dysfunction.
