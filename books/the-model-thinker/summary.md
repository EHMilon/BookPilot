When you build a sandbox, you can pour any shape you want into it. But the sandbox itself has edges. You can't build beyond them. Scott Page's argument in *The Model Thinker* is that our minds are the same way: each mental model — a simplified representation of reality — is a sandbox. It captures some things perfectly and misses others entirely. Use one model and you'll see one pattern. Use ten models and you'll see the forest.

But here's the catch: most of us don't have ten models. We have two or three — the ones we learned in our field, the ones that feel intuitive, the ones that worked before. And when a problem doesn't fit those models, we force it, like a child trying to fit a star-shaped block into a square hole. The result: bad predictions, bad decisions, and the baffling feeling that reality keeps surprising us.

The antidote is **multimodel thinking** — the practice of using a diverse toolkit of models to understand any situation. Not to find the "right" model, but to see the problem from enough angles that you can't be completely wrong.

---

Page starts with a demonstration. Take any complex system — a city, a market, an ecosystem. A single model can explain some of its behavior. A second model explains different behavior. A third explains something else. None of them is complete. But together, they converge on something useful.

*"All models are wrong, but some are useful. The insight is that collections of models, built from diverse perspectives, are far more useful than any single model."*

The math: if each model is wrong in different ways, the errors cancel out. This is the **wisdom of the crowds** applied to models themselves. No single model captures reality. But a diverse collection of models — each with different assumptions, different blind spots, different simplifications — will capture more of reality than any one of them.

**The Trap:** Our education and professional training push toward depth in a single paradigm. Economists learn economic models. Engineers learn engineering models. Biologists learn biological models. But the most interesting problems — poverty, climate change, organizational design — don't respect disciplinary boundaries. If you only have one toolbox, every problem looks like a nail.

Page organizes the book around model types. A few highlights:

**Linear models.** The simplest: X causes Y. More education leads to higher income. More advertising leads to more sales. Linear models are easy to build and easy to understand. They're also almost always wrong — reality has feedback loops, thresholds, and non-linearities that linear models miss.

**Probability models.** The world is uncertain. Probability models help you quantify that uncertainty. The **normal distribution** (bell curve) is the most famous, but Page warns against its overuse. Many real-world phenomena — wealth, city sizes, pandemic case counts — follow **power law distributions** where extreme events are far more common than the bell curve predicts.

*"If you only know one distribution, the normal distribution, you will consistently underestimate the probability of extreme events. You'll be surprised by financial crises, pandemics, and technological disruptions — when you should have expected them."*

**Network models.** Everything is connected. Network models reveal how information, disease, and influence spread. The key insight: the structure of the network matters more than the strength of the connections. A few highly connected "hubs" can spread something through the entire system. That's why influencers exist — and why superspreader events drive pandemics.

**Game theory models.** Strategic interaction. When your outcome depends on what others do, game theory provides the framework. The **Prisoner's Dilemma** — two criminals, each choosing to cooperate or defect — explains why individually rational choices produce collectively stupid outcomes. It's the model behind everything from arms races to climate negotiations.

**Agent-based models.** This is Page's specialty. Instead of assuming equilibrium (like traditional economics), agent-based models simulate thousands of individual actors following simple rules — and watch what patterns emerge. The result: **emergent phenomena** that couldn't be predicted from the rules alone. Traffic jams, market crashes, and the evolution of cooperation are all emergent phenomena.

Page devotes substantial attention to the famous **wisdom of crowds** effect: when you aggregate many independent judgments, the average is often surprisingly accurate. But the effect depends on two conditions: **diversity** (people use different information and methods) and **independence** (they don't copy each other).

*"Diversity is not just a nice-to-have. It's a mathematical necessity for collective accuracy. A crowd of clones is no smarter than one person."*

**The Trap:** Organizations that prize consensus destroy the conditions for wisdom. When everyone conforms to the same framework, reads the same reports, and talks to the same people, the "crowd" is effectively one person with many voices. Diversity without independence is just groupthink with a better wardrobe.

One of Page's most practical applications is **prediction markets** — markets where people bet on outcomes. The market price represents the collective belief. And prediction markets consistently beat expert forecasts across domains — elections, sports, product launches, even Oscar winners.

Why? Because markets aggregate diverse information. Each trader brings their own model, their own data, their own bias. The market price is the weighted average of all those models. It's not perfect — but it's consistently better than any single expert.

*"When you want to know what will happen, don't ask the smartest person in the room. Ask the room."*

**The Trap:** We love experts. We want someone who *knows*. But expertise is narrow — a brilliant economist can be terrible at predicting geopolitics. The multimodel approach: instead of looking for the perfect expert, aggregate diverse perspectives. You'll be wrong less often.

One of the most important warnings in the book: **overfitting** — the tendency to build models that explain the past perfectly but fail on new data. This is the curse of complexity. A simple model that's wrong in predictable ways is more useful than a complex model that perfectly fits old data.

*"An overfit model is worse than no model at all. It gives you the illusion of understanding while guaranteeing you'll be surprised."*

**The Trap:** In business, we reward people who can tell compelling stories about the past — narratives that make perfect sense of what happened. But these narratives are often overfit. They explain everything except the future. The best thinkers aren't the ones with the clearest explanations of the past. They're the ones whose models produce the most accurate predictions going forward.

---

*The Model Thinker* is a manifesto for intellectual diversity. The central argument: no single model can capture reality. Use one model and you'll see one pattern — and miss everything else. Use a diverse toolkit of models and you'll see the full picture. The book's practical payoff: you don't need to be an expert in everything. You just need enough models — Page suggests about a dozen — to see any problem from multiple angles. The errors cancel. The insights compound. You become harder to fool, easier to convince, and more useful in unfamiliar situations.

The idea that model diversity works as a mathematical principle, not just a nice sentiment. I'd always thought "get diverse perspectives" was good advice. I didn't realize it was a *provable* improvement — that a collection of diverse, imperfect models will reliably outperform any single model, including one that's more accurate on its own. This changes how I think about teams, information sources, and even my own thinking.

The book is dense — 448 pages of model descriptions with mathematical notation. Page is a professor and writes like one. The material is fascinating but requires real effort to digest. Some readers will find the math intimidating, though Page does an admirable job of keeping it accessible. The biggest weakness: the book has more explanatory power than prescriptive power. It tells you *why* multimodel thinking works but doesn't give you a clear system for building your own toolkit. You finish knowing you should use more models, but not always sure which ones to apply when.
