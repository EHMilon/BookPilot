There's a story the Heath brothers tell early in **Made to Stick** that will ruin you for boring communication forever. It's about a nurse who found a patient's heart stopping, called a code, and watched the crash cart arrive — but no one could find the defibrillator pads. The pads were in a different place than expected, and by the time someone found them, the patient died. The hospital investigated and produced a report. The punchline? The report was 263 pages. Nobody read the whole thing. And the exact same error happened again in another room three months later. Somebody needed to make that story *stick*.

Chip and Dan Heath wrote this book to answer one question: why do some ideas survive while others evaporate? It's not complexity — many stickable ideas are genuinely sophisticated. It's not truth — plenty of false ideas outlive correct ones. The difference is structural. Ideas that stick share a pattern, and the Heaths compressed that pattern into an acronym: SUCCESs.

> "A sticky idea is an idea that is understood, remembered, and changes something."

The first virtue of sticky ideas is simplicity — but not the kind that dumbs things down. The Heaths call it *finding the core*. Southwest Airlines' core strategy: "We are THE low-fare airline." Not "we compete on operational efficiency and customer delight." Low-fare. That's it. Every decision at Southwest is measured against that one sentence. If a new route doesn't support low fares, they don't do it.

The enemy of sticky is *choice paralysis*. Every time you add another point, you dilute every other point. The commander's intent in the military — frontline troops don't need to know every detail of the battle plan, they need to know the single outcome the commander wants. Everything else is subordinate.

> "If you say three things, you don't say anything."

**The Trap:** Smart people love complexity. They think a simple message is a dumbed-down message, so they layer in nuance, exceptions, and caveats until the idea is intellectually bulletproof and practically useless. The real skill is *finding* the core — which requires knowing what *not* to say.

The shift: simplicity isn't about being reductive. It's about being *prioritizing*. What's the one thing that makes everything else make sense? Say that.

The second virtue exploits a quirk of attention. Our brains are pattern-matching machines. They filter out the expected — that's how you can drive home without remembering the last ten minutes. To *get* attention, you have to break the pattern.

The Heaths use the example of a flight attendant on a safety demo. Everyone tunes out. But one attendant started with: "There may be 50 ways to leave your lover, but there are only 6 ways to leave this plane." Surprise. Attention. Now they're listening. Surprise gets the brain to sit up and say "what?"

But surprise doesn't last. The next layer is *interest* — keeping attention by creating a gap in knowledge. The Heaths call this the *Gap Theory of Curiosity*. When people sense they're missing something, they want to close the gap. A movie trailer doesn't work by showing the whole plot; it works by showing *enough* to make you feel the gap. Same with a mystery, a puzzle, or a headline.

**The Trap:** Most communicators aim for clarity first and interest second. They lead with "the purpose of this presentation is to update you on quarterly metrics." That's the pattern-matching brain's off switch. Interest doesn't come after clarity — it *enables* clarity.

The shift: before you explain anything, ask yourself *what's the gap?* What do people not know that they need to know? Open with that gap, not with your conclusion.

Abstraction is the death of stickiness. "We need to foster synergies across verticals" — no image forms in the mind. But "a $20 glass table shattered in the lobby because maintenance and design didn't talk" — that's a picture.

The Heaths tell the story of a school principal who wanted to reduce the number of students going to the nurse with vague complaints. She could have sent a memo about "reducing unnecessary healthcare utilization." Instead, she pointed out a specific problem: *ice*. Kids were coming to the nurse for ice for minor bumps, and the nurse was running out of ice for real emergencies. "Save the ice for kids who really need it." Concrete. Memorable. Actionable.

> "Abstraction makes it harder to understand an idea and to remember it. It also makes it harder to coordinate our activities with others."

**The Trap:** Experts suffer from the *Curse of Knowledge*. Once you know something deeply, it becomes hard to imagine not knowing it. You speak in abstractions because the concrete details feel obvious to you — but they're not obvious to your audience. You can't un-know what you know.

The shift: can you describe your idea in terms a non-expert could *see*? If it can't be drawn, it can't be sticky.

People need to believe an idea before they'll act on it. The obvious route to credibility is authority — an expert, a study, a statistic. But the Heaths show that *anti-authority* can be just as powerful. When a former smoker with a hole in their throat says "smoking killed my voice," they're more credible than a doctor with a pamphlet. Why? Because they have *skin in the game*. They're not trying to convince you; they're just telling you what happened.

Statistics are powerful but cold. The Heaths' rule: *use one statistic with a story.* A statistic alone is abstract. A story alone is anecdotal. Together, they're evidence. The idea of *human-scale* — instead of a billion dollars, say "enough to buy every American a cup of coffee."

> "Statistics are helpful, but they rarely generate the 'aha' moment all by themselves."

**The Trap:** People bury their audiences in data, thinking more numbers = more credibility. But the brain doesn't process data — it processes *meaning*. A single vivid, believable story can outweigh a spreadsheet.

The shift: for every claim you make, ask: *what's the human-scale proof?* Find the story, the anti-authority, the testable detail.

People don't act on logic alone. They act on feelings. The Heaths show that one of the most reliable ways to generate emotion is through the *Mother Teresa principle*: "If I look at the one, I will act. If I look at the mass, I will not." Donated kidneys get more attention than statistical deaths. A single child in a well gets millions in donations; a famine affecting thousands gets shrugged at.

This is why the "Save the Children" ads show *one* child, not a graph. The brain processes individual suffering emotionally; mass suffering statistically. This is not a bug — it's how we're wired.

> "Don't splash in the statistics. Find the one person."

**The Trap:** Professionals (especially in business) think appeals to emotion are unprofessional. They stick to "the data shows" and wonder why no one acts. But data without emotion is just information. Emotion is the engine of action.

The shift: who is the *one person* affected by your idea? Tell their story. Then bring in the data.

The SUCCESs framework isn't a checklist — it's a *recipe*. And the final ingredient, stories, is where everything comes together. The Heaths argue that stories are the most powerful form of sticky communication because they simulate experience. When you hear a story, your brain doesn't just process language — it *experiences* the events.

Mental flight simulators. Stories let people rehearse decisions in a low-stakes environment. The "Chicken Soup" series, the famous New Coke disaster, the Jared Subway story — all sticky because they're stories. They show cause and effect, struggle and solution, a world where choices have consequences.

> "Stories are like flight simulators for the brain."

**The Trap:** People tell stories that are *nice* but not *useful*. A good sticky story has a clear application — it's not just entertainment. It teaches something. The best stories have a *moral* that doesn't need to be stated.

The shift: instead of giving advice, tell a story that *shows* the advice in action. Trust your audience to draw the conclusion.

**The core takeaway** of **Made to Stick** is that stickiness isn't magic — it's a craft. The six principles, simple to understand and hard to execute, form a checklist you can apply to any idea. Not every idea needs every principle, but every sticky idea uses at least three.

**What will actually stick** is the *Curse of Knowledge*. That single concept — you literally can't imagine what it's like not to know what you know — explains almost every communication failure in the world. The expert who talks over the beginner's head. The executive whose strategy is crystal clear to him but opaque to everyone else. The teacher who can't understand why students don't get it. The curse is real, and the Heaths' framework is the antidote.

**The honest criticism** is that **Made to Stick** can feel formulaic. The SUCCESs framework is brilliant but the book's own examples sometimes feel *too* clean — as though the Heaths only chose stories that fit their model. The chapter on storytelling is the weakest; it feels rushed, and the advice on *how* to craft a story is thin compared to the depth of the other chapters. Some readers will also find the tone *too* chipper — the Heaths write with relentless enthusiasm that occasionally reads like a motivational newsletter.
