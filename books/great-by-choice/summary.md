**Great by Choice** by Jim Collins and Morten Hansen

If *Built to Last* asked what makes companies endure across generations, and *Good to Great* asked what turns mediocrity into excellence, *Great by Choice* asks a different question: what happens when the world outside is genuinely hostile? The book emerged from a question Collins couldn't let go of after 9/11 and the dot-com crash. In a chaotic, unpredictable environment — what he calls a "world of high uncertainty and massive disruption" — why do some companies not just survive but thrive, while others of equal talent and resources get crushed? Collins and Hansen studied companies that outperformed their industries by at least ten times over fifteen-plus years in turbulent conditions. The result is a book less about growth and more about resilience — and it's the leanest, most focused thing Collins has written.

**The 10Xers vs. the comparison companies.** Collins's method is to find "10X companies" — those that beat their industry index by at least 10x over the study period — and compare them to similar companies in the same industry that faced the same challenges but failed to thrive. The 10Xers, it turns out, are not faster, more innovative, more visionary, or luckier. They're disciplined. Fanatically, almost boringly disciplined. In a chaotic world, our instinct is to move faster, make bigger bets, and react more aggressively. Collins's data says the opposite: the 10Xers are more conservative, more measured, and more consistent than their peers.

**Fanatic Discipline — the 20 Mile March.** This is the book's signature concept. Imagine you're walking from San Diego to the tip of South America. If you march 20 miles every day, no matter what — not 10 when it's beautiful, not 40 when you feel great — you'll make it. The 20 Mile March is about setting a performance floor and ceiling that you stick to, regardless of the weather. In good times, you don't overextend. In bad times, you don't panic and cut. The 10X companies had explicit "20 Mile March" criteria — specific growth or profit targets they met every year, boom or bust. Collins uses the example of Amgen, which committed to a specific annual revenue growth target and hit it almost every year for two decades, even during recessions. The comparison companies were erratic: they overhired in good years and fired in bad ones, invested too much when confident and too little when scared. They rode the emotional roller coaster of their industry while the 10Xers simply kept marching. We confuse consistency with complacency. The 20 Mile March is not easy; it's harder than sprinting. Sprinting is exciting. Marching 20 miles every day for ten years is a grind. Most leaders can't resist the allure of the big push.

**Empirical Creativity — fire bullets, then cannonballs.** Innovation is great. But the 10Xers didn't innovate wildly; they innovated carefully. They fired low-cost, low-risk "bullets" — experiments, pilots, small bets — to figure out what worked. Then, when they found the target, they fired a "cannonball" — a big, focused investment. The comparison companies either fired cannonballs without testing (risking huge failures) or never fired cannonballs at all (staying small). The innovation culture tells you to "go big or go home." Collins says: go small first. Test your assumptions with cheap experiments. Only once you've confirmed the pattern do you commit. This is the opposite of the Silicon Valley "move fast and break things" ethos.

**Level 5 Ambition — the product paradox.** Collins's earlier work introduced "Level 5 Leadership" — leaders who combine intense professional will with personal humility. In *Great by Choice*, this appears as **Level 5 Ambition**, applied specifically to product companies. The 10Xers didn't obsess over competitors. They obsessively focused on their product and their customers. They weren't trying to beat the market; they were trying to master their craft. The paradox: the companies that cared least about beating the competition were the ones that did it most consistently. Competitor obsession is a form of laziness. It's easier to watch what others do and react than to build deep understanding of your own customer. The 10Xers did the hard work of knowing their own business better than anyone else.

**SMaC — Specific, Methodical, and Consistent.** The 10Xers operated by a set of durable, explicit operating principles — Collins calls them SMaC recipes. These aren't values or mission statements. They're concrete rules: "We will never have a net cash position below 20% of revenue" or "We will only enter markets where we can be #1 or #2." These rules don't change with the market cycle; they change only when the evidence is overwhelming. The comparison companies had implicit or flexible principles that shifted with every new management book or market downturn. Collins found that the SMaC recipes of 10Xers were remarkably stable — most changed fewer than 20% of their rules over decades, while comparison companies were constantly overhauling theirs. Flexibility sounds smart. But in a crisis, principles you can't remember are useless. The SMaC recipe must be simple enough that every employee can repeat it.

**Return on Luck — the luck factor.** Both the 10Xers and the comparison companies had good and bad luck. The difference was what they did with it. The 10Xers were better at leveraging good luck — they recognized unexpected opportunities and acted on them decisively. They were also better at surviving bad luck — they had cash reserves, conservative debt, and a culture of discipline that prevented panic. Collins calls this **Return on Luck** — it's not about being luckier, but about getting more value from the luck you have. We envy successful people and attribute it to luck. The data says luck is distributed evenly. The skill is in extraction.

The idea I couldn't shake after reading *Great by Choice* is the **20 Mile March**. I've seen so many organizations that sprint in Q4 to save the year, crash in Q1, and live on an emotional roller coaster. The idea of setting a pace you can sustain through any conditions — and demanding that discipline of yourself — is spiritually and practically powerful. The second idea is **fire bullets, then cannonballs**. I've watched companies bet the farm on unvalidated hunches, and I've watched companies never bet at all and atrophy. The middle path — cheap experiments first — is so obvious after you read it and so rare in practice.

*Great by Choice* is the least methodologically ambitious of Collins's major books — the sample size is small, the "10X" threshold feels arbitrary, and some of the companies studied (Amgen, Intel) are enormous outliers that shared exceptional market tailwinds. The book also suffers from a problem that afflicts all of Collins's work: it identifies correlating factors but can't prove causation. The writing is tighter than *Built to Last* but still carries the Collins trademark of meticulously over-explaining every point. And the "luck" chapter, while insightful, reads like a concession that the 20 Mile March and empirical creativity aren't enough to guarantee success.

Leaders operating in volatile industries — tech, energy, finance, media. Also great for anyone building a business in an emerging market or going through a period of upheaval. If your environment is stable and predictable, the book's insights will feel like overkill. Also skip if you're impatient with Collins's long-form case study style. And if you haven't read *Good to Great*, start there — this is a sequel that builds on the earlier framework.

1. Define your 20 Mile March: pick one key metric (revenue, users, profit) and set a specific low-end and high-end target that you commit to hitting every quarter for the next two years. Write it down. Share it with your team.
2. Identify one big bet you're currently considering. List three cheap "bullets" (small experiments) you can run in the next 30 days to test the core assumption behind it.
3. Write your SMaC recipe — three to five specific operating principles you will not change, regardless of market conditions. "We will never ___" or "We will always ___." Post them where the team can see them.
4. Audit last year's luck. For each piece of good luck, ask: "Did we get maximum value from it?" For each piece of bad luck, ask: "Did our response make things better or worse?" Write down one change to improve your Return on Luck.
