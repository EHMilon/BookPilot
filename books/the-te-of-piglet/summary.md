The smallest character in the Hundred Acre Wood may be the most powerful one you've never taken seriously. Piglet — tiny, anxious, squeaky-voiced Piglet — does something in the Winnie-the-Pooh stories that most of us can't do in real life: he faces what terrifies him and acts anyway. Benjamin Hoff noticed this and wrote an entire book around it, because he realized Piglet isn't just a children's character. He's a living embodiment of a deep Taoist principle called **Te** — the power of the small.

Hoff's first book, *The Tao of Pooh*, introduced millions of readers to Taoism through the lovable, simple-minded Pooh Bear. This companion volume centers on the overlooked one — the character everyone dismisses until he proves them wrong. And through Piglet, Hoff makes an argument that cuts against everything our culture tells us about success: your smallness isn't a weakness. It's the whole point.

**Te** is usually translated as "virtue" or "power," but neither word captures it. It's the unique quality that arises from being fully who you are — not trying to be bigger or stronger or louder, just being exactly your size and acting from that place. In Taoist thought, the *Tao Te Ching* (the central Taoist text) is literally the "Book of the Way and Its Virtue/Power." Pooh represents the *Tao* — the Way, the effortless flow of life. Piglet represents the *Te* — the virtue that comes from living in harmony with that flow.

Hoff unpacks this through a forgotten Taoist concept called **Tz'u** (pronounced "tsu"): the virtue of compassion, tenderness, and care. In the Taoist tradition, Tz'u is the first of the Three Treasures — the qualities that sustain a wise life. "I have three treasures," Lao Tzu wrote. "The first is compassion." And no one in the Hundred Acre Wood embodies compassion like Piglet. He checks on his friends. He worries about Eeyore's birthday. He's the one who shows up, even when he's scared.

> "There's one thing about being small: No matter what happens, you can't get any smaller."

One of the most provocative claims Hoff makes is that the Western obsession with size — bigger houses, bigger resumes, bigger impact — is actually a form of spiritual poverty. He quotes Lao Tzu: "The heavy is the root of the light. The still is the master of the restless." Piglet's power is not despite his smallness. It's because of it. The small can see what the large misses. The quiet can hear what the loud drowns out. The humble can receive what the proud blocks.

> "The wise person does not go about showing off his wisdom. The small does not complain about his smallness. The doer does not boast of his deeds."

Hoff weaves the Taoist concept of **P'u** (the Uncarved Block) — the original, simple nature of things before society complicates them — into Piglet's character. Piglet hasn't been "carved" by ambition or ego. He's still whole. Most of us have been shaped, polished, and diminished by the demands of the world. Piglet reminds us what we looked like before.

> "Piglet is not trying to be anything. He just is. And that is precisely his power."

**The Trap here** is the Cult of Bigness. We're trained from childhood to want more — more influence, more followers, more accomplishments. We celebrate the loudest voices, the biggest gestures, the most extravagant wins. Piglet says no. The power worth having doesn't come from getting bigger. It comes from becoming more fully yourself at the size you already are. The trap is thinking that growth always means expansion when sometimes it means deepening.

Hoff uses the other Pooh characters as cautionary examples of what happens when Te is blocked. **Tigger** represents uncontrolled, undirected energy — bouncing through life without purpose or compassion. He's all Yang, no Yin. **Eeyore** is the opposite: stuck in passive despair, unable to find the light. He's given up on Te entirely. **Rabbit** is clever, organized, and constantly frustrated — he thinks he knows the right way and can't understand why others don't follow. **Owl** is full of knowledge but empty of wisdom — he can recite facts and spell things out, but he misses what's actually happening. Each of them has a form of power that gets in the way of real power.

> "Rabbit knows a lot, and is very clever, but nobody listens to him. Owl knows a lot, and is very clever, but nobody listens to him either."

The cutting observation: these characters are us. We're all some degree of Tigger (chaotic energy), Eeyore (learned hopelessness), Rabbit (controlling frustration), or Owl (performative knowledge). The Taoist path isn't about becoming a Pooh — it's about recognizing which character you're playing and loosening its hold on you.

One of the most moving sections in the book retells the story of Piglet and Pooh trapped in a flood. Pooh recites a poem to stay calm. Piglet, waist-deep in water, tries to be brave. Eventually they're rescued by Christopher Robin, who calls them both "brave." But the book notes: Pooh's bravery is native — he doesn't know enough to be scared. Piglet's bravery is conscious — he knows exactly how small and vulnerable he is, and he acts anyway. That's the deeper courage. That's Te in action.

Hoff also introduces the concept of **Wu Wei** — the Taoist art of effortless action — and shows how Piglet practices it not by being passive, but by being completely present. When Piglet helps Eeyore find his tail, he doesn't analyze or strategize. He just shows up. That's Wu Wei: not laziness, but action without the clutter of overthinking.

> "The wise person is not searching for anything. He is doing what comes naturally. But he does what comes naturally to him as a wise person, which is not necessarily what comes naturally to someone else."

**The Trap here** is mistaking Wu Wei for passivity. Some readers hear "effortless action" and assume Taoism is about sitting around. But Piglet doesn't sit around. He acts — constantly, courageously. The effortlessness isn't about doing less. It's about doing without the internal drama. You still move. You just stop wrestling with yourself while you do it.

The book also confronts an uncomfortable truth: modern society is structurally anti-Taoist. Our education system rewards Owl (show what you know). Our economy rewards Rabbit (optimize, organize, achieve). Our entertainment rewards Tigger (loud, fast, extreme). And our media laughs at Piglet. The small, quiet, compassionate characters don't get headlines. But Hoff argues they're the ones who hold the world together.

> "The Taoists say that the world goes round not because of the people who do a lot of things, but because of the people who do the right things."

Let me be honest about the criticism. *The Te of Piglet* is less tightly constructed than its predecessor. *The Tao of Pooh* flowed effortlessly from idea to example. This one sometimes feels like Hoff is trying too hard — the connections between Piglet and Taoism can stretch thin, and the book becomes repetitive in its middle third. Hoff's tendency to lecture about modern society's failings sometimes veers into grumpy-old-man territory, and readers who aren't already sympathetic to Taoist thought may find the framing precious.

The book also explores **Tz'u** (compassion) as a counterweight to the aggression of modern life. Hoff points out that in Chinese philosophy, the Three Treasures are compassion, moderation, and humility — not wealth, power, and fame. Every civilization that has prioritized the latter over the former has crumbled. Piglet's compassion isn't weakness. It's the foundation of a sustainable life.

> "The first of the Three Treasures, Tz'u, is Love. Not romance, not family feeling, but the love that comes from understanding the unity of all things."

There's also a harder question: can the "power of the small" actually work in a world that punishes gentleness? Piglet's approach is beautiful in the Hundred Acre Wood, but if you're a woman in a corporate environment, or a minority in an unjust system, being small and compassionate may not save you. Hoff doesn't fully address this. The book's worldview assumes a fundamental benevolence in the universe that not everyone experiences.

Anyone who feels too small for the world they're in — the quiet ones, the overlooked ones, the people who've been told they need to be louder and bigger. Also, anyone who loved *The Tao of Pooh* and wants more depth.

Readers looking for a practical, step-by-step philosophy. This is poetic and impressionistic — you have to meet it halfway.

The idea that courage isn't the absence of fear but fear acting anyway — and that the most powerful thing you can do is be exactly who you are, at whatever size that happens to be.

1. **Identify your blocking archetype.** Which character do you default to — Tigger (scattered energy), Eeyore (hopelessness), Rabbit (control), or Owl (performance)? Write it down. For one day, notice when you slip into that pattern. Just notice. No judgment.

2. **Pick one act of small kindness.** Do something for someone else without telling anyone. Make it anonymous if possible. Notice how it feels. That feeling is Tz'u in practice — the Taoist treasure of compassion.

3. **Name one place where you're trying to be bigger.** Maybe it's your job, your social media presence, your friend group. Ask: "What would it look like to be fully myself at the size I am right now, instead of trying to grow?" Sit with the discomfort of that question.
