Sheryl Sandberg was on vacation with her husband, Dave Goldberg, when he collapsed in a gym and died. He was forty-seven. They'd been married eleven years. They had two children. And then, in a moment that every married person has imagined in the abstract and no one is prepared for, he was gone.

*Option B* begins in that moment, and it doesn't flinch. Sandberg writes about the raw weeks after — the "void" she calls it, a word that feels deliberate in its flatness because there's no poetry for that kind of grief. But the book isn't about grief. It's about what comes after grief, when you have to decide whether to let it define you or let it shape you.

The title comes from a concept she shares early: after Dave died, a friend told her that Option A (having Dave alive) was gone, so she needed to "kick the shit out of Option B." That line — blunt, profane, practical — is the book's operating philosophy. Resilience isn't about pretending the loss didn't happen. It's about making the best of the life you have left, not despite the loss, but with full acknowledgment of it.

Sandberg co-wrote the book with Adam Grant, the Wharton psychologist who wrote *Give and Take* and *Originals*, and the two voices weave together in a way that works. Sandberg brings the raw material — the experience of loss, the moments of collapse, the small victories. Grant brings the research — the studies on post-traumatic growth, the psychological frameworks for resilience, the data on what actually helps.

The book's central framework revolves around the **Three P's** that determine how people respond to adversity, drawn from Martin Seligman's work: *Personalization, Pervasiveness,* and *Permanence*. Personalization is the belief that you are at fault. Pervasiveness is the belief that the event will affect every area of your life. Permanence is the belief that the aftermath will last forever.

Sandberg caught herself engaging in all three. She personalized Dave's death — if only she'd noticed his symptoms sooner. She let the grief become pervasive — she couldn't be a good mother, a good leader, a good friend. And she believed the pain would be permanent — that she would never feel joy again. The research Grant brings shows that these three cognitive distortions are the difference between people who get stuck in grief and people who eventually grow through it.

**The Trap with the Three P's:** The danger of frameworks like this is that they can sound like blame — as if people who struggle with grief are just not thinking correctly. Sandberg is careful to avoid this. She makes clear that these patterns are normal, automatic, and deeply human. The goal isn't to never feel them; it's to catch them when they happen and gently correct course.

There's a powerful chapter on **building resilient communities.** Sandberg describes how the people around her, despite good intentions, often made things worse. The platitudes — "He's in a better place," "Everything happens for a reason" — landed like small cruelties. But genuine support came from people who showed up without scripts: the friend who just sat with her while she cried, the colleague who said "I don't know what to say, but I'm here." She and Grant explore the concept of *speaking the unspeakable* — acknowledging pain directly instead of trying to paper it over.

One of the most moving sections involves her children. Sandberg describes trying to navigate her own grief while helping her son and daughter navigate theirs. She and Grant introduce the concept of **the "recovery narrative"** — the idea that children need to know, not just that bad things happen, but that people survive them. She starts saying the names of people who lost parents and went on to live full lives. She normalizes the conversation about death rather than making it taboo. "We tell our children to look for the helpers," she writes. "We need to teach them to be the helpers too."

The book also addresses **post-traumatic growth** — the counterintuitive finding that many people emerge from trauma with *greater* appreciation for life, deeper relationships, renewed purpose. Grant's research shows that roughly half of people who experience a traumatic event report at least one area of positive growth afterward. This isn't toxic positivity — no one is saying the trauma was a gift. But the growth is real, and the book documents it without glossing over the cost.

There's a memorable story about the day Sandberg tried to get back to work. She was terrified of failing, of being a diminished leader, of letting everyone down. She walked into a meeting, sat down, and realized she'd forgotten how to contribute. She describes the feeling as being underwater — she could see people's mouths moving but couldn't process the words. Grant explains that grief literally impairs cognitive function: the brain is processing loss at a level that leaves fewer resources for complex tasks. The insight isn't that you can power through; it's that you need to give yourself grace for the period when you can't.

**Honest Criticism:** Sandberg's privilege is the elephant in every room of this book. She had financial security, a supportive network, access to world-class psychological research, and enough career capital to take months off. The book acknowledges this but doesn't dwell on it, and the resilience advice sometimes reads like it was written for someone with a safety net that most people don't have. The chapter on returning to work — while valuable — assumes you have a job that's flexible enough to accommodate grief.

The book also leans heavily on Sandberg's personal experience, and while that's the source of its emotional power, it also narrows the lens. Grief from a spouse's sudden death is one kind of loss. The book is less useful for people dealing with chronic illness, financial collapse, systemic injustice, or the slow erosion of hope rather than a single catastrophic event.

**What Will Actually Stick With Me:** The line "Option A is not available, so let's kick the shit out of Option B" lives in my head permanently. But deeper than that, the idea that resilience isn't about bouncing *back* — returning to who you were before — but bouncing *forward* into someone different. The book made me rethink what I offer to grieving friends: not solutions, not perspective, not silver linings. Just presence. "We cannot know exactly how someone else is feeling," Sandberg writes. "But we can try."
