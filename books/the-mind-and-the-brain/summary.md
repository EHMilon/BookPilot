Jeffrey Schwartz is a UCLA neuropsychiatrist who became famous for one thing: proving that the human mind can change the physical structure of the brain through nothing more than directed, focused attention. Not as a metaphor. As a biological fact measurable with PET scans.

*The Mind and the Brain*, co-written with science journalist Sharon Begley, is the most philosophically ambitious book in the neuroplasticity canon. Doidge showed you *that* brains change. Schwartz shows you *how* attention drives that change — and then makes an argument so unfashionable that many of his peers still won't touch it: that the mind is not identical to the brain. That the mind can direct the brain's activity. That you have a ghost in the machine, and the ghost has agency.

If you're a materialist who believes consciousness is just an epiphenomenon of neural firing, this book is going to bother you. That's kind of the point.

Schwartz developed his framework through his work with OCD patients. He noticed something strange in brain scans: people with OCD had hyperactivity in a part of the brain called the **orbitofrontal cortex** (OFC). The circuit kept firing, kept sounding the alarm, even when there was no actual threat. The compulsion wasn't a character flaw. It was a brain lock — a stuck gear in the neural transmission.

Standard treatment was medication (SSRIs) or exposure therapy — both of which work by chemically or behaviourally quieting the OFC. Schwartz developed a different approach: what he called **The Four Steps** — Relabel, Reattribute, Refocus, Revalue. Instead of fighting the obsessional thought, patients learned to recognise it as an error signal from a malfunctioning circuit (Relabel), to understand it's caused by a brain glitch not a real threat (Reattribute), to consciously shift focus to an alternative behaviour (Refocus), and to devalue the obsessional thought over time (Revalue).

Here's where it gets neuroplastic: patients who practised these steps for weeks showed measurable changes in their PET scans. The OFC activity went down. The **caudate nucleus**, which normally helps shift gears between thoughts, lit up more. The brain had physically changed because the patients had chosen to think differently.

> "The mind can change the brain. This is not a metaphorical statement. It is a literal, scientifically established fact."

This challenges the dominant neuroscientific paradigm so directly that it's still controversial. The standard view — call it **neural determinism** — holds that all mental states are caused by physical brain states, period. Thinking is a byproduct of the brain's machinery. Schwartz says the data points the other way: the patient's act of *choosing* to relabel an obsessional thought, an act of the mind, caused a measurable change in the brain's physical structure. Mind → brain, not just brain → mind.

To make this case, Schwartz dedicates a substantial portion of the book to a history of the neuroplasticity revolution — from William James's early musings about plasticity in 1890, through the long dark age of the "localisationist" dogma, to the pioneering monkey experiments of Merzenich and Jenkins. It's a necessary foundation, but it's also the book's slowest section. If you've already read Doidge, you'll find yourself skimming.

The core idea is what Schwartz calls **self-directed neuroplasticity**: the deliberate, mindful use of attention to reshape neural circuits. This is distinct from passive neuroplasticity — changes that happen because of injury or external stimulation. Self-directed neuroplasticity requires a conscious agent making choices. It requires the mind.

> "The mind is what the brain does — and what the brain does is what the mind tells it to do."

Schwartz introduces the concept of **the will** — a term modern neuroscience has been allergic to since Freud. He argues that will is not an illusion but a function of the **prefrontal cortex**, particularly the **anterior cingulate cortex**, which acts as a kind of neural spotlight, directing attention to one thing over another. Every time you choose to attend to something — consciously, deliberately — you are exercising your will, and that exercise changes the brain.

The book leans heavily on the work of the philosopher of mind **David Chalmers** and the concept of the **hard problem of consciousness** — how physical processes give rise to subjective experience. Schwartz doesn't solve the hard problem, but he makes a narrower, more defensible claim: that subjective experience (mind) and neural activity (brain) are causally linked in both directions. The evidence from his OCD patients is the best empirical support for this view that neuroscience has produced.

**The Trap:** It's tempting to read this as "I can think my way out of any problem." That's not what Schwartz says. The OCD patients still needed medication in many cases. The neural circuits that drive deep depression, trauma, or addiction are not easily overwritten by a good attitude. Self-directed neuroplasticity is real, but it's slow, effortful work. Schwartz calls it **mindful attention** — not passive awareness, but active, repeated, effortful redirection of focus. Think of it less like flipping a switch and more like carving a river channel one stone at a time.

Schwartz also explores how his approach applies beyond OCD. He reports success with Tourette's syndrome (patients learning to relabel tics), anxiety disorders, and even some forms of depression. The principle is the same in every case: identify the pathological neural pattern, recognise it as a false alarm, and repeatedly redirect attention to a healthier alternative. The repetition changes the brain.

> "It is the repeated, focused effort that is essential. You cannot simply decide to change your brain. You must do the work."

The book's weakness: Schwartz's philosophical ambition sometimes outpaces his evidence. The argument for mind-brain duality — that the mind is a "relatively independent entity" from the brain — goes further than most neuroscientists would accept. The PET scan data shows correlation, not causation. Critics argue that Schwartz's patients may simply have been learning cognitive techniques that indirectly changed their brain activity through well-understood feedback loops, with no need for a "dualistic" explanation. Schwartz dismisses this too quickly.

Schwartz and Begley also explore how the Four Steps apply beyond OCD to everyday patterns. The authors describe a phenomenon called **brain lock in daily life** — moments when you're stuck in a mental loop that isn't serving you. It could be rumination over a past conversation, obsessive checking of email, or compulsive scrolling through social media. In each case, the mechanism is the same: a neural circuit has become stuck in a repetitive firing pattern, and the orbitofrontal cortex is signalling "this is important" even when the loop is maladaptive. The Four Steps offer a way out: recognise the loop as a neural glitch, attribute it to the brain's wiring rather than a real problem, consciously shift attention elsewhere, and — gradually — devalue the loop so it loses its grip on your attention.

There's also a fascinating section on **the neuroscience of attention** — how the brain chooses what to focus on in a world overflowing with sensory input. Schwartz argues that modern digital life is a form of attention-training in the wrong direction. Every notification, every tab opened, every interruption strengthens the neural circuits for distraction and fragmentation. The brain becomes better at skimming and worse at focusing. The implication: your daily choices about where to direct your attention are not trivial. They're shaping your brain's attentional architecture. If you spend your day in a state of fragmented attention, you are literally building a brain that cannot focus. If you protect your attention — single-tasking, deep work, mindfulness — you build a brain that can.

And there's a practical limitation: the Four Steps work brilliantly for OCD and anxiety — conditions characterised by intrusive thoughts that can be recognised and reframed. They're less obviously applicable to conditions where the patient can't identify a specific cognitive distortion (some forms of depression, for instance) or where the damage is structural (stroke, TBI).

For what it is, though — a rigorous, evidence-informed argument that your attention is not passive, that your mind can direct your brain's rewiring, and that this has real therapeutic power — it's one of the most important books in the field.
