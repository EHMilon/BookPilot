When I arrived in South Korea, the first written language I learned wasn't Korean. It was the silent language of the subway. Older people stand with their hands pressed together, eyes closed, even in rush hour. Young couples share one pair of earbuds. No one eats. No one talks on phones. Everyone watches the doors.

The book Learning to Live in South Korea begins here — in the observation that living in Korea means learning a whole system of social physics that no one explains to you because it was never written down. This isn't a phrasebook guide. It's a decoder ring for the unwritten rules that govern Korean life, written for the foreigner who's past the "wow, look at all the neon" phase and is starting to realize they have no idea what's actually going on.

The book spends its first major section on *nunchi* — the Korean superpower of reading the room. If you're living in Korea and you don't have nunchi, you're walking around without peripheral vision.

The core rule: never say or do the first thing that comes to your mind. Pause. Observe. Check the social temperature. Is the most senior person in the room speaking? Is the mood tense or relaxed? Should you pour drinks for others before pouring for yourself? Nunchi is the split-second assessment that tells you.

> "Nunchi isn't mind-reading. It's listening with your eyes."

The book breaks down how foreigners most often fail at nunchi: speaking too bluntly, refusing offers of food or drink without the proper ritual of refusal, not knowing the hierarchy of who sits where at a dinner table (the most senior person sits facing the door, furthest from it), and addressing someone by their name instead of their title.

Over-nunchi. Some foreigners learn nunchi so well they become paralyzed, constantly second-guessing whether their next move is appropriate. The book's advice: you will make mistakes. That's fine. Koreans don't expect you to have perfect nunchi — they expect you to *try*. The effort itself communicates respect.

*Hoesik* — company dinner — is not optional. If you work in Korea, you will drink with your colleagues. The book provides a survival guide:

The golden rule: when drinking with someone older or more senior, turn your head away from them when you drink. Hold your glass with two hands when they pour for you. Pour for them first. Never fill your own glass. Never refuse the first drink offered — one refusal is polite, two is rude.

> "In Korea, drinking isn't about the alcohol. It's about the vertical line. Every pour, every turn of the head, every hand on the glass is a sentence in a conversation about respect."

Foreigners often mistake Korean drinking culture for pure hedonism or toxic masculinity. It's not (entirely). The drinking is a social lubricant for things that can't be said sober — critiques of the boss that need to be aired, apologies that need to be made without losing face. The *soju* is the excuse.

Korean hierarchies look rigid from the outside. *Oppa*, *noona*, *hyung*, *unnie* — these aren't just labels. They determine how you speak, who pays, who pours, who enters a door first, how you end an email. The book helps you map the hierarchy of your specific context: by birth year, by job title, by university, by length of relationship.

The liberating insight: once you accept the hierarchy, you can move within it freely. The rigidity is on the surface. Underneath, relationships in Korea are warm, generous, and surprisingly informal once the formalities are established.

> "You don't escape the hierarchy by ignoring it. You escape it by honoring it — and once you have, there's no one more direct and warm than a Korean friend."

Some foreigners rebel against the hierarchy, assuming it's patronizing or oppressive. The book argues this is a category error. Korean hierarchy is not about power — it's about *responsibility*. The senior person is obligated to take care of the junior person. The junior person's obligation is respect, not submission. If you experience the hierarchy as oppression, you're likely not in a healthy workplace.

The book covers the practical hell of Korean life without romanticizing it: finding housing (you'll need a *key money* deposit of tens of thousands of dollars), navigating the healthcare system (excellent but confusing), the garbage disposal system (you will be yelled at by an ajumma for putting food waste in the wrong bag), the mobile phone registration (you need an ARC, which you can get with your visa, which you need to get your bank account, which you need for your phone — a famous Korean catch-22).

But it also covers what makes Korean daily life beautiful: the 24-hour convenience stores that are actual community hubs, the *jjimjil-bang* where you can sleep for twelve dollars, the mountain hiking trails accessible from every neighborhood, the *bingsu* cafes that appear in summer with shaved ice mountains taller than your head.

A surprising chapter covers Korean apartment living. Unlike Western apartment buildings where neighbors are strangers, Korean apartments — especially older ones — function as mini-communities. There are *ban* (neighborhood watch groups), *gyemoim* (informal savings clubs that also serve as social networks), and the famous *ajumma network* — middle-aged women who know everything about everyone, who will tell you if your child is misbehaving, who will bring you soup when you move in.

The apartment complex in Korea has its own social physics: the *jip-jib* (collective move-in day when the whole building welcomes new residents), the *dok-seo-sil* (communal study rooms where students do homework under the watch of rotating parents), and the unspoken rules about noise, garbage, and shoe placement.

> "In a Korean apartment, privacy isn't the absence of neighbors. It's their respectful presence."

Not everyone wants this level of community. The book acknowledges that the Korean apartment social structure can feel suffocating to Westerners who value privacy. The advice: set boundaries early, politely. Koreans understand needing space — they just need you to express it through the proper channels.

The book assumes a foreigner who is male, working in a corporate job, and based in Seoul. The female foreigner experience — navigating different social expectations, safety considerations, and workplace dynamics — gets a single chapter when it deserves more. The rural living section is an afterthought. The book was clearly written by someone based in Seoul for someone moving to Seoul. The perspective on hierarchy is also somewhat forgiving; it underplays how oppressive Korean workplace hierarchy can be, especially in traditional companies, for young workers who face genuine exploitation under the pretense of "family."

Living in Korea means living through a calendar that has an opinion about your life. Chuseok (harvest festival) demands you travel to your hometown, perform ancestor rites, and eat songpyeon with family. Lunar New Year requires *sebae* (deep bows to elders) and money in silk bags for children. Buddha's Birthday means lantern festivals that turn entire city neighborhoods into rivers of light.

The book's advice: don't fight the calendar. Korean holidays are not optional for social participation. If you're living in Korea, you will be expected to participate, or at least to know what's happening and why. The foreigner who doesn't know when Chuseok is will find themselves alone in a deserted city with closed restaurants and no explanation.

> "In Korea, the calendar has a voice. Listen to it, and you'll always know what to do. Ignore it, and you'll always be confused."

Each holiday comes with specific foods, specific obligations, and specific emotional tones. Chuseok is anxious (traffic, family expectations, the pressure of ancestral rites). Lunar New Year is joyful but formal. Buddha's Birthday is serene and beautiful. Learning the emotional rhythm of the Korean year is as important as learning the language.

Anyone moving to Korea for work or study. Anyone who's been in Korea for six months and is starting to realize how much they don't understand.

Short-term tourists. If you're here for two weeks, you don't need to understand hoesik etiquette. Just eat the food and take the photos.

1. Visit a Korean convenience store (GS25, CU, 7-Eleven). Buy a banana milk and a triangle kimbap. Stand at the counter and eat it. Watch how Koreans eat standing up — fast, efficient, heads down, no dawdling. That's a Korean eating habit.
2. Learn the age hierarchy: when you meet a new Korean, ask their birth year (or look it up on a shared timeline). Practice using the correct honorific (*hyung*, *noona*, *oppa*, *unnie*, *seonbae*) at least once in natural conversation.
3. Go to a *jjimjil-bang*. Spend two hours cycling through hot rooms, cold rooms, the salt room, the sleeping hall. Notice how the social tension dissolves — age hierarchy doesn't exist in a bathhouse.
4. Practice the drinking pour: pour water or tea for someone else at the table, using two hands. When someone pours for you, hold your glass with two hands. Small gesture. Huge signal.
