Benjamin Hoff

The most dangerous thing about **The Tao of Pooh** is how easy it is to dismiss. A book that explains Daoism using Winnie the Pooh? It sounds like a gimmick, a cute gift book for graduation. And it is — until it isn't. Benjamin Hoff's slim masterpiece smuggles one of the most sophisticated philosophical traditions in human history into a format so accessible that millions of people have absorbed Daoist principles without ever knowing they were being taught philosophy. The book works because Pooh *is* the Taoist sage, and Hoff had the audacity to point it out.

Hoff was an American writer who worked for a time as a children's book editor. He was familiar with A. A. Milne's *Winnie the Pooh* from his own childhood, but he rediscovered it as an adult and was struck by something: Pooh embodies the Daoist principle of *wu wei* (effortless action) more perfectly than any serious philosophical treatise could. "The book came to me," Hoff said in interviews — he woke up one morning knowing he had to write it. The result is a book that introduces Daoism through characters we already love: Pooh (the uncarved block, the sage who doesn't try), Piglet (the small but loyal companion who learns courage), Eeyore (the pessimist trapped in his own complaints), Rabbit (the hyper-organized busybody who mistakes activity for accomplishment), Owl (the intellectual who mistakes knowledge for wisdom), and Tigger (the excessive yang energy that needs balancing).

The obvious trap is assuming this is a shallow book for shallow people. It's not. Hoff knows his Daoist sources deeply — he quotes Laozi, Zhuangzi, and the *I Ching* throughout, and the quotations are well-chosen and contextually accurate. The book's genius is that it *performs* what it teaches. It doesn't lecture you about simplicity — it's *simple*. It doesn't argue for spontaneity — it's *spontaneous*. The trap is thinking a book that's easy to read must be easy to understand. Pooh's way — the way of no-trying — is one of the hardest things a human being can actually practice.

Hoff organizes the book around the *Pu* principle — the "Uncarved Block." In Daoism, the uncarved block represents the original, unspoiled nature of things — the state before civilization, education, and anxiety carved us into shapes that don't fit. Pooh *is* the Uncarved Block. He doesn't try to be anything other than what he is. He's not the smartest, not the strongest, not the most organized, not the most knowledgeable. But he's the one who's *present*. When Christopher Robin needs help, Pooh shows up. When there's a problem, Pooh doesn't analyze it — he wanders toward it with open attention and usually blunders into the solution. Hoff's argument is that this "blundering" is actually the highest form of intelligence: the intelligence of not-knowing, the wisdom of trusting the process instead of trying to control it.

Pooh can seem like an advertisement for laziness. He's a bear of very little brain who sits around eating honey and humming to himself. The trap is thinking *wu wei* means doing nothing. It doesn't. Pooh acts constantly — he goes on expeditions, he rescues friends, he builds houses for Eeyore. The difference is that his action is *responsive*, not *driven*. He doesn't have an agenda that makes him force things. When he acts, it's because the situation calls for it, not because he's trying to prove something or achieve a pre-planned goal. The modern reader, steeped in productivity culture, reads "effortless action" and thinks: "Great, I can stop trying." But *not trying* is different from *trying to not try*, which is what most of us would do. The true uncarved block doesn't know it's an uncarved block.

The book's treatment of the other characters is its most effective teaching device. Rabbit represents the Confucian impulse: he organizes, he plans, he schedules, he gets things done. But Rabbit is also perpetually stressed, perpetually frustrated, perpetually trying to control things that can't be controlled. Owl represents intellectual knowledge: he can spell "Tuesday" (even if he spells it wrong), he knows facts, he gives long speeches. But Owl is useless in a crisis and misses what's right in front of him. Eeyore represents the pessimist's trap: he's so convinced the world is disappointing that he actively works to find disappointment, even when things are going well. Tigger represents excessive yang energy — bouncing, bouncing, always bouncing, never settling, never resting. Only Pooh, the Uncarved Block, is at peace.

The chapter on "The Pooh Way" of problem-solving is worth the price of the book alone. Hoff recounts the story from Milne where Pooh and Piglet are tracking the Heffalump. They walk in a circle, following their own footprints, getting more and more excited as the "tracks" multiply. Pooh eventually realizes: "I have been Foolish and Deluded... I am a Bear of No Brain at All." He sits down and stops. The problem disappears — not because he solved it, but because he stopped making it worse. This is the Daoist principle of *wei wu wei*: action through non-action. Sometimes the most intelligent response to a problem is to see that you are the one creating it.

Hoff also addresses the question of suffering, which is where the book reveals its depth. He doesn't pretend that Daoism eliminates suffering. But he shows, through Eeyore, that much of our suffering is *manufactured*. Eeyore's tail is pinned on wrong, his house falls down, his birthday presents are useful things like a burst balloon and a pot of honey (which Pooh eats before giving it to him). But Eeyore's real problem isn't his circumstances — it's his interpretation of them. He finds gloom in every situation because he's looking for it. The Daoist alternative isn't toxic positivity (pretending everything is fine) but *equanimity* (not needing everything to be fine in order to be fine yourself).

The idea I couldn't shake is that simplicity is not a lack of intelligence — it's a *kind* of intelligence that modern culture actively suppresses. We're trained to complicate things, to add more, to optimize, to strategize. The Tao of Pooh suggests that the most advanced spiritual realization might be the one you had as a child before you learned to overthink. I found myself, after reading this book, noticing when I was being Rabbit (frantic, organizing, controlling) and asking: *What would Pooh do?* The answer was always the same: *less*.

The book is too gentle with Western culture. Hoff blames the "scholar" mentality and organized religion for our disconnection from simplicity, but he doesn't address systemic issues — capitalism, inequality, environmental destruction — that make it genuinely difficult to live the simple, uncomplicated life he recommends. There's also a romanticization of childhood here that can feel naive: children are not actually uncarved blocks; they're small people learning to navigate a complex world, and many of them are anxious, competitive, and stressed. And the book occasionally treats Daoism as *merely* "being chill," which is a reduction that would make Laozi grimace.
