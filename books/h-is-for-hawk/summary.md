Let me tell you about the most dangerous thing I've ever wanted. In the months after my father died, I found myself obsessed with a wild animal — a goshawk, the "woods' most murderous killer," a creature that hunts by watching its prey die slowly, that breaks the necks of rabbits and eats the eyes of pheasants while they're still alive. And I thought: if I can train this animal, if I can hold its wildness in my hands and not flinch, then maybe I can survive my own.

That's the opening Helen Macdonald earns in *H is for Hawk*, though she earns it more subtly than my paraphrase suggests. She writes with a poet's compression: every sentence is loaded, every image earned through the kind of attention that only grief can sharpen. Her father, the photographer Alisdair Macdonald, died suddenly of a heart attack on a London street. She was in her thirties. The world stopped making sense. And in that senselessness, she decided to do something she'd wanted to do since she was a child: buy and train a goshawk.

> "Grief is a house of a thousand rooms. I'd moved through all of them, and now I found myself in a room I'd never seen before. It was the shape of a hawk."

*H is for Hawk* is one of those rare books that won both the Costa Book of the Year and the Samuel Johnson Prize for non-fiction, and it deserves both. It's three books woven into one: a memoir of grief, a natural history of the goshawk, and a biography of T.H. White, the troubled author of *The Once and Future King*, who also tried to train a goshawk in the 1930s and wrote *The Goshawk* about his spectacular failure. Macdonald reads White's book as a teenager, then returns to it as a grieving adult, and the parallel story becomes a mirror in which she sees both her own darkness and her way out.

White's story is the dark counterpoint to Macdonald's. In 1937, the author of *The Sword in the Stone* bought a goshawk named Gos and tried to train it using a medieval falconry manual. He locked himself in a cottage in the English countryside and basically tried to break the bird's spirit. He starved it, kept it awake for days, grabbed it in rage when it didn't obey. The bird responded by becoming more terrified and more violent. White's *Goshawk* is a book about a man trying to dominate a wild thing and being broken by the attempt. Macdonald reads it and recognizes herself.

**The Trap** for anyone who trains a hawk — or anyone who grieves — is the temptation to become as wild as the thing you're trying to control. Macdonald describes the early weeks of training Mabel (her goshawk, named after the Latin *mabilis*, "lovable") as a kind of voluntary exile from the human. She stops answering the phone. She stops eating with friends. She lives in a world of raw meat, leather jesses, and the precise calibration of a predator's hunger. She becomes, in her own words, "a hawk" — not metaphorically, but neurologically. The hawk's world is a world of edges, of small movements, of the weight of prey in the fist. Macdonald's grief turns her into a version of herself that can inhabit that world without wanting to leave it.

> "I wanted to be a hawk. I wanted to be something that could kill with its feet, something that could dive from the sky and punch the life out of something and not feel a thing."

> "A hawk is a wild thing. It doesn't love you. It doesn't need you. It chooses you, and that choice is revocable at any moment."

This is the heart of the book — a relationship built on nothing but mutual willingness, moment by moment.

The **training sequences** are the book's most gripping passages. Macdonald describes the ancient falconry technique of "manning" a hawk — the process of making the bird comfortable with human presence by keeping it on your fist for days at a time, never letting it fly, letting it learn that you are not a threat. The goshawk is the most difficult of all hawks to man because it's wired for fight, not flight. It doesn't flee from predators; it attacks them. So Macdonald has to earn Mabel's trust without ever giving Mabel a reason to attack — and Mabel is a creature that can remove her eye with a single strike of its foot. There's a scene where Macdonald falls asleep on the couch with Mabel on her fist, and the bird, slowly, impossibly, closes its eyes too. It's one of the most tender passages in modern nature writing — a moment of trust between species that feels like a minor miracle.

One of Macdonald's most striking insights is that a hawk doesn't love you. A dog loves you. A cat tolerates you with affection. A hawk uses you — you are a mobile perch and a source of food. The bond between a falconer and a hawk is not love; it's *instruments*. The hawk's wildness is not something you can tame; it's something you can temporarily *leverage*. And that's what Macdonald needs — not the unconditional love that everyone offers a grieving person (and which often feels suffocating), but a relationship that is purely functional, purely present, and asks nothing of her except competence.

> "You do not train a hawk. You negotiate with it. You earn its willingness, inch by inch, and one wrong move and it's gone."

**The book's best stories** are the ones about flying Mabel free. Macdonald describes loosing the hawk in the Cambridgeshire countryside and watching it climb, spiraling up into the sky until it's a speck, and then waiting, heart pounding, for it to return. Goshawks are not homing birds like falcons. They can fly away and never come back. Every flight is an act of faith. And when Mabel returns — dropping out of the sky at 70 miles per hour, talons extended, braking at the last second to land on Macdonald's fist — it feels like the bird is choosing her. Which it is. And which, she realizes, is all any of us can really ask of the wild: that it chooses to stay with us for a little while longer.

The **T.H. White sections** are brilliantly intercut. Macdonald sees in White's failed training a version of what she could become: a man who tried to escape his own pain through domination and control, and ended up more isolated than ever. White was a closeted homosexual in a deeply repressive era, a man who had been abused as a child and never recovered. His goshawk book is a record of cruelty disguised as falconry. But Macdonald reads it with compassion, not judgment. She sees what White was trying to do — same as her: turn the wild into a container for the unbearable.

**Criticism:** The White interludes, while fascinating, disrupt the momentum of the main narrative. Some readers find them intrusive — you're in the middle of Macdonald's intensely personal story and suddenly you're back in 1930s England with a depressed author. The book is also dense with falconry terminology (jesses, baffles, creance, mews) that can be disorienting. The prose is so finely wrought that it occasionally feels overwritten — every sentence polished to the point where you notice the polish. And the ending, while emotionally honest, is abrupt. Macdonald emerges from her grief, but the transition from hawk-woman back to functioning human is sketched rather than earned.

**What stuck with me** is the thing I can't unsee: that grief sometimes gives you superpowers. The hypervigilance, the detachment, the ability to focus on a single thing for hours — these are symptoms of trauma, but they're also the exact skills you need to train a hawk. Macdonald's grief made her temporarily incapable of being a normal human. But it made her capable of something else, something ancient and strange. The book's deepest argument is that wildness is not something you visit — it's something you become, when the structures of ordinary life collapse.
