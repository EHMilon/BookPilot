Shunmyo Masuno is a Zen Buddhist priest who also designs rock gardens. That combination — monk and artist, disciplined and deliberate — is the book before you open it. **Zen: The Art of Simple Living** is a hundred short lessons, each barely a page, each organized around one clean principle. You could read it in an afternoon or stretch it across a season. Masuno's argument is that simplicity is not about owning less; it's about perceiving more. Most of us live surrounded by enough — enough time, enough space, enough beauty — but we've trained ourselves to look past it. The book is a series of tiny interventions designed to wake up your attention.

Masuno opens with a problem you might not have named: most of what you do all day is fragmented. You eat while scrolling. You work while worrying about something else. You're with people but thinking about your phone. The Zen tradition calls this a dispersed mind, and Masuno's first move is to call you back into your body with a simple instruction — do one thing at a time. When you wash the dishes, just wash the dishes. When you eat, just eat. When you listen, just listen. The shift sounds trivial but it changes the texture of every hour. He tells a story of a young monk who asked his teacher how to practice Zen. The teacher said: "When you eat, eat. When you sleep, sleep." The monk said: "But everyone does that." The teacher replied: "No. When they eat, they think of a hundred things. When they sleep, they dream of a thousand." The Trap is that you'll treat this as a productivity tip — something to optimize your output. It's not. It's an invitation to taste your own life rather than rush through it.

Masuno makes what sounds like a radical claim: you should stop trying to do everything. Not "prioritize better," not "learn to say no," but actively reduce the amount of work you take on to the point where you can give each piece your full presence. He tells a story about his own father, a tea ceremony master who refused to take on more students than he could truly teach. When Masuno asked why, his father said: "If I multiply myself, I dilute myself." The Trap is the modern assumption that more output equals more value. You've absorbed this so deeply that reducing your commitments feels like failure. Masuno asks you to consider the opposite: that the quality of your attention is the scarce resource, not your time. "When you try to do everything," he writes, "you end up doing nothing well."

You know you should breathe. You know deep breathing calms you. But Masuno isn't giving you a meditation technique — he's pointing at something more fundamental. He describes the Japanese concept of *kokoro*, which loosely means "heart-mind" — the unified seat of thought, feeling, and intention. When you're agitated, your kokoro is scattered. The quickest way to gather it is to focus on your breath. Not as a spiritual practice. As a practical reset. When you pause to breathe consciously, you interrupt the autopilot that runs most of your day. He suggests a tiny ritual: before entering your home, take one breath at the threshold. Before starting a meal, take one breath. Before speaking in a tense conversation, take one breath. These are not meditation sessions. They are punctuation marks that keep you from being swept away by the current of your own habits. "The breath is a bridge," he notes, "between the body and the mind — and most of us have stopped crossing it."

Masuno is a garden designer, and this is where his expertise shines. He describes the Japanese rock garden — a space of deliberate emptiness, where stones and gravel are arranged to create a sense of stillness. The garden doesn't try to entertain you or impress you. It just sits there, being itself, and in its presence, you can do the same. He suggests creating a "garden" in your own life — not necessarily a literal one with plants, but any space devoted to nothing but stillness. A corner of a room. A chair by a window. A bench on your balcony. The rule: this space has no purpose other than to hold you in stillness. No phone, no book, no agenda. He writes about the Zen concept of *mu* — emptiness that is not absence but possibility. The Trap is that you'll think this is a waste of time because nothing "happens" there. That's the point. "The most important things in life are not things at all — they are moments of awareness."

Modern life is hard on ceremony. We've stripped away most of the rituals that used to mark transitions — waking, eating, working, coming home. Masuno argues that without these markers, life blurs into an undifferentiated stream of tasks. His solution: create tiny rituals. Light a candle before you work. Bow to your desk when you sit down. Pour tea with deliberate attention. The content of the ritual matters less than the intention behind it — the act of saying "this moment is different from the one before." He describes the Zen tea ceremony (*chanoyu*) not as an elaborate performance but as a reminder: every encounter is unique and will never happen again. This is the concept of *ichigo ichie* — one meeting, one chance. The Trap is thinking you don't have time for rituals. You don't have time for the scattered, reactive existence that rituals would replace. "Without ceremony," Masuno observes, "life is just one thing after another. With it, life becomes a series of moments worth remembering."

Masuno's most subversive idea is that enlightenment — if you want to call it that — is not a peak experience you achieve after years of meditation. It's the full experience of an ordinary moment. He writes about a famous Zen story: a monk asked his teacher, "What is Zen?" The teacher replied, "Your ordinary mind." The monk pressed: "What is the ordinary mind?" The teacher said: "When tired, sleep. When hungry, eat." There is no secret teaching beyond this. The entire point of Zen practice is to bring you back to the moment you're already in, so fully that it reveals its depth. Masuno extends this to daily life: cooking, cleaning, walking — these are not obstacles to spiritual practice. They are the practice itself. The Trap is that you'll keep looking for a more profound answer. Every time you think "there must be more to it than this," you're missing the point. There isn't more. This is it. "If you cannot find peace in this moment," he writes, "you will not find it in any other."

The book's greatest strength is also its limitation. The hundred-lesson format means no idea gets more than a page or two, which makes the book feel like a collection of aphorisms rather than a sustained argument. Deeper exploration of Zen philosophy — the *Heart Sutra*, the *koan* tradition, the history of Zen in Japan — is entirely absent, and readers looking for intellectual depth will find the book frustratingly shallow. Masuno is writing for the overwhelmed, not the curious. Additionally, the advice can feel culturally specific in ways that don't always translate — the suggestion to bow to your workspace or pour tea with ceremony assumes a relationship to ritual that many Western readers don't have. The author's Zen credentials are real, but he sometimes leans on the authority of tradition rather than making his case through reasoning. Finally, the book can read, at times, like a productivity manual dressed in monk's robes — the command to "do one thing at a time" is indistinguishable from secular focus advice, and the reduction of centuries of Zen to life hacks may irritate purists.

If you are chronically overwhelmed, have tried meditation apps and found them tiresome, and suspect that the answer might be simpler than you're making it — this book will land like a hand on your shoulder. Skip it if you want rigorous philosophical treatment of Zen or if you're allergic to aphorisms.

Pick one threshold you cross daily — your front door, your office, your bedroom. For the next two days, take one conscious breath every time you cross it. No more. Just one. Notice what changes in your transition between spaces.

Eat one meal alone, with no phone, no book, no screen. Just you and the food. Chew slowly. Notice the textures and tastes you usually miss because your attention is elsewhere.

Find one spot in your home or outside. Sit there for one minute doing nothing. No agenda. No phone. Just sitting. Extend to three minutes when you're ready.

Before you start anything new this week, eliminate one recurring commitment that drains you without serving what matters. Reduce your load, then give your full attention to what remains.
