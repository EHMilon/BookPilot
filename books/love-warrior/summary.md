The difference between *Carry On, Warrior* and *Love Warrior* is the difference between surviving a storm and walking into one with your eyes open. The first book was Glennon Doyle trying to keep her life from falling apart. The second is her watching it collapse and deciding, somewhere in the rubble, that maybe that's what needed to happen.

*Love Warrior* was published in 2016, chosen for Oprah's Book Club, and marketed as a story about saving a marriage. Glennon had written about her husband Craig and their struggles before, but this was the full story: the betrayal, the affair, the weeks of not knowing if they'd make it. She wrote it as a testament to the power of commitment and forgiveness. And then — two years later — she left him for Abby Wambach. That timing makes *Love Warrior* a strange artifact. A book about fighting for a marriage, written by a woman who was still, at the time, fighting for a marriage she would eventually release. Did she know, even as she wrote it? Probably not consciously. But reading it now, you can see the seams.

> "When I was a girl, I thought love was something you fell into. Now I know it's something you climb into — and the climbing is the whole story."

**The Trap here** is reading *Love Warrior* as a how-to for saving your marriage. That's how it was marketed. But the book is less a manual and more an excavation. Glennon isn't telling you how to fix your relationship. She's telling you what she found when she dug deep enough into hers: that she had been performing a version of herself for twenty years, and her husband didn't know her because she didn't know herself. The crisis wasn't Craig's affair. The crisis was that she had been absent from her own life.

"I married Craig because he was safe," Glennon writes. "Because he would never leave. But I never asked myself if I would stay." This is the painful honesty that makes *Love Warrior* more than a standard betrayal memoir. She accepts her share of the responsibility — not for his affair, but for the distance between them that made it possible. It's a risk to write this way. Acknowledging your own complicity in a failed relationship can sound like excusing the other person's betrayal. The structure follows the arc of their post-affair recovery. Craig confesses. Glennon spirals. They enter therapy. They scream, cry, and sit in silence. They go on a couples retreat. Slowly, painfully, they start rebuilding. But the real work, as Glennon discovers, isn't about restoring trust in Craig. It's about restoring trust in herself.

**Body Trust** is the book's most original concept. Glennon had spent her life at war with her body — bulimia, binge eating, constant self-criticism. She didn't trust her body to know what it needed, so she controlled it, starved it, and judged it. The affair forced her to realize that she'd been doing the same thing to her whole self. She'd been living from the neck up, cutting off everything below — the instincts, the appetites, the knowing. The affair was a crack in her armor. And for the first time, she let herself feel through the crack.

> "My body had been trying to tell me something for years. I'd been too busy being good to listen."

**The Trap here** is the belief that you can think your way through a relationship crisis. Glennon tried that: she read books, analyzed patterns, assigned blame. It didn't help. What helped was deciding to be present in her own skin — to feel hunger, anger, desire, fear, instead of analyzing them. The trap is staying in your head when the problem is that you've abandoned your body.

Glennon's exploration of **her body** — as a site of both trauma and recovery — is where the book reaches its deepest insights. She describes her bulimia as a response to feeling out of control: "When I couldn't control what was happening around me, I could at least control what went into my body." The affair forced her to realize that control was an illusion. She couldn't control Craig. She couldn't control what people thought. She couldn't even control her own body — it had desires and reactions she didn't authorize.

> "My body had wisdom my brain didn't. I had spent thirty years trying to overpower my body with my mind. And my body had been trying to save me the whole time."

The sections on therapy are raw and specific. Glennon shares the brutal homework their therapist gave them: stay in the room when you want to leave. Say the thing you're afraid to say. Ask the question you don't want the answer to. She and Craig fought in that room, wept in that room, and eventually found something like tenderness there. But the book doesn't pretend they arrived. It ends in a kind of fragile hope — two people who have decided to keep trying.

**Craig's voice** is notable by its absence. He's described, quoted, and contextualized, but he doesn't speak for himself. Glennon is careful to portray him fairly — she doesn't make him a villain — but *Love Warrior* is her story, not theirs. This is both a strength and a limitation. It's honest about the limits of memoir: you can only tell one half of any relationship. But it also means the reader never knows how much of this story is constructed, how much Craig would recognize.

The prose in *Love Warrior* is more crafted than in *Carry On, Warrior*. Glennon has found her rhythm — short sentences, biblical cadence, moments of startling clarity. Some passages read like prose poems. She's stopped trying to be funny or relatable. She's just trying to be true.

> "The only way out is through. There is no shortcut. No bypass. No end run around pain. You have to go straight through the middle."

One of the most uncomfortable sections deals with **sex after betrayal**. Glennon writes about the decision to be physically intimate with Craig after the affair, and the complicated mix of disgust, longing, duty, and hope that accompanied it. She doesn't romanticize the sex or demonize it. She describes the exact, awkward mechanics of trying to rebuild desire when trust is shattered. "We had to learn each other's bodies again — but first we had to learn each other's truths." It's a kind of candor that most marriage memoirs avoid, and it makes the book feel less like a TED Talk and more like a conversation you weren't supposed to overhear.

> "After the affair, I didn't want to touch him. I also didn't want to stop touching him. The contradiction was the truth."

Let me be honest about the criticism. *Love Warrior* exists in a strange limbo now. It was written as a triumph of marital recovery and has become, unintentionally, a document of a marriage's final phase. Oprah called it "a book about the redemption of love." But Glennon later said she was trying to make a square peg fit a round hole, and eventually she accepted that the peg was never square. This doesn't invalidate the book — the struggle was real, even if the outcome was different. But it does date the book in an uncomfortable way. Readers who know the ending may find the hopeful passages hollow.

The book also leans heavily on Christian framing — forgiveness, surrender, grace — that may not resonate outside that tradition. And there's a risk of vicarious trauma: Glennon describes the affair discovery in visceral detail, and readers who have experienced betrayal may find it triggering rather than healing.

Anyone who's been betrayed in a relationship and is trying to decide whether to stay or leave. Also, anyone who's ever felt like they're performing a life instead of living one.

Readers who want a clear answer about whether to save a marriage. The book doesn't provide one. It provides a process, not a conclusion.

The image of Glennon sitting in her car, unable to go inside, waiting for the courage to face what she'd find. And the phrase "body trust" — the radical idea that your body knows what you need better than your brain does.

1. **Three deep breaths in your body.** Sit somewhere quiet. Close your eyes. Take three breaths, but don't think about them. Feel them — in your chest, your belly, your ribs. That's body trust. That's the beginning.

2. **Write the thing you're afraid to say.** Don't send it. Just write it. Glennon's therapist told her to say the hard thing in the room, but the first step is admitting to yourself what it is. Name it.

3. **One body kindness.** Do one thing today that honors what your body needs, not what your brain thinks it should want. Eat when you're hungry. Stop when you're full. Rest when you're tired. The war with your body doesn't end with a victory. It ends with a truce.
