**Breath** by James Nestor

You take about 25,000 breaths a day. You've been doing it since the moment you were born. You've never thought about it for more than a few seconds. James Nestor wants to change that — because, he argues, modern humans have forgotten how to breathe correctly, and this forgetting is making us sick in ways we don't connect to our lungs. *Breath* is a work of investigative journalism that reads like an adventure story: Nestor travels to ancient tombs, consults with pulmonologists, joins a free-diving champion, visits a Russian dentist who breaks people's jaws, and gets his own nasal passages and mouth surgically packed to experience what it's like to breathe through a straw. The book's central claim is audacious: we have lost the ability to breathe properly, and restoring it can treat everything from snoring to anxiety to asthma.

**The great extinction of the nose.** Nestor begins with a startling fact: humans are the only mammals whose faces have been shrinking over evolutionary time. We've lost our prominent jaws, large sinuses, and wide nasal passages because of diet — specifically, the shift to soft, processed foods that require less chewing. This has led to smaller mouths, crooked teeth, and — most critically — narrowed airways. Orthodontists have treated it as a dental problem for generations, pulling teeth and wiring jaws. Nestor argues it's a breathing problem: we've engineered our own inability to breathe through our noses. Mouth breathing has been normalized. But Nestor presents evidence that chronic mouth breathing is linked to everything from snoring to cognitive decline. The nose is not a backup airway — it's the primary organ for breathing, designed to filter, warm, and pressurize air.

**The lost art of nasal breathing.** Nestor's own experiment: with Stanford researchers, he had his nose completely packed for ten days, forcing him to breathe only through his mouth. The results were alarming — his blood pressure spiked, his heart rate variability crashed, he developed sleep apnea, his snoring went from zero to loud, and his cognition fogged. When the packing came out, everything returned to normal. The lesson: nasal breathing is not optional. The nose produces nitric oxide, a molecule that opens blood vessels, lowers blood pressure, and kills pathogens. Mouth breathing bypasses this entire system, which Nestor calls "the worst medical mistake humans ever made." He traces the epidemic of mouth breathing to changes in diet — soft food requires less chewing, which leads to underdeveloped jaws and narrowed nasal passages over generations. People think mouth breathing is harmless — maybe a little unattractive, but not unhealthy. Nestor's data says it's a major health risk that's been hiding in plain sight.

**The perfect breath: 5.5 seconds in, 5.5 seconds out.** Nestor discovers that the traditional breathing ratio found in ancient texts — a slow, rhythmic pattern of roughly 5.5 seconds inhale and 5.5 seconds exhale — corresponds to a resonant frequency that synchronizes the heart, lungs, and brain. This cadence, about 5.5 breaths per minute, maximizes heart rate variability, relaxes the nervous system, and puts the body in a state of calm alertness. Most modern adults breathe 12-20 times per minute — fast, shallow, and inefficient. We think deep breathing means taking a big gulp of air. Nestor shows that slower, not deeper, is the key. It's not about volume; it's about frequency.

**The Buteyko method and carbon dioxide tolerance.** Nestor explores the work of Konstantin Buteyko, a Soviet doctor who discovered that many breathing problems stem from *overbreathing* — taking in more air than the body needs. Overbreathing flushes too much carbon dioxide from the blood, and CO2 is not just waste; it's essential for oxygen release. Without enough CO2, the hemoglobin holds onto oxygen instead of releasing it to tissues — a phenomenon called the **Bohr effect**. Buteyko developed a method of breath reduction — breathing less, not more — that has helped thousands of asthmatics reduce their medication. Nestor visits a Buteyko clinic and tries the method himself, finding that a few minutes of reduced breathing raises his CO2 tolerance and shifts his autonomic nervous system toward calm. When you feel short of breath, your instinct is to gasp for air. Buteyko says the opposite: the urge to breathe more is a sign that you need to breathe *less* to restore CO2 balance. Gasping makes the problem worse.

**Mouth taping, tongue posture, and the Mewing phenomenon.** Nestor dedicates significant space to the mechanical aspects of breathing. Mouth taping — placing a small piece of porous tape over the lips at night — forces nasal breathing during sleep, reducing snoring and improving sleep quality. Tongue posture — resting the tongue against the roof of the mouth instead of the floor — opens the airway and supports proper facial development. Nestor examines the "mewing" trend (named after orthodontist John Mew) — placing the tongue on the palate to widen the jaw over time — and finds credible science underneath the social-media hype. These interventions sound like fringe wellness. Nestor cites published research in major journals showing that mouth taping improves sleep apnea, that tongue posture affects airway size, and that chewing hard foods in childhood prevents dental crowding.

**The free diver's breath hold.** Nestor learns from free-divers — athletes who hold their breath for minutes at a time at extreme depths — that the body is far more capable of oxygen conservation than we realize. Free-divers breathe slowly and deliberately, reducing their breath rate to 2-3 breaths per minute before dives. The discipline, Nestor realizes, is not about holding breath longer; it's about the preparation — the slow, rhythmic breathing that oxygenates the body without hyperventilating. This pre-dive breathing is the opposite of what most people do under stress, which is to gasp and hyperventilate, making the situation worse by flushing out CO2. Our default approach to breath is "more oxygen is better." Free-divers know that the goal is not more oxygen but efficient oxygen use.

The idea I couldn't shake after reading *Breath* is the **mouth breathing catastrophe**. I had never considered that the way I breathe — through my mouth when I'm distracted, sleeping, or exercising — might be causing measurable health problems. The idea that the nose is an active organ, not a passive passage, changes the way I think about something I do 25,000 times a day. The second idea is the **Bohr effect** — the counterintuitive discovery that CO2 is essential for releasing oxygen. It rewrites the simple narrative of "breathe in oxygen, breathe out CO2" into something more complex and more interesting.

Nestor is a journalist, not a scientist, and the book sometimes overclaims. He presents correlational evidence as causal, cherry-picks studies that support his thesis, and treats anecdotal results from extreme experiments (his own ten-day nasal packing) as more definitive than they are. The book also has a "here's the secret that mainstream medicine doesn't want you to know" tone that gets tiresome. Some of the interventions he recommends — like Buteyko-style reduced breathing — can be genuinely dangerous for people with certain conditions (pulmonary hypertension, severe asthma) if done without supervision. The mouth taping advice should come with a warning for people with obstructive sleep apnea not yet treated with CPAP. And the Mewing content, while scientifically grounded in part, has been co-opted by online communities making claims far beyond what the evidence supports.

Anyone who snores, has asthma or anxiety, or wants to improve their sleep quality. Also great for athletes looking for marginal gains. People with untreated sleep apnea should see a doctor before trying mouth taping. Also skip if you're looking for rigorous, peer-reviewed evidence — this is popular science, not a textbook.

1. Today, pay attention to your breath. Every time you remember, check: are you breathing through your nose or your mouth? Consciously switch to nasal breathing each time. Do this for two days.
2. Try the 5.5-second breath: set a timer for 5 minutes. Inhale through your nose for 5.5 seconds, exhale for 5.5 seconds. That's it. Do this twice today.
3. Tonight, place a small piece of porous medical tape vertically over your lips before bed. If it feels uncomfortable, remove it. Most people adapt within a few nights. Order some from a pharmacy.
4. Practice tongue posture: close your mouth, place the tip of your tongue on the roof of your mouth just behind your front teeth, and flatten the entire tongue against the palate. Hold for 30 seconds, several times a day. This should become your default tongue position.
