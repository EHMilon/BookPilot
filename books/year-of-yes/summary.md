Shonda Rhimes was a cultural phenomenon — the creator of *Grey's Anatomy*, *Scandal*, *Private Practice*, the showrunner who built Thursday night into a television empire — and she was terrified of interviews. Terrified of public speaking. Terrified of the red carpet. Terrified of being seen. She had seventeen shows on air, an Emmy collection, and a calendar so full that she never had to face the one thing she was most afraid of: saying yes. **Year of Yes** is the story of what happened when her sister, at a Thanksgiving dinner, looked at her and said the sentence that changed everything: "You never say yes to anything." Rhimes, defensive, protested — and then realized her sister was right. She had built a life that allowed her to hide in plain sight. The success was real. The isolation was also real. So she made a deal with herself: for one year, she would say yes to everything that scared her.

The core insight of the book is deceptively simple: fear and shyness are not the same thing. Rhimes spent her life believing she was shy — a quiet, introverted person who simply didn't like attention. The year of yes taught her that she was not shy. She was scared. Shyness is a preference; fear is a cage. And she had built her cage so beautifully that no one noticed she was trapped in it.

The first yes was the hardest. She was asked to give a commencement speech at Dartmouth, and every instinct screamed no. She said yes. She spent the next six months in a state of dread, writing and rewriting the speech, convinced she would bomb. She gave it. It went well. And something shifted.

*The Trap:* The obvious trap is the "just say yes" cliché — the idea that saying yes to everything is the path to a fuller life. Rhimes is clear that she is not advocating for universal yes-ness. She's advocating for saying yes to the things that scare you, specifically because they scare you. The yes has to be directed at fear, not at exhaustion.

Rhimes organizes her year around categories of yes: yes to work, yes to play, yes to food, yes to body, yes to voice. Each section follows the same arc: the fear, the resistance, the yes, and the discovery on the other side.

The most surprising revelation is that Rhimes was already saying yes to work — obsessively, compulsively — but it was the wrong kind of yes. She was saying yes to production schedules and staffing decisions and the machinery of television. She was not saying yes to the creative risks that scared her. The year of yes forced her to ask: what would I do if I weren't afraid of failing? The answer was a show about a Black woman managing a crisis — which became *Scandal*. The show existed before the year of yes; the willingness to *own* it as hers, to stop downplaying her ambition, came during it.

This section is the most fun. Rhimes, who had convinced herself she hated parties, going out, and having fun, realized she had never actually *tried* having fun as an adult. She had spent her twenties building a career and her thirties working constantly. Play felt frivolous, irresponsible, dangerous. She forced herself to go to a party. She hated it at first. She kept going. She started to enjoy it. She learned the distinction between being alone by choice and being alone by default.

*The Trap:* The "play" section can feel like a wealthy person's problem — it's easier to have fun when you have a driver and an assistant and a house in the Hamptons. Rhimes acknowledges this obliquely but doesn't fully grapple with how privilege shapes her ability to say yes. A mother working two jobs cannot simply "say yes" to a weekend away.

Rhimes has always been fat, and she has always hated her body. The year of yes forced her to confront the weight of that hatred — not the weight on the scale but the weight of a lifetime of self-rejection. She writes about buying clothes that fit, about learning to see her body as an instrument rather than an ornament, about the radical act of looking in the mirror and not looking away. Her reframe is subtle but powerful: "I stopped trying to lose weight long enough to ask myself what I was trying to lose." The answer was shame, not pounds.

This is the emotional climax. Rhimes, whose characters say the things she could never say, learned to use her own voice. She writes about the terror of speaking without a script, of being interviewed and having to be herself rather than the smart, edited version of herself. She describes the physical sensation of wanting to disappear — the heat, the tight throat, the desperate wish to be anyone else — and the practice of staying present anyway. By the end of the year, she had testified before Congress (about diversity in Hollywood) and given keynote speeches to thousands of people. The fear never fully left. But it stopped being in charge.

*The Trap:* The voice chapter risks the "lean in" problem — celebrating individual voice without acknowledging that some people's voices are silenced by structures that individual will cannot overcome. Rhimes is a Black woman in an industry that silences Black women daily. Her individual yes is real and powerful; it is also a story that was possible because of her specific position.

The most quoted section of the book is not about yes at all — it's about the question Rhimes hates most: "How do you do it all?" Her response has become legendary: "I don't do it all. I don't even do half of it. I do some of it. Badly. And I outsource the rest." She writes about the myth of the superwoman, the lie that successful women are effortlessly managing career, family, body, and soul. She outsources everything she can. She orders takeout. She lets the laundry pile up. She stopped pretending to be good at things she's not.

This chapter alone is worth the price of the book for anyone who has ever felt inadequate looking at someone else's Instagram. "I don't want to be a role model. I want to be a warning: if Shonda Rhimes can let the laundry pile up, so can you."

*The Trap:* The dismissiveness can become a pose. Rhimes is clearly proud of her productivity and her empire, and the "I don't care" attitude sometimes feels like a performance. The truth is more complicated: she cares deeply, works obsessively, and the outsourced labor is made possible by wealth that most people don't have.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is that the opposite of fear is not courage — it's presence. Rhimes didn't become fearless. She didn't stop being afraid of interviews or red carpets or speaking her mind. She just stopped letting fear decide. That's a different thing entirely, and it's more useful. Courage has a heroic quality that feels unattainable. Presence is just showing up, scared, and not leaving. That, I think, is the real meaning of the year of yes: not a year of bravery, but a year of showing up anyway.

The book is too long for what it contains. The central insight — say yes to what scares you — is powerful but not complex, and Rhimes stretches it over three hundred pages with significant repetition. The writing is conversational to the point of sloppiness in places; sentences that Rhimes would never let through a script get published as wisdom. The book also glosses over her immense privilege in ways that can feel oblivious: the yes to play is easier when you have nannies and assistants; the yes to work is safer when you have a decades-long track record of success. And the book's treatment of her weight loss journey at the end feels like it undermines the body acceptance chapter — she eventually has gastric sleeve surgery and loses significant weight, and the book's framing of this as a yes rather than a surrender to the same body standards she critiqued is unconvincing.
