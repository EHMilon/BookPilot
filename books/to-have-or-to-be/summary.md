Erich Fromm published *To Have or to Be?* in 1976, near the end of his life, and it reads as his final diagnosis of what ails modern civilization. The book grew out of a distinction he had been developing for decades: between the **having mode** of existence (orienting life around possession, consumption, and control) and the **being mode** (orienting life around experience, activity, and growth). The title is not rhetorical. Fromm genuinely believes that this single distinction — how you relate to the world, to yourself, to others — determines the quality of your entire existence. It is, he claims, the fundamental choice of human life.

Fromm traces the psychological origins of the having mode to the fear of death. If I identify myself with what I have — my body, my money, my status, my beliefs — then loss becomes annihilation. The having mode is a defense against mortality. "To have" is to build a fortress against the void. The problem is that the fortress is built of sand. No amount of possessions can make you immortal, and the frantic accumulation only makes the fear worse. "Man's anxiety about death," Fromm writes, "is not the fear of suffering but the fear of losing what one has." The being mode, by contrast, faces death not as a loss but as a natural transition — there is nothing to cling to because you were never a thing to begin with.

The having mode is the default setting of industrial capitalism. In this mode, I relate to everything as an object to be acquired, owned, consumed, or controlled. I have a car. I have a job. I have a partner. I have anxiety. I have a religion. The verb "to have" colonizes every domain of life. Even my own identity becomes something I have: "I am the person who owns X, earns Y, belongs to Z." Fromm traces this orientation back to the property-based societies of the modern West, but he argues it has become a character structure, not just an economic arrangement. "The having mode of existence," he writes, "is rooted in the biological urge for survival. The greed for having, however, is not rooted in human nature; it is the result of a particular social order."

The being mode is harder to describe because Western languages are built around having. To be is not to possess but to experience, to relate, to act. "In the being mode," Fromm writes, "one is not a thing; one does not have a self; one is." The being mode is alive in the child who is fully absorbed in play, in the artist who loses herself in creation, in the lover who is present to the other without grasping. It is the experience of aliveness itself — not as a possession but as a state. Fromm draws heavily on Meister Eckhart, Marx, and the Buddha to articulate this mode. The common thread across these traditions is the idea that the highest human state is not accumulation but letting go — not having more but being more.

**The Trap** is to read this as a simple condemnation of material possessions. Fromm is not saying you should give away everything you own. He is saying that the *orientation* of having — the deep belief that more possessions, more experiences, more relationships will fill the emptiness — is fundamentally mistaken. The emptiness cannot be filled from outside. It can only be dissolved from within. "The having mode is based on the principle of deadness," Fromm writes. "It is the mode of appropriation and possession, of control and consumption. The being mode is based on the principle of aliveness." You can have very little and still be in the having mode. You can have a lot and be in the being mode. The distinction is not about quantity. It is about relationship.

Fromm applies this distinction to every major domain of human life. **Learning**: in the having mode, you accumulate information like property — facts, dates, formulas to be stored and retrieved. In the being mode, you learn by engaging, questioning, transforming yourself through the encounter with knowledge. **Memory**: in the having mode, you memorize mechanically. In the being mode, you remember by making the past alive again through active recall — you do not have the memory, you are the one who remembers. **Conversation**: in the having mode, people talk to exchange information or to reinforce their positions. Each person is waiting for their turn to speak. In the being mode, conversation is a genuine encounter — both people are changed by it. **Authority**: in the having mode, authority is something you possess (I am the boss, I have the power) or something you submit to (they have authority over me). In the being mode, authority is based on competence — the teacher who knows more, the guide who has gone further.

One of the book's most powerful chapters is Fromm's analysis of **religion** through the having/being lens. In the having mode, religion becomes a possession — I have faith, I belong to the correct church, I perform the right rituals. "Faith becomes the possession of a system of ideas," Fromm writes, "a set of dogmas which one accepts as true because they are handed down by an authority." This is exactly what Jesus and the prophets were attacking: the transformation of a living relationship with God into a dead possession of correct beliefs. The great religious traditions, Fromm argues, all began as critiques of the having mode. They all became its instruments. Buddhism, with its teaching of non-attachment, is perhaps the most explicit attempt to escape the having mode. But even Buddhism, in practice, often becomes a new thing to have — I have enlightenment, I have a meditation practice, I have the correct interpretation of the dharma.

The book ends with **practical proposals** for shifting from having to being. Fromm offers a list of concrete steps: simplify your needs, reduce your consumption, develop your capacity for solitude, learn to give without expecting return, overcome greed and hatred through awareness, let go of the ego. These are not self-help tips. They are the outline of a radical transformation. Fromm knows how difficult this transformation is. "To be fully born," he writes in the book's closing pages, "is the task of a lifetime." He also acknowledges that individual transformation, while necessary, is not sufficient. The having mode is embedded in social structures — capitalism, consumer culture, the advertising industry — that constantly reinforce it. Personal change must be accompanied by social change.

**The honest critique.** The book suffers from the same abstraction that plagues all of Fromm's later work. The having/being distinction is powerful but oversimplified. Real life is a mixture of both modes, and Fromm does not give much guidance on how to navigate that mixture. His critique of capitalism is so total that it leaves no room for the genuine goods that material prosperity has brought — health, longevity, freedom from backbreaking labor. The practical proposals at the end feel perfunctory compared to the depth of the diagnosis. And the book's tone can be preachy. Fromm writes like a man who has seen the truth and is tired of waiting for others to see it. The reader can feel lectured rather than invited.

**Who should read it and who should not.** Read it if you have ever felt that having more does not make you feel more alive. Read it if the consumer lifestyle feels hollow and you want a language for why. Do not read it if you are looking for a balanced assessment of capitalism — Fromm is not interested in balance. He is interested in transformation.
