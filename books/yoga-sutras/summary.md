There's a joke that's been circulating in yoga circles for decades: no one has actually *read* the Yoga Sutras — everyone has read a commentary on them. The original text is 196 aphorisms, some only three words long. You could memorize the whole thing in a week. But you could spend a lifetime trying to live it. That's both the problem and the point.

The Yoga Sutras were compiled somewhere between 400 BCE and 400 CE by a sage named Patanjali — and right away we hit our first complication. Patanjali probably wasn't one person. The name may refer to a scholar, a grammarian, a physician, or all three. The text itself is a synthesis of older traditions, pulled together like a field guide to the mind at a time when the mind was the only territory worth mapping. It's the foundational text of classical yoga, but it bears almost no resemblance to what most people practice when they roll out a mat in 2025.

There's a story about a monk who asked his teacher how long it would take to master the Yoga Sutras. The teacher said, "How many sutras are there?" The monk said, "One hundred and ninety-six." The teacher said, "Then it will take you one hundred and ninety-six lifetimes." This is the text's energy. It's not trying to be efficient. It's trying to be true.

Let's start with the problem the sutras are trying to solve. Patanjali's diagnosis is blunt: human beings live in a state of chronic misidentification. We think we're our thoughts, our bodies, our emotions, our roles. We're not. Beneath all of that is something he calls *purusha* — pure awareness, the seer, the witness. The practice of yoga, in his framework, is the systematic process of disentangling awareness from all the things it has gotten tangled up in. The famous second sutra says it in ten words: *Yogash chitta vritti nirodhah*. "Yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations of consciousness."

This distinction between *purusha* (pure consciousness) and *prakriti* (nature, including the mind and body) is the axis the entire system turns on. In Patanjali's cosmology, everything you can perceive — including your thoughts, your emotions, your sense of being a person — belongs to *prakriti*. It's all matter, even the subtle stuff. The *seer* — the one who witnesses all of this — is *purusha*. And the tragedy of human existence is that we confuse the two. We think we're the movie when we're actually the screen.

I don't know if I *believe* this, metaphysically speaking. But I've found it useful as a *practice*. When I'm caught in anxiety, asking "who is noticing this anxiety?" doesn't make the anxiety go away, but it creates a tiny gap between me and it. And in that gap, something shifts. That's what Patanjali is building toward.

That's the whole project. Everything else is commentary.

*Chitta vritti* — the fluctuations of the mind — come in five varieties, according to Patanjali. Correct knowledge (*pramana*), incorrect knowledge (*viparyaya*), imagination (*vikalpa*), sleep (*nidra*), and memory (*smriti*). What's striking is that even *correct* knowledge is included. Patanjali isn't saying stop thinking incorrectly. He's saying stop identifying with *any* of the mind's activity, even the accurate parts. The problem isn't that you have bad thoughts; it's that you think you *are* your thoughts. Whether they're right or wrong is secondary.

**The Trap in Reading This Too Literally:** A lot of Western Buddhists and non-dual types have taken this to mean "don't think" — that the goal is a blank mind. Patanjali doesn't say that. He says don't be *ruled* by the fluctuations. A still lake reflects clearly. A choppy one doesn't. The water is the same either way. The practice is about clarity, not emptiness.

The sutras are organized into four chapters (*padas*). The first (*Samadhi Pada*) lays out the goal and the methods. The second (*Sadhana Pada*) gives the practical path — the eight limbs, the kleshas (afflictions), the mechanics of karma. The third (*Vibhuti Pada*) describes the powers that arise from deep practice — and includes a strong warning about getting attached to them. The fourth (*Kaivalya Pada*) describes liberation itself.

I want to camp on the second chapter because that's where most of the useful, daily-life material lives. Patanjali introduces the *kleshas* — five afflictions that are the root of all suffering: ignorance (*avidya*), ego (*asmita*), attachment (*raga*), aversion (*dvesha*), and clinging to life (*abhinivesha*). These aren't sins. They're not moral failings. They're simply the patterns that keep the cycle spinning. And they work in a chain: ignorance feeds ego, ego creates attachment, attachment creates aversion, and the whole system is held together by the biological drive to keep existing.

*Avidya* — ignorance — is the root of all the others. Not ignorance of facts, but ignorance of your true nature. You think you're the body, so you spend your life trying to protect it, adorn it, extend it. You think you're the mind, so you spend your life trying to prove it, entertain it, medicate it. Patanjali says: your true nature is awareness itself. Everything else is a costume you've mistaken for skin.

**The Trap with *Abhinivesha*:** The deep fear of death (*abhinivesha*) is the last klesha to dissolve, Patanjali says, because it's encoded in the very structure of conscious existence. Even the wise feel it. The goal isn't to become fearless; it's to recognize that what you *really* are was never born and can't die. This is the hardest part of the text to swallow if you're not already inclined toward non-dual philosophy. If you're a materialist, or even a skeptic, the whole framework can feel like metaphysical speculation dressed as science.

The practical path — the eight limbs (ashtanga) — is where the rubber hits the road. The yamas (restraints), niyamas (observances), asana (posture), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (sense withdrawal), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), samadhi (absorption). What's often lost in modern yoga is that *asana* in Patanjali's system means exactly one thing: a comfortable, steady seated position for meditation. That's it. The 500 fancy poses we now call "yoga" are a later development. The original system only needed you to sit still and not fall over.

Patanjali defines *asana* in Sutra 2.46: *Sthira sukham asanam*. "The posture should be steady and comfortable." That's the entire instruction. Everything else — the blocks, the straps, the alignment cues, the 90-minute flow — is elaboration. And that's fine, as long as you know that the elaboration is not the source.

The third chapter is where things get weird. Patanjali describes the *siddhis* — psychic powers that arise from deep concentration: knowledge of past lives, invisibility, strength of an elephant, the ability to read minds. For centuries, commentators have debated whether these are literal descriptions of powers, metaphors for psychological insight, or a kind of mystic wish-fulfillment. I think the most useful reading is the warning he embeds: *te samadhav upasarga vyutthane siddhayah*. "These powers are obstacles to samadhi." In other words, if you start getting psychic powers and get excited about them, you've missed the point. The point is freedom, not powers. The text itself includes the antidote.

The text also distinguishes between different kinds of samadhi — the famous distinction between *samprajnata* (with seed, where the object of meditation is still present) and *asamprajnata* (without seed, where even the object dissolves). This is where the sutras go full mystic and you either trust the map or you don't. There's no empirical way to verify whether these states exist. The sutras are a map of inner experience written by someone who had the experience and tried to describe it. If you haven't had it, the descriptions can sound like poetry at best, nonsense at worst.

My own relationship with this is: I use what works, I stay curious about what doesn't, and I don't pretend to have mastered anything. The sutras are not a test you pass. They're a conversation you keep having.

**Honest Criticism:** The Yoga Sutras are fragmentary, elliptical, and almost impossible to read without a commentary. Different translations disagree on fundamental meanings. The text assumes a metaphysical framework (rebirth, karma as cosmic law, a permanent soul) that many modern readers will reject. And the recommendations — celibacy, extreme asceticism, withdrawal from society — feel archaic and, for most people, actively unhelpful. The text was written for monks in forest hermitages. Applying it to a life in 2026 with a job, a phone, and relationships requires significant translation.

Also: the text has been weaponized. Modern "authentic yoga" purists often use Patanjali to dismiss body-positive, accessible, or trauma-informed yoga as "not real yoga." That's a misuse. Patanjali himself would probably have been more interested in your compassion than your alignment.

**What Will Actually Stick With Me:** The kleshas. I think about *avidya* constantly — the way I mistake my thoughts for myself. And *raga/dvesha* — the push and pull, the grabbing and pushing away that exhausts me every day. The sutras gave me a vocabulary for noticing when I'm caught. They didn't stop the catching, but they made me faster at seeing it.

Also Sutra 1.12: *Abhyasa vairagyabhyam tan nirodhah*. "Practice and detachment together still the fluctuations." Not one or the other. Both. You need the discipline to keep showing up (*abhyasa*) and the willingness to let go of results (*vairagya*). Without practice, detachment becomes laziness. Without detachment, practice becomes obsession. They balance each other like two wings.
