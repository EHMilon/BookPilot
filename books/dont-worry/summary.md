Shunmyo Masuno wrote two books in quick succession, and *Don't Worry* is the sequel that stands on its own. Where *The Art of Simple Living* was about decluttering your lifestyle, this one goes straight for the knot in your chest. It's about worry — the constant, low-grade hum of anxiety that many of us have normalized as just "how life feels."

Same format: short chapters, each a single lesson. But the voice is sharper here. Masuno knows that worrying isn't a mental habit you can just "let go of" — it's a survival mechanism that's gone rogue. His approach isn't to fight anxiety but to see through it.

Masuno wrote this book in response to what he saw as an epidemic of unnecessary suffering. As a Zen priest, people come to him with their deepest anxieties — work pressure, family conflict, existential dread. What he's noticed is that most of them aren't suffering from real problems. They're suffering from *anticipatory* suffering — the pain of imagining futures that haven't arrived. His temple in Yokohama is a quiet sanctuary in one of the world's busiest cities, and he sees the contrast every day. Outside the gate: noise, speed, worry. Inside: stillness, slowness, peace. This book is his attempt to help people build a temple inside themselves.

Masuno's opening argument is deceptively simple: most worry is about things that haven't happened. We project into the future, imagine worst-case scenarios, and then feel the emotional weight of those scenarios as if they were real. The suffering is real. The cause is imagined.

*"Worry is interest paid on a debt you may never owe."*

He doesn't mean this as a platitude. It's a practical instruction. When you're worrying about something, ask yourself: *Is this happening right now, in this moment, or am I projecting?* If it's a projection, the worry is not a signal — it's static. You can let it go.

Masuno draws on his Zen training to reframe anxiety. In zazen practice, when a distracting thought arises, you don't fight it. You notice it, label it, and return to the breath. The anxiety is not the problem — the *struggle against anxiety* is the problem. The resistance creates the tension.

**The Trap of Anxiety Management** — This is the book's most counterintuitive claim. The entire self-help industry around anxiety — the breathing exercises, the cognitive reframing, the meditation apps — can become another form of avoidance. You're not managing anxiety. You're performing rituals to feel like you're in control. Masuno suggests a harder path: just let yourself be anxious without trying to fix it. The anxiety passes through you, like weather. It's your *reaction* to anxiety — the panic at feeling it — that turns a passing storm into a permanent climate.

*"Do not try to stop the waves. Learn to ride them."*

Masuno introduces *zanshin*, a Japanese concept from martial arts meaning "the mind that remains." A samurai doesn't relax completely after striking. He maintains a residual alertness. Not tension — just awareness. The same applies to worrying. You can't turn off your ability to anticipate danger, nor should you. But you can calibrate it. Most worries are false alarms. The practice of *zanshin* is to keep your awareness open without letting any single anxious thought dominate the field.

Masuno retells a classic Zen story: a monk is meditating by a river when he sees a boat drifting toward a dock. He watches, annoyed, as the boat bumps into the dock. Then he realizes the boat is empty. His anger dissolves instantly. The lesson: our anxiety is almost always about the *imagined passenger* — the person we think is steering the boat toward us. When we realize the boat is empty, there's nothing to be angry at. Most of what worries us is an empty boat bumping into things.

*"If you can regard everything as an empty boat, no one will be able to make you angry."*

One of Masuno's sharpest observations is that perfectionism is a form of worry — you're worried that things won't be good enough, that you won't be good enough, that someone will notice a flaw. But perfection doesn't exist in Zen. A perfectly raked garden is lifeless. The fallen leaf, the slightly crooked stone, the moss growing where it wasn't planned — that's what makes the garden real. Masuno suggests doing one thing imperfectly every day, deliberately. Send an email with a typo. Leave a picture frame slightly crooked. The world doesn't end. And you start to accept that imperfection is not a failure — it's the natural state.

*"The perfect person is not the one who never makes mistakes. It is the one who accepts that mistakes are part of being human."*

Another story lands differently. Masuno tells of an emperor who asked a Zen master what he worried about. The master said: "I worry about whether I will wake up tomorrow." The emperor laughed — "But you're a master. Shouldn't you be beyond worry?" The master replied: "A master is not someone without worry. A master is someone who has accepted that worry is part of being alive."

This reframes the entire project. The goal is not a worry-free life. That's impossible and would be undesirable — worry kept our ancestors alive. The goal is a life where worry doesn't *run* you.

One of the book's most practical chapters addresses the feeling of being overwhelmed by too many concerns. Masuno's advice: shrink your container. If a problem is outside your direct control — the economy, other people's opinion of you, global politics — it doesn't belong in your worry-container. Let it go. Focus only on what you can directly affect.

*"There is no point worrying about things you cannot change. The energy is better spent on what you can."*

**The Trap of Global Empathy** — Masuno gently critiques modern consciousness. We're connected to everything. A war across the world, a friend's distant crisis, a stranger's online suffering — we carry it all. This is not compassion. It's fragmentation. Real compassion starts with being fully present to the person in front of you. You can't save everyone. Saving one person, in this moment, is enough.

Masuno recommends a practice that sounds paradoxical for anxiety: do nothing. Not meditate. Not breathe deeply. Not count. Just sit and let your mind do whatever it does. Don't try to control it. Don't try to calm it. Just watch it. He compares this to a jar of muddy water. If you keep shaking the jar, the water stays muddy. If you set it down and leave it, the mud settles on its own. Your mind is the same. Constant interventions keep it stirred up. Sitting without trying gives it permission to settle.

One of the most effective sections describes a physical practice. When you notice yourself worrying, deliberately relax your jaw. Drop your shoulders. Unclench your hands. The body and mind are not separate. If you can relax the body, the mind follows. Masuno suggests doing this every time you catch yourself worrying — not to "fix" the worry but to remind yourself that the tension is optional.

His chapter on sleep is notable. He says insomnia is often just the mind refusing to surrender control for the night. Sleep is a small death — a letting go of the self. If you can't sleep, it's because you're still holding on to the day, still trying to solve problems that won't be solved until morning. His advice: when you lie down, say to yourself, "There is nothing more I can do today." Then close your eyes and mean it.

*"Sleep is the ultimate act of trust — that the world will survive without your vigilance."*

Like *The Art of Simple Living*, this book suffers from the one-page format. The insights are real but thinly explored. A reader with clinical anxiety or depression may find the advice frustratingly inadequate — "just relax your jaw" is not going to stop a panic attack. The book works best for the worried well: people whose anxiety is manageable but unpleasant.

The Zen framing, while consistent, occasionally feels like a dodge. "Be present" is good advice but it's not always *useful* advice, especially when the present moment is genuinely painful. Masuno doesn't fully address the gap between spiritual insight and clinical reality.

And some chapters feel like padding. The advice to "take a walk" or "drink tea slowly" is repeated enough that the book could have been fifty pages shorter without losing anything essential.
