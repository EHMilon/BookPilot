This book is like nothing else Eagleman has written, and for a while I didn't know what to do with it. *Sum* is a collection of forty short stories about possible afterlives. Not "heaven and hell" afterlives—strange, philosophical, heartbreaking afterlives. If *Incognito* interrogates the subconscious brain and *Livewired* celebrates plasticity, *Sum* asks the question those books can't touch: what does it all *mean*?

Eagleman wrote this while finishing his PhD. It's a brain scientist's creative explosion—a what-if machine running on forty different settings. The result is a book you finish in two hours and think about for two years.

> "Each of us contains something like a small theater, in which we are the audience, the actor, the director, the lighting technician, the usher, the ticket taker, and the janitor."

**The afterlife as a re-run.** My favorite story in the collection: *Sum* imagines an afterlife where you relive your life, but with a twist—all the moments of each type are grouped together. You spend two months driving on highways. Seven months having sex. Six days clipping your nails. Fifteen months looking for lost items (keys, phones, glasses). *Eighteen months waiting in line.* The first time I read this, I laughed out loud. The second time, I felt a chill.

The story reveals what Eagleman calls **the math of life**: most of our existence is spent on things we don't think about. The big moments—weddings, births, achievements—are vanishingly rare in the actual arc of a life. Most of life is the ordinary texture: the commute, the washing up, the waiting. And if that's true, then how we spend our ordinary moments is the truest measure of who we are.

> "In heaven, you live your life again—but this time all the parts that belong together are consolidated."

**The Trap:** This story, and the book as a whole, could be read as a call to eliminate the mundane and maximize novelty. I think that's the wrong reading. Eagleman's point isn't that we should spend less time clipping our nails. It's that we should *notice* that we're clipping our nails. The ordinary isn't the enemy of the meaningful. It's where meaning actually lives, if we have the awareness to see it.

**The afterlife as a missed connection.** Another story that stopped me: you die and arrive in the afterlife, where you meet the people you could have been—the versions of yourself that would have emerged if you'd made different choices at key moments. The doctor you could have been. The spouse you could have been. The artist you gave up on. They're all there, fully realized, looking at you with a mixture of recognition and disappointment.

This is Eagleman's exploration of the **multiverse of the self**. Every decision we make prunes away other possible selves. The paths not taken don't disappear—they persist as latent possibilities in the architecture of our brains. The person you are is the result of an immense number of forks, each one closing off a potential version of you.

The story hits harder because Eagleman isn't moralizing. He's not saying you should have been the doctor. He's not saying you made the wrong choice. He's saying those other selves are real, in some sense—they exist in the map of roads not taken. Acknowledging them is part of understanding who you became. Denying them is denying the weight of your own choices.

**The afterlife as a conversation with God made of humans.** One of the most creative stories: God is not a single being but a community—a "hive mind" made up of all the people who ever lived. God was once imperfect, making mistakes, being petty and generous and confused. But as more people die and join the hive, God becomes wiser, more complete, less flawed. The universe is God slowly learning to be better by absorbing human experience.

Eagleman is a scientist, but this story is as theological as anything I've read. It imagines a God that *needs* humanity, that *grows* through our experiences. Your life, in this telling, is not a test you pass or fail. It's an experience that adds to the database of the divine. Every joy, every mistake, every act of kindness or cruelty—it all becomes part of something larger.

**The afterlife as a search.** In my favorite story of the entire collection, you die and go to a kind of waiting room. There's no God, no judgment, no heaven or hell. You're told you can stay here as long as you need. Some people stay for days. Some stay for millennia. Because what does it mean to "move on" when you no longer have a body, a brain, a concept of time?

The story is about the **adjustment period**—the psychological transition from being a biological creature to something else entirely. Eagleman's neuroscience background shows: he understands how deeply our experience of time, self, and meaning is anchored in our biology. An afterlife without a brain isn't just a change of scenery. It's a collapse of everything that makes experience possible.

> "In the waiting room, you have no watch. There is no time. So you cannot tell if you have been waiting five minutes or five thousand years."

---

**The afterlife as a cosmic rebalancing.** In another story, Eagleman imagines an afterlife where God is a meticulous accountant. Every good deed and bad deed is quantified, but not in the way you'd expect. Small kindnesses are worth more than grand gestures because they require less ego investment. A smile to a stranger counts more than a million-dollar donation. The cosmic accounting is not about outcomes—it's about the *cost* to your ego.

This story resonated because it flips the usual morality on its head. We assume grand gestures matter more. Eagleman suggests the opposite: the small, unobserved kindnesses are the ones that reveal who we truly are, because they have no audience, no recognition, no reward. The story is a mirror held up to your daily life: how do you behave when no one is watching? Not because someone *is* watching in an afterlife sense, but because those unobserved moments *are* your character.

**The afterlife where you finally see everything from other perspectives.** One of the most technically creative stories: after death, you experience every moment of your life from the perspective of everyone it affected. You feel your mother's worry when you stayed out late. You feel the stranger's embarrassment when you made a thoughtless joke. You feel the subtle shift in your partner's heart when you said "I love you" distractedly. Every interaction is re-experienced from the other side.

The experience is not punishment. It's *completion*. You lived your life from one perspective, but your life wasn't just yours—it touched others, and that touch is part of the full story of who you were. This is Eagleman's most Buddhist story: the self is an illusion, and understanding your life means understanding the web of relationships you were embedded in. The "you" that you thought you were is only half the picture. The other half is everyone you affected.

**The afterlife as a cosmic joke.** And then there are the pure fun ones. In one story, you meet God and God says, "I made the universe as a joke. There is no meaning. Enjoy the absurdity." It's a two-page story that destroys every theological system in the book, then winks. Eagleman the scientist is showing his hand: the honest answer to "why are we here?" might be "no reason at all." And that's not depressing—it's liberating. If there's no cosmic script, you get to write your own.

**The afterlife as an infinite museum of what you overlooked.** In the collection's most quietly devastating piece, the afterlife is a vast museum. One wing is all the sunsets you walked past without looking. Another is the conversations you cut short. Another is the books you said you'd read but never opened. The museum is infinite, and it's all things you already had the opportunity to experience but didn't.

This story cuts because it's not about sin or virtue. It's about *attention*. The tragedy isn't that you did bad things—it's that you missed good things. Eagleman is making a neuroscientific point in fictional form: your brain is limited, and you spend most of your life filtering out more than you take in. The museum is a monument to the world you could have experienced if you'd been fully present. The only solution, in life, is to pay attention.

---

**The Core Takeaway:** There is no single "correct" vision of what comes after. The only honest answer to the question of meaning is: we don't know. But we have the capacity to imagine—and the way we imagine reveals what we value. *Sum* is not a book of answers. It's a book of questions, posed as stories. What matters to you? What have you left undone? What version of yourself are you not becoming? The answers aren't in the book. They're in you, stirred up by the stories.

**What Will Stick:** The piece I still think about is the suggestion that we don't grow *toward* something—we grow *into* something that already exists. The version of us that we're trying to become isn't in the future. It's already there, in the space of possibilities that our brain is constantly shaping. Our job isn't to build a new self. It's to remove the obstacles that keep us from being the self we already are.

**Honest Criticism:** *Sum* is not a neuroscience book, and readers coming from Eagleman's other work may be confused or disappointed. The stories are uneven—some are stunning miniatures (the re-run, the missed connection, the waiting room) and others feel like first drafts of ideas that deserved more development. The philosophical weight can feel heavy for such short pieces; Eagleman doesn't always earn the profundity he reaches for. And the book's brevity (112 pages) means it reads more like an exercise than a fully realized work. It's a writer's notebook published as a book, and some readers will find that charming and others will find it incomplete. The male gaze is also notable—most of the stories are narrated from a male perspective, and the treatment of women in the scenarios feels like a blind spot.
