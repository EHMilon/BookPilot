Erich Fromm spent his career arguing that modern humanity's deepest crisis is not economic or political but existential: we've forgotten how to *be* and become expert at *having*. This short, posthumous book is where he tries to turn diagnosis into practice. *The Art of Being* is Fromm's attempt to write an instruction manual for the soul — not a therapy, not a spiritual system, but a set of practices for dismantling the having mode and cultivating the being mode. It's incomplete, it's occasionally naive, and it contains some of the most useful ideas about how to actually change that Fromm ever wrote.

The book had a strange publishing history. Written in the 1970s, Fromm never finished it to his satisfaction. It was published after his death from his manuscripts, which means it reads less like a polished argument and more like a master at work in his workshop — sketches, repetitions, experiments. There's something appropriate about this. A book about the art of being shouldn't be too polished.

Fromm's core distinction is simple to state but devastating to apply. Every orientation to life falls along a spectrum between two poles. The **having mode** experiences reality through possession, consumption, and control. I *have* a car, I *have* a relationship, I *have* knowledge, I *have* anxiety. Everything is objectified. Even the self becomes something you own and manage — "I have a good personality" is a having-mode statement.

The **being mode** experiences reality through activity, presence, and relation. Being doesn't accumulate or consume. It participates. When you truly love, Fromm says, you're not "having" love; you're *being* love — the loving is the activity of your whole person. When you truly know something, you don't "have" knowledge; you're being the process of knowing.

Fromm is careful to say this isn't about material possessions per se. You can be materially simple but mentally consumed by having — wanting experiences, status, spiritual achievements. And you can be materially wealthy while living in the being mode — where your relationship to possessions is one of use and delight, not clinging and identity.

The easiest misreading is to turn "being" into another form of having. "I want to have more being," or "I'm practicing being." That's the having mode colonizing the language of being. Fromm is clear: being is not an achievement. It's an orientation you return to, not a state you acquire.

This isn't just individual psychology, Fromm insists. The having mode is built into the structure of industrial capitalism. Private property, consumption-driven identity, hierarchical organizations — all train us to experience ourselves and the world as things to be possessed. You don't just accidentally become a having-mode person. You are educated into it.

Fromm's critique here extends to what he calls the **marketing character** — the type of person for whom the self is a product to be sold. You develop the personality traits that will fetch the highest price on the personality market. You manage your image, your brand, your "value proposition." You don't have a self; you have a portfolio of desirable qualities. This is the having mode applied to identity itself.

There's a tempting radicalism here — "capitalism made me this way." Fromm would agree, but he doesn't let you off the hook. The sociocultural critique is necessary but insufficient. Even if the system changes, the individual still has to do the work of unlearning having. No revolution, Fromm says, produces a new person automatically.

This is where the book becomes genuinely useful. Fromm outlines specific practices for shifting from having to being, and they're surprisingly concrete for a psychoanalyst.

**Awareness.** Fromm means something more precise than "paying attention." He means becoming aware of the *unconscious* dimensions of your experience — the ways you automatically slip into having-mode without noticing. He recommends a practice of sitting quietly and simply paying attention to what arises: thoughts, sensations, emotions, without labeling them good or bad, without trying to change them. This is close to mindfulness meditation, but Fromm gives it a specific therapeutic purpose: to expose the having-mode patterns you normally run on autopilot.

**Concentration.** Not the productivity kind — the *presence* kind. Fromm advocates for learning to be fully in whatever you're doing, whether it's reading a book, listening to music, or having a conversation. Real concentration, he says, is the enemy of the having-mode. When you're genuinely concentrated, you're not trying to possess the experience. You're *in* it.

**Letting go of the ego.** Fromm takes a page from Zen Buddhism here. The having-mode is fundamentally ego-driven — the ego that wants to secure, possess, and defend. The being-mode requires a temporary suspension of the ego — not its destruction, but its relaxation. Fromm recommends practices of "letting go" — voluntary simplicity, moments of surrendering control, learning to experience without needing to own.

These practices can become spiritual materialism — using meditation or simplicity to build a better identity. "Look at me, I'm so non-attached." Fromm warns that even the practice of letting go can be co-opted by the having mode. The point is not to achieve anything, even "being." The point is to *be*.

Fromm devotes a substantial section to the limits of psychoanalysis. Traditional analysis, he argues, often becomes another form of having — a way of acquiring knowledge about yourself without actually changing. You learn about your Oedipus complex, your defense mechanisms, your childhood wounds. You accumulate self-knowledge like a collector. But knowing about yourself is not the same as transforming yourself.

What genuine therapy requires, Fromm says, is not insight but *experience* — a real encounter with the analyst that disrupts the having-mode patterns. The analyst doesn't *give* you anything. The analyst creates conditions in which you can experience yourself differently. The shift from "understanding why I am this way" to "experiencing that I can be otherwise."

The concept that lodged itself in my mind is Fromm's observation that most of what we call "thinking" is actually just the rearrangement of received ideas. We think we're generating opinions, but we're actually just reshuffling things we've taken from others. "Most people," Fromm writes, "have the illusion of thinking but actually they are only rearranging their own prejudices." Real thinking, being-mode thinking, is different. It's active, generative, risky. It doesn't defend what it already has. It's open to being changed by what it encounters.

The book is fragmentary and repetitive — you can feel the unfinished manuscript. Fromm says the same thing about being and having in five different ways, and some sections feel like notes for a longer argument rather than a completed chapter. The practices he recommends are genuinely useful, but he spends very little time on *why* they're so difficult to sustain or what to do when they fail. It's also culturally limited in its prescriptions; Fromm's ideal person sounds very much like a 1970s Western intellectual — contemplative, anti-consumerist, secular-spiritual.

The Zen influences are acknowledged but not deeply engaged. Fromm borrows the language of letting go and non-attachment but doesn't grapple with the rigorous monastic traditions that developed these practices over centuries. His DIY approach to spiritual practice — "just sit and be aware" — works for some and fails for others.

This is the book to read if Escape from Freedom left you wanting a *way forward* — if you accepted Fromm's diagnosis and now need a treatment plan. Skip it if you want systematic philosophy or rigorous spiritual guidance; treat it as the unfinished notebook of a brilliant mind, which is exactly what it is.

1. **The awareness sit.** Sit quietly for fifteen minutes. Don't meditate formally. Just notice what arises. But with a twist: watch for *having-mode* thoughts. "I should have a better job." "I need to have that experience." "I have this problem." Just notice them without judgment. Each one is a data point about how your mind operates.

2. **One activity, full presence.** Choose one routine activity — drinking tea, showering, walking to a destination. Do it with full concentration. No phone, no music, no thinking about other things. If your mind wanders, notice and return. The practice isn't the activity; it's the quality of presence.

3. **The having inventory.** Spend ten minutes writing down everything you're trying to *have* right now — possessions, experiences, relationships, status, even personality traits. Then ask yourself for each: "If I stopped trying to have this, what would be left?" The answer is not nothing.

4. **One genuine encounter.** Have one conversation today where you don't try to achieve anything — no persuading, no impressing, no extracting information. Just be fully present to the other person. Notice how painful and liberating this is.
