Michael Stone was that rare thing in the yoga world: a teacher who was as comfortable debating Derrida as he was leading a pranayama workshop. A scholar of Buddhist and Yogic philosophy, a psychotherapist, and a social activist, Stone wrote *The Inner Tradition of Yoga* as a corrective — a sharp, loving argument against the commodification of yoga and a call to return to its ethical and psychological depths. He died in 2017 at the age of 42, leaving behind a body of work that is still catching up to the questions he was asking.

The book's central provocation is this: modern yoga has become a self-improvement project when it was always meant to be a self-transcendence project. We practice to feel better, sleep better, look better, perform better — all while the original tradition insisted that the point of practice was to dismantle the very self that wants those improvements. Stone doesn't say this judgmentally; he includes himself in the critique. He's not standing above the problem, he's swimming in it, and the book reads like someone working out his own contradictions in real time.

This is the book's operating thesis. The modern mind operates on a scarcity model: if I get X (a better body, a calmer mind, a more spiritual life), then I will be happy. Stone argues that yoga offers a fundamentally different model. Instead of trying to satisfy desires within the current framework, yoga questions the framework itself. It asks: what if the problem isn't that you haven't gotten what you want, but that you're conditioned to want in a way that can never be satisfied?

"The practice of yoga," Stone writes, "is not a path to getting what you want. It's a practice of uprooting the very mechanism of wanting, so that you can meet life as it is, not as you wish it to be." This is a harder sell than "yoga will help you find peace," and Stone knows it. He spends the book making the case that this harder path is the only one that leads anywhere real.

Stone spends a crucial chapter on what he calls "spiritual materialism" — the tendency to use spiritual practice as another way to build a better self rather than to see through the self entirely. He draws on Chögyam Trungpa's work here but applies it specifically to yoga. The person who uses asana to get a better body, meditation to get a calmer mind, and philosophy to feel more enlightened is still caught in the same trap: using practice to reinforce the very self that practice is meant to dissolve.

The most recognizable form of this, Stone argues, is spiritual perfectionism — the belief that with enough practice, you will eventually stop being angry, anxious, or reactive. This is a fantasy. Stone is clear: practice doesn't eliminate difficult emotions. It changes your relationship to them. You still get angry; you just don't get *consumed* by anger. You still feel anxiety; you just don't *become* your anxiety. The person who claims to have transcended all negative emotions is either deluded or dishonest.

The book's treatment of the five kleshas (afflictions) — avidya (ignorance), asmita (egoism), raga (attachment), dvesha (aversion), and abhinivesha (fear of death) — is the most psychologically sophisticated I've encountered. Stone doesn't treat them as faults to be eliminated. He treats them as structures of experience that are neither good nor bad, but that condition our perception in ways we don't notice. The goal is not to get rid of the kleshas. The goal is to see them clearly enough that they no longer run the show unconsciously.

Avidya — the fundamental ignorance or mis-seeing — is the root klesha, and Stone's treatment of it is particularly nuanced. He doesn't mean ignorance as "not knowing facts." He means the way we habitually misperceive reality. We mistake the temporary for the permanent. We mistake the self for a solid, unchanging entity. We mistake pleasure for happiness. This mis-seeing is not a moral failing; it's the default operating system of the human mind. Yoga is the process of upgrading the OS.

The Trap for spiritual practitioners is using the klesha framework to beat themselves up. Stone warns against this explicitly: "When you notice attachment arising, the response is not 'I should not be attached.' The response is 'Ah, attachment is here.' The noticing itself is the practice. The judgment is another klesha."

This is Stone's most distinctive contribution. Most yogic philosophy treats the body as something to be transcended — a temporary vessel that the spirit inhabits. Stone, drawing on both his Buddhist training and his psychotherapy background, insists that this is a misunderstanding. The body is not a vehicle for awakening; awakening happens in and through the body. You don't meditate to leave your body behind. You meditate to inhabit it more fully.

"You can spend years on a meditation cushion trying to see through the illusion of the self," Stone writes, "but the body will keep pulling you back into the particular, the embodied, the here-and-now. And that is not a distraction from the practice. That is the practice."

This insight has profound implications for how we practice asana. The pose is not preparation for meditation. The pose is meditation — as long as you bring the same attention to it that you would to your breath. Stone describes a practice of "embodied mindfulness" where every sensation, every alignment adjustment, every moment of holding a pose becomes the object of meditation rather than a distraction from it.

Perhaps the most challenging section of the book is Stone's argument that yoga without social engagement is incomplete. He draws on the concept of karma yoga — the yoga of action — to argue that personal transformation must express itself in social transformation. A practice that makes you calmer but doesn't change how you engage with suffering in the world is not fulfilling the tradition's promise.

Stone was himself deeply involved in social activism, and he brings that experience into the book with stories of practicing with incarcerated people, of working with addiction, of the discomfort of bringing practice into contexts that are not clean, quiet, and comfortable. "If your yoga can only happen in a studio with incense and a specific teacher," he writes, "it's not yoga. It's a hobby. The real test of practice is how you meet the difficult moments of your actual life."

The book is dense. Stone's writing style is academic in places, and readers without some background in Buddhist or Yogic philosophy will find themselves lost in the middle chapters. The concepts are complex, and Stone doesn't always provide the concrete examples needed to ground them. There were sections I had to read twice, and sections I'm still not sure I fully understood.

The organization is also loose. The book reads more like a collection of essays than a systematic treatment, which means it circles back to the same ideas from different angles rather than building a linear argument. Some readers will find this meditative; others will find it frustrating.

And Stone's critique of the yoga industry, while accurate, is familiar by now. The commodification of yoga, the loss of the tradition's ethical foundations, the spiritual bypassing — these complaints have been standard in yoga criticism for years. Stone doesn't add much new to this analysis; he just states it with more intellectual rigor than most.
