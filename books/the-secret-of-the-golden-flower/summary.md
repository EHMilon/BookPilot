Thomas Cleary

A book that begins with a central meditation practice described as "turning the light around" — and that simple phrase, once you understand it, changes everything. **The Secret of the Golden Flower** is one of the most important texts of Chinese internal alchemy (*neidan*), the esoteric Daoist tradition that sought not just wisdom but literal transformation of the body and mind. Discovered in the West through Richard Wilhelm's 1929 translation (with a famous psychological commentary by Carl Jung), this edition by Thomas Cleary restores the text's original Daoist context, stripping away the Jungian overlay and presenting the work as it was meant to be understood: as a practical manual for transforming the energy-body into an immortal vehicle.

The text was written during the Qing dynasty (17th-18th century), though it claims to transmit teachings from the much earlier School of Complete Reality (*Quanzhen*), founded by Lü Dongbin and Wang Chongyang. It's a synthesis of Daoist alchemy, Chan (Zen) Buddhism, and Neo-Confucian philosophy — exactly the kind of syncretic work that makes purists in any single tradition uncomfortable but that practitioners found deeply useful. The "Golden Flower" is the *elixir of life*, the perfected energy-body that results from successful alchemical practice. The "secret" is the method for producing it.

The core practice is deceptively simple. You sit in meditation and instead of focusing your attention outward (on the world, on problems, on other people) or even inward on your thoughts, you "turn the light around" — you direct your awareness back to its own source. You look for the place from which looking arises. Cleary translates it as "reversing the light" or "turning the radiance around." The paradoxical effect is that when you stop chasing thoughts and instead rest in the *awareness behind them*, something shifts in the body. The energy (*qi*) that was dispersed through attention to external things begins to gather. The "golden flower" — a luminous center of transformed energy — begins to form.

The beginner reads "golden flower" and pictures a glowing lotus blooming in the chest. Then they spend months trying to *make* themselves see a golden flower, which completely misses the point. The golden flower is a *metaphor* for the result of practice, not a visualization target. You don't try to see light. You turn your attention inward with the right quality of awareness, and the light — a subtle luminosity experienced in meditation — arises naturally. The trap is turning the symbol into the goal. The secret isn't the flower. The secret is the *turning*.

The text is built around a series of aphorisms and instructions. Its nine chapters alternate between practical meditation guidance and theoretical explanations of the alchemical process. The theory is rooted in the *I Ching* and the system of the Five Elements (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) and the Eight Trigrams. The practice involves circulating energy through the microcosmic orbit (the circuit of energy up the spine and down the front of the body), reversing the normal flow of generative energy, and refining the three treasures: *jing* (essence), *qi* (vital energy), and *shen* (spirit). The goal is to transform *jing* into *qi*, *qi* into *shen*, and *shen* into emptiness — the state of pure, undifferentiated awareness called the "Tao."

The most famous technique associated with this text is the circulation of light through the two main energy channels (the Governing Vessel up the spine and the Conception Vessel down the front). Modern qigong teachers love this technique, and it's genuinely powerful. But the text itself warns repeatedly: don't get attached to the circulation. Don't try to *feel* the energy moving. Don't guide it with your attention. The practice is to *stop doing* and let the circulation happen on its own. The trap is turning a subtle, body-based teaching into a mental manipulation — you end up straining and steering instead of settling and allowing.

The most famous section — the one that caught Jung's attention — describes the process of "observing the thoughts within the space of the mind." The text says: "When thoughts arise, you should not follow them. Nor should you suppress them. Simply observe them. Watch them appear and disappear. Like a mirror reflecting images, the mind remains clear and empty while thoughts pass through it." This is essentially the same instruction you'll find in Dzogchen Buddhism, in Zen, and in the Christian contemplative tradition. The text's distinctive contribution is the *energetic* dimension: it's not just about mental awareness but about the *subtle body*. When you observe thoughts without engaging them, the energy that was feeding the thoughts returns to its source. This gathered energy is the raw material of the golden flower.

Cleary's translation is deliberately spare. Where Wilhelm's version (translated from a German translation of the Chinese) is often poetic and interpretive, Cleary's is clinical and precise. He includes extensive notes on the alchemical terminology, explaining what each Chinese character means and how it was used in the tradition. This makes his edition more accurate and more difficult — you feel like you're reading a technical manual for spiritual surgery rather than a mystical poem.

One of the most fascinating aspects of the text is its treatment of the dream state. The Secret of the Golden Flower teaches that the boundary between waking and dreaming is one of the great training grounds of the spiritual path. When you're awake, your attention is pulled outward by the senses. When you're dreaming, your mind is active but your body is still, and the energy that would normally be spent on physical activity goes toward the dream-content. The advanced practitioner learns to maintain awareness *during* dreaming — the famous practice of lucid dreaming, which Daoists called "the spirit roaming while the body sleeps." The text says: "If in the midst of dreams you can maintain awareness, your spirit is not scattered." This is not a side curiosity. It's central to the alchemical project: if you can hold the light even when the conscious mind has let go of control, then the transformation can continue twenty-four hours a day.

The idea I couldn't shake is the concept of "turning the light around." It's a simple gesture — redirecting attention from the objects of consciousness to the *source* of consciousness — that I'd encountered in different forms (as "self-inquiry" in Advaita, as "looking for the watcher" in various meditative traditions). But the Golden Flower text adds something unique: it tells you *why* this simple act works. When you turn the light around, you're not just understanding something intellectually. You're literally reversing the flow of energy in your body. The energy that was leaking out through your senses and your thoughts is being conserved and transformed. This is an embodied understanding that pure intellectual philosophy can't give you.

The text is opaque to the point of uselessness without a teacher. The alchemical terminology — "the lead of the water of the north," "the fire of the wood of the south," "the union of the dragon and the tiger," "the marriage of water and fire" — is arcane and deliberately confusing. This was a safety feature (esoteric texts were not meant to be understood by outsiders) but it makes the book frustrating for modern readers. Cleary's notes help, but they can't bridge the gap entirely. The text also assumes a cosmology (a universe of subtle energies, correspondences between body and cosmos, literal spiritual immortality) that most modern readers simply don't share. You have to suspend a lot of disbelief to practice according to this text.
