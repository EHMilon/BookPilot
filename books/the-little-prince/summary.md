A pilot crashes in the Sahara desert. Stranded, dying of thirst, he meets a small boy who has fallen from a distant asteroid and needs help drawing a sheep. This is how the most translated book in the French language begins — not as a children's story, but as a meditation on loneliness, love, and the things adults forget when they "grow up." Antoine de Saint-Exupéry was an aviator and poet who wrote *The Little Prince* in 1943, during the darkest days of World War II, as an exile in New York, pining for his occupied France and for the wife he couldn't reach. He would disappear on a reconnaissance flight a year later, never to return. The book he left behind looks like a simple fable. It is, in fact, a blueprint for how to love without possessing, see without your eyes, and hold onto what matters in a world that wants you to count numbers instead.

The narrator opens by explaining that when he was six, he drew a boa constrictor digesting an elephant. Adults couldn't see it. They saw a hat. They told him to stop drawing and study geography and arithmetic instead. So he became a pilot, living among people who could only see the surface of things, and learned to judge them by what they could count.

> *"All grown-ups were once children — but few of them remember it."*

This is the book's thesis, stated before the story even begins. Growing up, as Saint-Exupéry sees it, is not a process of gaining wisdom. It's a process of losing imagination, narrowing your attention to measurable things, and forgetting how to see what's right in front of you. The adults in the book are all, without exception, absurd in their single-mindedness.

The Little Prince visits six neighboring asteroids on his journey, each occupied by a single adult. The **king** commands the stars but can only order them to do what they'd do anyway. The **conceited man** wants nothing but applause. The **drunkard** drinks to forget that he drinks. The **businessman** counts stars he claims to own but can do nothing with. The **lamplighter** follows orders so mechanically that he lights and extinguishes his lamp every minute because the planet rotates faster and faster, and he can't change the rules. The **geographer** writes about oceans and mountains but has never left his desk.

Every single one is trapped, and every single one is *certain* of their importance. They are adults who have taken one aspect of life — power, vanity, addiction, ownership, duty, knowledge — and mistaken it for the whole.

> *"It is much more difficult to judge oneself than to judge others. If you succeed in judging yourself rightly, then you are indeed a man of true wisdom."*

You can read these as charming caricatures of silly adults. But each one is a mirror. The king is anyone who mistakes authority for understanding. The businessman is anyone who thinks they own what they can count. The lamplighter is anyone who follows rules without asking whether they still make sense. The book asks quietly: which asteroid do you live on?

The Little Prince lands on Earth, walks through a garden of five thousand roses — all identical to his single rose back home — and is devastated. He thought his rose was unique in the universe. It isn't. Then the fox appears, and the fox changes everything.

> *"And now here is my secret, a very simple secret: It is only with the heart that one can see rightly; what is essential is invisible to the eye."*

The fox asks to be **tamed** — a word Saint-Exupéry lifts from ordinary French (*apprivoiser*) and fills with extraordinary weight. To tame means to create bonds. To be unique, not because you are biologically or statistically unrepeatable, but because someone has chosen you and you have chosen them. The five thousand roses are not the Little Prince's rose because he has not spent time with them. His rose is unique because he *cares* for her, waters her, protects her, and loves her. Uniqueness is not a fact you discover. It's a bond you create.

This is the philosophical heart of the book. Saint-Exupéry is not a naive idealist. He knows that his rose is not literally the only rose in the universe. But he also knows that the word "unique" has two meanings — the statistical and the relational — and we have been trained to value only the first. The fox, a wild animal, teaches that relationship is what gives life meaning. Without bonds, time is just time. With bonds, time becomes *ritual* — anticipation, presence, memory.

The fox's lessons are a miniature ethics of love. The ritual of coming at the same time each day so that the Little Prince can be anticipated. The patience of sitting at a distance, slowly drawing closer, so that trust can build. The commitment to responsibility: "You become responsible, forever, for what you have tamed."

> *"What makes the desert beautiful is that somewhere it hides a well."*

The fox knows that the value of something is not intrinsic but relational. The wheat fields that were meaningless to him before will now remind him of the Little Prince's golden hair. He has been given something the grown-ups on the asteroids don't have: a reason to see the world differently.

The Little Prince finds a well in the desert — a miracle — and drinks. He tells the narrator he must go back to his rose. He allows a snake to bite him, not as death but as a return. The book ends with the narrator looking at the stars and hearing bells, because somewhere on one of them, the Little Prince is laughing. The final pages are an instruction: if you find yourself in the desert, at the place where the Little Prince appeared, please let the narrator know, quickly.

Let's be direct: *The Little Prince* is beloved partly because it confirms what people already want to believe — that they're deep, that they see with their hearts, that they're not like the silly grown-ups. The line between genuine wisdom and comforting sentiment is thin here, and the book crosses it often. The criticism of adulthood is broad enough to be unhelpful: yes, many adults are narrow and obsessed with numbers, but many are also genuinely wise, and some children are petty and cruel. The binary is too clean. The prose, in translation, can feel stilted, and the watercolors — while charming — are not technically accomplished. The book's structure is episodic to the point of randomness; it works as a series of vignettes but stumbles as a narrative. And for all its talk of love and uniqueness, the Little Prince's love for his rose is described entirely in terms of her fragility and neediness — she is vain, demanding, and dependent. Is that really a healthy model of love, or is it a romanticized version of caretaking that can slide into enabling?

Anyone who has ever felt that adulthood has made them smaller. Anyone in a long-term relationship who wants to remember what taming meant. Children — actual children — who are old enough to think about death. And adults who have forgotten that they were once children. Skip it if you need plot, complexity, or a story that doesn't stop to philosophize every few pages.

The essential things in life — love, meaning, beauty, connection — cannot be measured, counted, or observed from the outside. They are created through relationship, attention, and choice. To "tame" is to bind yourself to another being and accept the responsibility that comes with it. Growing up is not a fall from innocence but a narrowing of vision — and you can choose to expand it again.

The fox's lesson about ritual has stayed with me more than any other. "Come back at the same hour each day," he says, so that the anticipation — the growing nearness of the event — becomes part of the joy. I started applying this to small things: a weekly call, a morning coffee, a walk at dusk. Making them *regular* didn't make them routine. It made them ceremonies. The fox was right: the time of approach is part of the experience.

1. **Choose one relationship and decide how you will make it unique.** The fox says uniqueness is created, not discovered. Pick someone you care about and do one small thing only you do for them — a specific greeting, a shared ritual, a particular way of paying attention. That's taming.

2. **Identify one "asteroid" you live on.** Are you the businessman, counting things that don't matter? The lamplighter, obeying a rule that stopped making sense? The geographer, knowing about things you've never experienced? Name it. That's the asteroid you need to leave.

3. **Tonight, look at the stars and think about someone you love who isn't with you.** Saint-Exupéry says the stars will be different when you know someone is laughing on one of them. Try it. See if the sky changes.
