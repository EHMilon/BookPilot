Imagine going through your entire life asleep. Not physically — your eyes are open, you go to work, you fall in love, you raise children, you achieve things. But spiritually, psychologically, you are walking through a dream, reacting to shadows, believing the projections of your own mind. That is how Anthony de Mello, a Jesuit priest and psychotherapist from India, saw most of humanity. And his book *Awareness*, compiled from his retreat talks after his death, is a relentless, loving, often hilarious assault on everything you think is real. De Mello doesn't want to improve your life. He wants to wake you up. And he warns you upfront: you will not like it. Waking up means losing your attachments, your identities, your certainties — everything you've built your sense of self upon.

De Mello's central claim is that human beings are not conscious. We think we are, because we can solve problems and hold conversations. But real consciousness, what he simply calls **Awareness**, is something far rarer. It's the ability to see things exactly as they are, without the filter of conditioning, without the commentary of the ego, without the desperate need for things to be a certain way. Most people, de Mello argues, are living on autopilot. They have been programmed — by their culture, their religion, their family, their education — to respond in predictable ways. They think they're choosing, but they're just playing out scripts. And the proof is how predictable they are: insult them, and they get angry. Praise them, and they get puffed up. Threaten their beliefs, and they get defensive. That's not freedom. That's a machine.

The trap is that you don't know you're trapped. That's the nature of the condition. De Mello calls the programmed self **The False Self** — the collection of labels, beliefs, roles, and memories that you mistake for who you are. "I am a Christian," "I am a liberal," "I am a successful person," "I am a victim" — all of these are costumes. They are not you. They are identities you picked up along the way and started defending as if your life depended on them. **The False Self** is terrified of being seen through, because its existence is contingent on your belief in it. When someone challenges your identity, you feel threatened not because the identity is true, but because the ego knows its own fragility. De Mello's teaching is like a surgeon's scalpel: he doesn't want to strengthen your **False Self**. He wants you to see through it so completely that it dissolves.

The method is deceptively simple, and de Mello calls it **Waking Up**. It doesn't require a guru, a monastery, or a decade of practice. It requires one thing: paying attention. Not the kind of attention you give to a task, but the kind of attention you give to your own mind in operation. Watch yourself throughout the day. Notice when you react. Notice the stories you tell yourself. Notice how desperately you want people to approve of you. Notice how you cling to your opinions as if they were your children. De Mello says that the simple act of watching, without judgment, without trying to change anything, is itself the awakening. You don't have to fix your reactions. You just have to see them. And in the seeing, something shifts. The mechanical quality of your mind becomes obvious. And what was automatic becomes available for choice.

One of de Mello's most powerful tools is his use of stories. Drawing from Sufi, Hindu, Buddhist, and Christian traditions, he tells parables that bypass the intellect and land directly in the heart. There's the story of the man who crossed a river and hung onto his boat even after reaching the other shore. De Mello asks: why would you carry a boat on your head once you're on dry land? Your past experiences, your achievements, your traumas — they were boats that got you across a river. But you're still carrying them. **The False Self** is built from boats you refused to leave behind. Another story: a dog sat on a nail and howled in pain. Another dog came by, saw the howling, and copied it without ever sitting on a nail. That second dog, de Mello says, is most of humanity — mimicking the suffering of others without understanding the source. We inherit anxieties, prejudices, and fears that were never ours to begin with. We carry them as if they were real. **Waking Up** means examining every inherited pain and asking: did I sit on this nail, or am I just howling because everyone else is?

The deepest layer of the book confronts **Attachment**. De Mello doesn't mean only the obvious attachments — to people, possessions, status. He means attachment to opinions, to beliefs, to images of yourself, to your version of how the world should be. He argues that **Attachment** is the root of all suffering, and he is unsparing in his examples. If you cannot hear your political views criticized without getting agitated, you are attached. If you need someone to love you back in order to feel okay, you are attached. If you have a fixed idea of what a "good person" does and you judge yourself or others against it, you are attached. The goal is not to become indifferent. It's to become free. A person free from attachment can love more deeply, act more effectively, and enjoy life without the constant undercurrent of anxiety because their happiness is not dependent on conditions. They don't need the world to behave a certain way. They are content because they are awake, not because everything is going their way.

The paradox de Mello presents is confronting: most of what you call love is actually need. You say "I love you," but what you mean is "I need you to complete me, to make me feel secure, to validate my existence." Real love, he says, can only exist between two free people — people who don't need each other but choose each other. The friction here is intense. Everything in you rebels against this. You want to believe that your attachments are proof of your depth, your loyalty, your humanity. De Mello is saying the opposite: your attachments are proof of your sleep. The mother who cannot let her child live independently, the spouse who cannot imagine life without their partner, the person who cannot change their mind about a belief — all of them are in the grip of **Attachment**, mistaking need for love and fear for commitment.
