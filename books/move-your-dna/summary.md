Katy Bowman is a biomechanist who thinks about movement the way a nutritionist thinks about food — not as something you do for 30 minutes a day, but as a nutrient that you're either getting enough of or you're not. *Move Your DNA* is her argument that the modern human movement diet is as catastrophically restricted as the modern human food diet, and the health consequences are just as severe. We know that eating a narrow range of processed foods makes us sick. Bowman's argument is that a narrow range of processed movements does the same thing — and the solution isn't more "exercise" (which is just one type of movement), but a complete restructuring of how we move throughout the day.

The foundational concept is **nutritious movement** — movement that provides the mechanical loads your body evolved to handle. Bowman argues that your cells don't know the difference between movement dose and nutrient dose. Mechanical forces (compression, tension, shear, torsion) are signals that cells — especially bone cells, cartilage cells, and muscle cells — require for normal function. Without these signals, cells atrophy, misbehave, and contribute to the chronic diseases we attribute to aging. "Movement is not a bonus feature of human biology," Bowman writes. "It is the operating system." The trap is that we think of movement as "exercise" — a discrete activity done for a specific purpose — and the rest of our time as "not moving." But the body responds to total movement load, not workout minutes. Sitting in a chair for 10 hours and then running for 30 minutes is not movement-rich. It's movement-punctuated sedentarism.

The book introduces **mechanical loads** — the specific forces that different tissues need. Bone needs compressive and tensile loads. Cartilage needs cyclic compression and release (which is why kneeling and squatting are important for knee health — they squeeze and release the meniscus, bringing in nutrients). Fascia needs torsion and shear (rotation and sliding). Blood vessels need the pumping action of muscle contraction. Lymphatic vessels need compression from movement to pump fluid — they have no central pump of their own. The trap is that most modern exercise modalities focus on a narrow range of loads: linear, forward-facing, at moderate or high speeds, in supportive shoes. We run, bike, elliptical, and walk on flat surfaces. We almost never squat deeply (unless we're lifting weights), kneel, crawl, hang, twist, balance on uneven surfaces, or carry loads asymmetrically. Each missing load type corresponds to a specific tissue that's under-utilized and under-resourced.

Bowman's most practical tool is the **Movement Multivitamin** — a daily practice that covers the full spectrum of missing loads. This includes: deep squatting (for hip and ankle mobility, pelvic floor health, and spinal decompression), hanging from a bar (for shoulder health, grip strength, and spinal decompression), crawling (for coordination, shoulder stability, and hip mobility), walking on uneven terrain (for foot strength, ankle stability, and proprioception), and carrying loads (for core strength, bone density, and gait mechanics). The Multivitamin is not a "workout" — it's about 10-15 minutes of diverse movement. Bowman's argument is that the Multivitamin doesn't replace your existing exercise. It fills in the gaps. Most people who run or lift weights are still missing 80% of the movement spectrum.

"Your shoes are a cast for your feet," Bowman writes, and this is one of the book's most memorable images. She argues that modern shoes (especially those with arch support, raised heels, and cushioned soles) immobilize the foot, preventing the 26 bones, 33 joints, and 100+ muscles, tendons, and ligaments from doing their job. The result: weak feet, flat arches, plantar fasciitis, and altered gait mechanics that propagate up through the knees, hips, and spine. The trap is that we treat foot pain with more support — orthotics, stability shoes, motion control — which further atrophies the foot muscles. Bowman recommends transitioning gradually to minimalist footwear (shoes with thin soles and wide toe boxes) and spending time barefoot on varied surfaces. The "gradually" is critical: going from cushioned shoes to barefoot running overnight is a recipe for injury.

The book extends the movement diet concept to **movement across the lifespan**. Bowman argues that children's movement deficits are even more severe than adults'. Children who spend their days in chairs, carrying backpacks, and playing on screens are not developing the movement foundations they'll need as adults. The reduction in childhood movement diversity — fewer trees to climb, less rough-and-tumble play, less walking to school — is creating adults with lower bone density, weaker connective tissue, and poorer coordination. Bowman doesn't blame parents; she blames the environment. Schools are designed for sitting. Playgrounds are designed for safety (which means low movement diversity). Streets are designed for cars, not children. The trap is that parents think "my kid is active" because they have soccer practice twice a week, while the child spends 40 hours sitting in school and at home. The movement deficit accumulates.

"I'm not asking you to do more. I'm asking you to do different," Bowman says. The book addresses the psychological barrier that most people face: the belief that more movement means more time and effort. Bowman reframes this as **movement integration** — making existing movements more nutritious rather than adding new blocks of exercise. Stand while folding laundry instead of sitting. Squat while picking up toys instead of bending. Walk to the store instead of driving. Carry groceries in a single bag instead of dividing the weight evenly. These micro-changes, accumulated over a day, provide the mechanical diversity that cells need. The trap is that we optimize for convenience (ergonomic chairs, kitchen stools, automatic everything) and then wonder why our bodies hurt. Every convenience that eliminates a movement is also eliminating a mechanical signal.

The honest criticism of *Move Your DNA* is that it's more diagnosis than prescription. Bowman is brilliant at identifying what's wrong with modern movement patterns, but the solutions she offers — while theoretically sound — are difficult to implement in a world built for sitting. You can't easily squat at work if your workplace has standard-height toilets and desks. You can't walk barefoot if your commute involves hot pavement and public spaces. The Movement Multivitamin is practical, but most people won't do it consistently because it requires conscious effort against a tidal wave of environmental cues to sit still. There's also a boundary issue: Bowman's "movement is a nutrient" analogy is powerful, but it can lead to a kind of movement orthorexia — the anxiety that you're not moving "right" or "enough." She acknowledges this but doesn't fully address it. And the book can be frustratingly abstract—Bowman provides the philosophy of nutritious movement in rich detail, but a reader looking for a structured daily routine has to piece it together from scattered examples rather than following a clear program.

Spend 10 minutes barefoot on a variety of surfaces in your home — carpet, tile, hardwood. Don't do anything specific. Just stand, shift weight, walk slowly. Feel your feet gripping each surface. If you wear shoes with arch support, this will feel strange. That's the point. Your foot intrinsic muscles are waking up.

Try to hold a deep squat (feet flat, heels down, hips below knees) for 60 seconds. If you can't, that's data. The inability to deep squat is a modern phenomenon — virtually all pre-industrial humans could. Hold onto a doorframe if needed. Practice 3 deep squats per day for 60 seconds each. This one movement improves hip mobility, spinal decompression, and ankle range of motion.

If you have access to a pull-up bar, hang from it for 20 seconds. Dead hang, no kipping, shoulder blades engaged but arms straight. If you can't hold 20 seconds, work up to it. Hanging decompresses the spine (counteracting sitting compression), strengthens grip, and mobilizes the shoulders. Bowman recommends 2-3 short hangs per day.

Walk for 10 minutes without headphones or distractions. Pay attention to your feet. Are you landing on your heel or midfoot? Is your foot turning out? Are you walking softly or slapping the ground? Most people are completely unaware of how they walk. Just noticing your gait pattern is the first step toward restoring natural movement. Bowman's ideal: midfoot strike, feet pointing forward, soft step, arms swinging naturally, spine tall.
