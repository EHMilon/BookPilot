I first read *Strength in What Remains* on a long train ride, and I made the mistake of reaching the Burundi sections in a crowded carriage. By page sixty I was crying — not the quiet, dignified kind but the kind you have to turn away from strangers to hide. The man next to me pretended not to notice. I'm grateful for that. There is no good way to explain why a book about a Burundian medical student's escape from genocide is making you weep in a Bangladeshi train compartment.

The book follows Deogratias Niyizonkiza — Deo — from the killing fields of Burundi to the streets of New York to his eventual return home. Tracy Kidder, who also wrote *Mountains Beyond Mountains: The Life of Dr. Paul Farmer, the Man Who Would Heal the World*, spent years with Deo, reconstructing a story that Deo himself could barely tell. The result is a book that refuses to be what it looks like: not an inspirational tale of overcoming adversity, but an autopsy of what survival actually costs.

---

Deo was a third-year medical student in Burundi when the genocide in neighboring Rwanda spilled over the border in 1993. He was Hutu. He had been treating Tutsi patients — which, in the madness of ethnic violence, made him a traitor to his own people. Hutu militias began hunting him.

Kidder reconstructs Deo's flight with a journalist's precision: the hiding in latrines, the bodies in the road, the moment when he watched friends being hacked to death with machetes. At one point, captured and marched toward what he believed was execution, Deo prayed a prayer I cannot forget: *"God, if you let me survive this, I will serve you."*

He survived. Not because of divine intervention but because of a series of infinitely fragile chances. A guard looked away. A truck broke down. A stranger shared food. Deo credits God; Kidder, more quietly, credits luck. Either way, the margin was inches.

*He had witnessed cruelty that was so far beyond normal, it seemed to belong to a different species of life, and yet it had been committed by human beings, by people who had names and families and who, on most other days, were probably kind to their children.*

This sentence — which I went back to read three times — is the book's thesis in miniature. The killers were not monsters. They were farmers, teachers, neighbors, armed with machetes and the permission of authority. Deo survived because some of those neighbors *chose* to help him, at enormous risk to themselves. And he almost died because other neighbors *chose* to hunt him. The same soil, the same people, the same week. The line between civilization and savagery is not a line. It's a breath.

---

Deo made it to New York with two hundred dollars, no English, and no plan. He slept in Central Park. He got a job delivering groceries, then learned the city as a bicycle messenger. He navigated immigration bureaucracy, homelessness, and the casual indifference of a city that sees a thousand desperate people a day and helps none of them.

What saves Deo — and this is the part that feels almost like a different genre — is not a system. It's individual people. A stranger named Sharon lets him sleep on her couch for months. A psychiatrist, Dr. Lisa, takes him as a patient for free. A priest, Father Ron, helps him find housing and community. An immigration lawyer works his case pro bono.

Kidder never makes this explicit, but his argument is quietly radical: **kindness is not a system**. No institution saved Deo. Individuals did. People who saw a traumatized young man and decided, in a series of small, unheroic choices, to help. The help was often imperfect — Sharon's couch came with tension, Dr. Lisa's patience had limits — but it was *real*. It was the difference between death and life.

*When you have nothing, you have nothing to be taken from you. This is a kind of strength.*

I wrestled with this section. I wanted the book to argue for better systems — better refugee support, better mental health care, better immigration policy. Kidder gives you those implications without stating them. But the book's emotional weight is on the *personal*. The message is uncomfortable: systems are important, yes, but in the end, people save people. And if you're waiting for the system to save the next Deo, you may be waiting too long.

---

Deo eventually gets into Columbia Medical School. The acceptance scene — reading the letter in a dingy apartment, alone, after years of work — should be triumphant. Kidder doesn't let it be. He has already shown you the cost: the survivor's guilt, the nightmares, the permanent sense of being alien in every room.

After graduating, Deo returns to Burundi. He builds a health clinic in the village that once turned him away. He treats patients, trains staff, works tirelessly. But the return is not a happy ending. He finds that he can't go home — not really. He is too American for Burundi and too Burundian for America. He is healed enough to function but not healed enough to forget.

*I thought if I went back, everything would be okay. But I found that the person who left was not the same person who returned.*

This is the book's deepest and most honest insight. Trauma does not resolve. It transforms. Deo is not cured. He has learned to live with what happened, but he still visits the places where it happened, still feels the fear, still carries the faces of the people he lost. The clinic is his way of making meaning from horror — but meaning is not healing. It's just meaning.

---

Kidder's presence in the story is sometimes intrusive. He includes himself more than some readers will want — his reactions, his questions, his admiration for Deo. It's the mark of a journalist who became a friend, which is both a strength and a limitation. The admiration is so transparent that the book occasionally reads like hagiography.

More troubling: Kidder is a white American journalist telling an African man's story. He is careful, respectful, and generous. But the power dynamics are present on every page. Who gets to frame the narrative? Who decides which details are important? Kidder does his best, but the best is still a white man writing a Black man's trauma for a largely white readership. The book would have been stronger if he had addressed this directly. There's a moment late in the book where Kidder asks Deo what he thinks about the project, and Deo's answer — careful, diplomatic, slightly evasive — is one of the most honest things in the entire narrative. Deo knows the story isn't fully his.

The second half also loses narrative tension. The Burundi sections are gripping because Deo's life is at stake. The New York sections are compelling because the stakes are still high — homelessness, deportation, madness. But once Deo is in medical school, the story becomes a series of incremental achievements. Important, yes. Dramatic, no. And the clinic he builds in Burundi, while genuinely noble, arrives in the narrative as a conclusion that was always inevitable. The book tells us early that Deo will go back and build something. The surprise is that the going back hurts more than the building helps.

---

I don't think about Deo's survival when I think about this book. I think about what survival *looks like* — the people who carried him, the small acts of decency that made the difference between life and death. I think about Sharon's couch. I think about Dr. Lisa's free sessions. I think about the guard who looked away.

And I think about the sentence that haunts me: *"He had witnessed cruelty that was so far beyond normal, it seemed to belong to a different species of life, and yet it had been committed by human beings."* There is no comfort in that sentence. But there is clarity. If ordinary people can do that, then ordinary people can also choose differently. The book doesn't say this. It shows it, in the form of a man who survived because enough ordinary people made the choice to be kind. That's not a solution. It's an invitation to be one of the helpers.

*A man steps off a plane at JFK with two hundred dollars, no English, no contacts, and the memory of hacking bodies with a machete still fresh in his muscles. Eighteen years later, he graduates from Columbia Medical School.*

That's the story. But the book is not about the eighteen years. It's about the debt that survival never stops collecting.

---

I think about Deo often. Not as a symbol — the refugee who made it — but as a specific person who carried specific wounds and still chose to build a clinic instead of a wall. That choice is the book's real argument. Not that trauma makes you strong. That trauma makes you a choice, every day, about what kind of person you will become. Deo chose to become a doctor. He could have chosen to become something else. The strength in what remains is the strength to choose again.
