A man dreams he is a butterfly, and when he wakes, the question is not "was it real?" but "which one is dreaming now?" Most translations of this famous passage make it sound profound and distant — a philosophical puzzle from an ancient China that feels like a different planet. Brook Ziporyn's translation makes it sound like a joke you're still laughing at hours later, not because it was particularly funny, but because you realize the joke is on you.

Ziporyn's *Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings* (Hackett, 2009) is, along with Burton Watson's classic version, one of the two essential English translations of the Zhuangzi. But where Watson aimed for clarity and accessibility — a "Zhuangzi that reads like a Western book" — Ziporyn aims for something more ambitious: a Zhuangzi that reads like *Zhuangzi*. That means preserving the jarring transitions, the sudden shifts in register, the wild inventiveness of the original's language. Ziporyn's Zhuangzi is not a calm wise man dispensing timeless truths. He's a comedian, a logician, a madman, and a mystic having a conversation with himself and sometimes letting you listen.

> "Now I don't know: is I, then, the 'I' that was dreaming the butterfly? Or is I the butterfly now dreaming that it is I? Between I and the butterfly there must be a difference. This is called the Transformation of Things."

Ziporyn's translation of the butterfly dream is typical of his approach. Where others render "the transformation of things" (*wu hua*, 物化) as a metaphysical observation, Ziporyn's reading makes it active, unsettling, necessary. The point is not that *things transform* (a bland truism) but that *you transform* — that the boundary between the self that dreams and the self that wakes is not fixed. You are not the same person who started reading this sentence.

The core of Ziporyn's approach is his fresh translation of key Daoist terms. He translates *Tian* (天) not as "Heaven" — with its Western connotations of an afterlife or divine judgment — but as "the Heavenly" — a distinction that matters because it shifts the term from a place to a quality, from a destination to a way of being. He translates *De* (德) not as "virtue" (too moral, too Confucian) but as "inner power" or "potency." He translates *Dao* (道) not as "the Way" (capital letters, reverence) but as "the Course" — active, directional, something you follow rather than something you contemplate.

> "The Course has no boundaries; the spoken has no constancy. But because there is 'that which is deemed right,' boundaries arise."

Ziporyn's translation is the most philosophically alert version of the Zhuangzi available. He studied under some of the greatest Chinese philosophers of the 20th century and is himself a philosopher — not just a scholar who knows Chinese. His translation choices are not about accuracy in a narrow sense (though they are accurate). They are about *continuing the argument* — making the Zhuangzi speak to contemporary philosophical questions about identity, relativism, skepticism, and the nature of language.

The Zhuangzi is too rich for any single interpretation. Zen practitioners claim it. Western environmentalists claim it. Postmodern philosophers find deconstruction in it. Anarchists find political theory. None of them are entirely wrong, and none of them are entirely right. The book resists ownership. Every reading that fixes it into a system has missed the point.

Ziporyn selects the seven "Inner Chapters" (generally accepted as the work of the historical Zhuangzi himself) plus selections from the Outer and Miscellaneous chapters. This is the standard structure, but Ziporyn's footnotes and commentary are extraordinary. He doesn't just explain what a passage means — he shows you *how it works*, what moves it makes, why it's funny or unsettling or both.

The famous Cook Ding passage gets one of Ziporyn's best treatments. Cook Ding is carving an ox for his lord, moving with the grace of a dancer. The lord asks how he got so skilled. Cook Ding replies: "What I care about is the Course, which goes beyond mere skill." He explains that he doesn't *see* the ox anymore — he *senses* its structure, its natural divisions, the spaces between joints. A good cook changes his knife every year because he cuts. An ordinary cook changes his knife every month because he hacks. Cook Ding has used the same knife for nineteen years because he finds the spaces.

> "There are spaces between the joints, and the blade of the knife has no thickness at all. When you put something that has no thickness into something that has spaces between, there is plenty of room — more than enough for the blade to wander around."

Ziporyn's word choice — "wander around" — captures the Zhuangzi's playfulness. The blade doesn't *cut*. It wanders. Skill so refined that it looks like aimlessness, purpose so aligned with reality that it feels like play. This is *wu wei* (無為) — effortless action — not as laziness or passivity but as the result of total attunement. Cook Ding has been doing this for nineteen years. The effortlessness is earned.

Pop spirituality reads "go with the flow" as "do nothing." Ziporyn's translation won't let you. Cook Ding spent nineteen years learning to carve an ox. The flow is not a starting point. It's an arrival. You don't choose effortless action — you practice so deeply that effort disappears.

One of Ziporyn's most valuable contributions is his reading of the Zhuangzi's relativism. The book is famous for saying that everything is relative — big and small, life and death, dreaming and waking — and this is often taken as a kind of philosophical nihilism: nothing matters, so relax. Ziporyn argues that this is a misreading. Zhuangzi is not saying nothing matters. He's saying that *no single perspective* has a monopoly on truth, and that wisdom consists of the capacity to shift between perspectives without being trapped by any one.

The passage most relevant here is the famous "piping of Heaven" or "the three pipings" — the wind blowing through holes in the ground, each hole making a different sound, each sound expressing the same wind differently. Human opinions are the same: each emerges from a different shape, a different perspective, a different "hole." The sage doesn't argue that their hole is the right one. They hear all the sounds as expressions of the same wind.

> "Blowing out into the ten thousand hollows, the sounds of all kinds come forth. Do you know what makes them this way? Great equality is brought forth when you harmonize them all."

Ziporyn's translation of the chapter "Discussion on Making All Things Equal" (*Qiwulun*, 齊物論) is the heart of the book. He renders the title as "The Equality of All Things and the Leveling of Perspectives" — which is more accurate and also more awkward, but it captures the double movement: things *are* equal in some sense, but you also have to *make* them equal by leveling your attachment to any particular perspective.

The most common misreading of Zhuangzi is that "all perspectives are equal" means no perspective matters. Ziporyn shows that Zhuangzi is more subtle. The point is not that all perspectives are *true* (which leads to lazy relativism) but that no perspective is *complete* (which leads to intellectual humility). You can hold a position while knowing it's partial. You can act decisively while knowing your view is limited. This is harder than either dogmatism (my view is complete) or nihilism (no view matters).

The passage on death is one of the most radical in the book. When Zhuangzi's wife dies, his friend finds him banging on a drum and singing. The friend is horrified. Zhuangzi explains: "When she died, I was sad like anyone. But I looked back to her beginning — before she was born, before she had a body, before she had a spirit. In the formless, a change happened and she got a spirit. Another change and she got a body. Another change and she was born. Now another change and she's dead. It's like the progression of the four seasons. She is sleeping peacefully in the great house of the universe. If I went on weeping and wailing, it would mean I don't understand destiny. So I stopped."

Ziporyn's translation highlights that Zhuangzi *did* grieve initially. He doesn't say "don't feel sadness." He says sadness comes from a particular perspective — the perspective that sees death as an ending. From the perspective of the Course, death is a transformation, like the changing of seasons. The emotion is not suppressed. It's *transformed* by understanding.

> "Life and death are connected as one. They are just different phases of the same process. Why treat one as good and the other as bad? The true person of ancient times knew nothing of loving life or hating death."

The book includes the famous debate about the joy of fish — Zhuangzi and his friend Huizi standing on a bridge. Zhuangzi says the fish are happy. Huizi, the logician, says: "You are not a fish. How do you know?" Zhuangzi replies: "You are not me. How do you know I don't know?" Huizi: "I'm not you, so I don't know what you know. But you're not a fish, so you don't know what fish feel." Zhuangzi: "Let's go back to your original question — you asked *how* I know, which implies you already knew that I know. I know it from standing here on the bridge."

Ziporyn's commentary here is particularly good. He shows that the debate is not just a clever word game. It's about the limits of logic. Huizi's position is logically airtight: you can't know the experience of another being. But Zhuangzi is making a different point: direct participation gives a kind of knowledge that logic can't reach. He knows the fish are happy because he's *there*, sharing the moment, attuned to the same river. The knowledge is not propositional. It's experiential.

The idea I couldn't shake is Ziporyn's reading of the "Transformation of Things" — that the boundary between you and not-you is not fixed. Not just the butterfly dream, but the whole book: your body is a collection of borrowed elements, your thoughts are responses to a world you didn't create, your self is a temporary arrangement. This is not, in Zhuangzi, a cause for existential panic. It's a cause for relief. You don't have to defend the boundaries of a self that doesn't exist. You can relax into the process, the wandering, the endless transformation.

Ziporyn's translation is not for beginners. His word choices are sometimes jarring — "Heavenly" for *Tian* is accurate but can sound awkward in English. His footnotes are scholarly and assume significant philosophical background. The book also selects only the "essential" writings — about half the Zhuangzi — which means you miss the wilder, more fragmentary passages that give the full text its chaotic energy. And Ziporyn's own philosophical commitments (he's a Hegelian and a process philosopher) influence his reading in ways that a more neutral translator might avoid. The Zhuangzi he presents is a philosopher engaged in serious argument about identity and relativism. The Zhuangzi that just tells stories about useless trees and talking skulls — that Zhuangzi is also in there, but slightly subdued by the analytical frame.
