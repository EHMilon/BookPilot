Most of what you believe about studying is a lie.

That's not hyperbole. It's the thesis of *Make It Stick*, written by two cognitive scientists (Peter C. Brown is a storyteller; Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel are the researchers) who have spent decades proving that the study techniques students swear by — re-reading, highlighting, massed practice — are almost entirely useless. Worse, they create an *illusion* of learning that leads to spectacular failure when real understanding is tested.

The book exists because of a painful gap: cognitive science has known for over a century what actually works for learning, but this knowledge has never made it into classrooms, textbooks, or study guides. Students keep using the same ineffective methods because nobody told them differently. Teachers keep teaching the same way because they were never trained in how memory works.

> "Learning is deeper and more durable when it's effortful. Learning that's easy is like writing in sand, here today and gone tomorrow."

The central finding of the book — and the one that should change your study habits forever — is that the act of *retrieving* information from memory is what makes it stick. Not re-reading. Not reviewing. Not highlighting. The effortful struggle to pull something out of your own head.

Roediger and colleagues have run dozens of experiments confirming this. In one classic study, students who read a passage and then took a test immediately recalled 50% more a week later than students who read the passage twice. The test group felt worse during learning — more frustrated, more confused. But they learned more.

This is the **testing effect**: the finding that retrieval practice — low-stakes, frequent testing — produces significantly better long-term retention than any amount of re-exposure. The harder the retrieval feels, the stronger the learning.

> "The effort of retrieving knowledge or skills from memory changes the nature of the knowledge itself — making it more accessible and more durable."

**The Trap:** Students avoid testing because it reveals gaps. It feels bad to not know something. Re-reading feels good because you can follow the material easily. The choice between feeling good while learning nothing and feeling bad while learning something is the fundamental emotional obstacle to effective studying.

**The Shift:** Replace every other study session with a self-test. Close the book. Write what you remember. Identify what you missed. Study that. Repeat.

Cramming — massed practice — creates the illusion of fluency. You study intensely the night before, do well on the test, and conclude it worked. But a week later, you remember almost nothing. The information never moved into long-term memory.

Spaced practice is the opposite: reviewing material over multiple sessions with increasing gaps between them. The gaps are not empty — they're when the brain consolidates memories. Learning that is spaced out in time is more robust, more flexible, and more durable.

The mechanism is **consolidation** — the process by which short-term memories are stabilized into long-term neural structures. This happens during sleep and during the "offline" periods between study sessions. Cramming doesn't allow enough time for consolidation. Spaced practice does.

> "Practice that's spaced out over time — with gaps for forgetting — produces stronger long-term learning than massed practice."

**The Trap:** Cramming works for tomorrow's test. It's a useful emergency strategy. The problem is that students mistake this short-term performance for real learning and never develop the spaced practice habit. They graduate with degrees and empty heads.

**The Shift:** Schedule three 30-minute review sessions for a single topic, spaced across a week (day 1, day 3, day 7), instead of one 90-minute cram session. The total time is identical. The learning is vastly superior.

Blocked practice — doing the same type of problem twenty times in a row — feels productive. Your performance improves visibly within the session. You feel like you're mastering the material.

But when the test comes — and the problems are mixed up — students who practiced in blocks perform significantly worse than students who practiced in an interleaved format (mixing different problem types randomly). Why? Because blocked practice trains you to *recognize the pattern* of a problem type, not to *select* the right approach. Interleaving forces you to discriminate between problems, which is what real-world testing requires.

> "Interleaving improves your ability to match the right approach to the right problem. Blocked practice only improves your ability to do one type of problem repeatedly."

**The Trap:** Interleaving feels worse during practice. Your performance within the session is lower. You get more questions wrong. This is not a sign that it's not working. It's a sign that it's working exactly as designed — your brain is doing the hard work of categorization and discrimination.

**The Shift:** In your next practice session, don't do twenty problems of one type. Do five problems each of four different types, randomly mixed. The confusion is the learning.

Two techniques that complement retrieval and spacing.

**Elaboration** is the process of explaining a concept in your own words and connecting it to things you already know. When you elaborate, you're building a richer network of associations around the new knowledge, making it easier to retrieve through multiple pathways. The authors suggest the "why" technique: ask yourself "why is this true?" or "how does this relate to what I already know?"

**Generation** is attempting to solve a problem before being shown the solution. This is deeply uncomfortable — you'll be wrong, confused, lost. But the act of struggling primes your brain. When you're subsequently shown the solution, your brain processes it more deeply because it's been activated by your failed attempt. The failure is fertilizer.

> "Trying to solve a problem before being taught the solution leads to better learning, even if you make mistakes."

**The Trap:** We want to see the answer first. We want to understand before we attempt. This is shy. Generation requires you to sit in confusion, try anyway, fail, and then learn from the failure. It feels inefficient. It is extraordinarily efficient.

**The Shift:** Before looking up an answer or asking for help, spend five minutes trying to figure it out yourself. Write down your best guess. Then check. The incorrect guess makes the correct answer more memorable.

This chapter might be the most valuable in the entire book for educators. Roediger and McDaniel demolish the "learning styles" theory — the idea that people are visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learners — with a decade of evidence showing it has no scientific support. Multiple large-scale studies have failed to find any benefit to matching instruction to a student's preferred style.

This matters because the learning styles myth has cost billions in educational resources and has prevented students from using techniques that actually work. A "visual learner" who avoids auditory instruction is missing out on an entire modality of learning for no good reason.

> "The widespread belief in learning styles has been thoroughly debunked by research. Yet it persists in teacher training programs and educational materials."

**The Trap:** The learning styles label becomes a self-limiting belief. "I'm not good at listening to lectures because I'm a visual learner." No — you're not good at listening to lectures because you haven't practiced listening. The more modes you engage, the better you learn.

**The Shift:** Use multiple modalities for every subject. Read it, say it aloud, draw it, teach it to someone. Don't pick one "style." Use them all. Your brain learns best when it processes information through every available channel.

The book's final major concept is **deliberate practice** — a term borrowed from Anders Ericsson's research on expertise. Deliberate practice is not just repetition. It's practice that is:
1. **Goal-oriented** (specific, measurable improvements)
2. **Focused** (full attention, not casual repetition)
3. **Feedback-rich** (immediate knowledge of results)
4. **At the edge of ability** (difficult enough to require effort)

The authors argue that deliberate practice is how experts in any field outperform others — not through innate talent, but through the cumulative effect of thousands of hours of structured, effortful practice.

> "Deliberate practice is the gold standard for skill development. It's not about how many hours you practice, but how you practice."

**The Trap:** People accumulate "practice hours" doing things they're already good at. This feels productive but produces no growth. Real improvement requires practicing at the edge of your ability, where you make mistakes and have to correct them.

**The Shift:** Identify the one thing in your skill set where you're most likely to fail. Practice just that component, with focused attention and immediate feedback, until it improves. Then find the next weakness.

The deepest argument of *Make It Stick* is both liberating and uncomfortable: learning is supposed to be hard. The struggle, the confusion, the repeated failure — these are not obstacles to learning. They are learning. The book asks you to stop looking for easier ways to study and instead embrace the difficulty, because the difficulty is the mechanism.

**The idea I couldn't shake** was the testing effect. I've been a "re-reader" my entire life — I would read a chapter twice, highlight key passages, and feel ready. Then I would forget everything within two weeks. The shift to retrieval practice was painful at first — I felt stupid when I couldn't remember what I'd just read. But after two weeks of using active recall, I realized my retention had more than doubled. The discomfort of retrieval is the cost of entry to actual knowledge.

**Honest criticism:** The book suffers from a problem endemic to academic writers: it says the same thing in too many ways. The core ideas — retrieval, spacing, interleaving — are presented early and then repeated through multiple studies and examples. The writing can feel dry and textbook-like, lacking the narrative energy that would make it more compelling. The case studies vary in quality; some feel illustrative, others feel forced.

1. Replace one passive study session with a retrieval test: read one chapter, close the book, write everything you recall for 10 minutes.
2. Schedule one concept for spaced practice: study it today, review in 3 days, review again in 7 days.
3. For one practice session, interleave three different problem types randomly instead of doing them in blocks.
4. Before looking up the answer to a question, spend 5 minutes trying to generate your own answer — even if you're sure you're wrong.
