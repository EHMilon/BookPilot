Katherine May opens her book in the coldest season of her life — not a literal winter, but a winter of the soul. Her husband becomes seriously ill, her son stops going to school, her own body begins to fail her in mysterious and frightening ways, and the life she had carefully built — the career, the routines, the sense of being a competent adult — crumbles around her. She finds herself standing in the middle of a supermarket, unable to remember what she came for, unable to remember why anything matters, feeling as though she has been hollowed out and left empty. This is what she calls **wintering** — not the season of snow and ice, but the season of withdrawal, of healing, of lying fallow when the world expects you to produce. "Wintering is a season in the cold," she writes. "It is a fallow period in life when you're cut off from the world, feeling full of fog and emptiness."

The trap that May identifies is not winter itself but our modern refusal to accept it. We have built a culture that worships productivity, optimism, and relentless forward momentum. Sickness is an inconvenience to be medicated away. Sadness is a disorder to be treated. Rest is laziness. Grief is a problem to be solved. We have convinced ourselves that the ideal human life is a straight line of upward progress, and anything that deviates from that line — any period of stagnation, confusion, or retreat — is a failure that must be corrected. May argues that this belief is not just wrong but dangerous. Life does not move in a straight line. It moves in cycles. There is spring and summer, and there is also autumn and winter. To deny the winter is to deny half of what it means to be alive.

The friction is that wintering is not optional. It comes for everyone. It arrives in the form of illness, loss, burnout, heartbreak, betrayal, or the quiet accumulation of exhaustion that eventually demands its toll. You do not choose to winter. You are chosen by it. And when it comes, the instinct to fight it, to push through, to pretend everything is fine, only makes the winter longer and harder. May tells the story of trying to force herself back to normal — going to work when she could barely function, smiling through conversations when she felt like screaming, pretending to enjoy social events when every interaction felt like a performance. The result was not recovery but collapse. The winter demanded to be honored, and it would not be ignored.

May draws a profound distinction between **the dark** — which is natural, necessary, and full of potential — and the fear of the dark that we have been taught. She travels to Norway to experience the polar night, weeks of continuous darkness, and finds that the people who live there do not fight it. They light candles, they move more slowly, they rest more, they tell stories, they accept that this is the season they are in. The darkness becomes a container for reflection, intimacy, and quiet growth that would be impossible in the brightness of summer. The problem, May suggests, is not the dark itself but our cultural insistence on treating it as an enemy. We have filled our nights with artificial light, our winters with forced cheer, our grief with platitudes. We have lost the ability to simply sit in the dark and let it do its work.

The concept of **the fallow season** is central to May's understanding of wintering. In traditional agriculture, fields were left fallow for a season — not because the soil was worthless, but because it needed to rest and regenerate. The fallow field looks barren, but underground, microorganisms are rebuilding the soil, nutrients are returning, and the land is preparing itself for future abundance. May argues that human beings need the same cycles of fallowness, but we have forgotten how to recognize them or honor them. When we feel ourselves going fallow, we panic. We reach for distractions, medications, productivity hacks, anything to avoid the sensation of being unproductive. But the fallow season is not empty time. It is essential time. The seeds that will grow in your next spring are being prepared in the dark soil of your winter.

May's exploration of **rest** as an active, intentional practice — not just the absence of work — is one of the most quietly radical ideas in the book. She describes learning to rest the way she once learned to work: with discipline, with attention, with respect for the craft of it. She takes cold-water swims in the English Channel, not because she enjoys the shock of the freezing water, but because the shock demands her full presence, and in that presence, her racing mind finally quiets. She walks alone on the beach, watches seabirds, reads books that have nothing to do with her work. She learns to pay attention to the small, slow processes of the natural world — the way frost forms on a window, the way light changes through a winter afternoon, the patient persistence of lichen on stone. These are not escapes from life. They are ways of being fully in life, on winter's terms rather than summer's.

The trap that many people fall into when they encounter the idea of wintering is to romanticize it, to turn it into a spiritual retreat, to imagine that lying in bed with a cup of tea watching the snow fall is the substance of recovery. May has no patience for this fantasy. Wintering is not picturesque. It is the time when your husband is in the hospital and you do not know if he will come home. It is the time when your child is suffering and you cannot fix it. It is the time when you are so exhausted that brushing your teeth feels like a heroic achievement. The truth of wintering is that it is ugly, lonely, and terrifying. The value of wintering is not that it feels good, but that it is necessary. Trying to skip it is like trying to skip the winter in a garden. You can do it with greenhouses and artificial lights, but the plants will be weaker for it, and eventually something will break.

May's book is structured as a series of essays that move through her own winters and the winters of people she encounters. She writes about the Icelandic tradition of *jólabókaflóðið* — the Christmas book flood, where books are given as gifts on Christmas Eve and the night is spent reading and drinking hot chocolate. She writes about the Celtic tradition of Imbolc, the festival that marks the beginning of spring, when the first signs of life appear in the frozen ground. She writes about visiting the caves of Lascaux, where prehistoric humans painted magnificent animals on the walls during the deep winter, creating beauty in the darkness not because they were happy but because beauty was something they could make when nothing else was possible. Each of these stories is a meditation on the same truth: winter is not an interruption of life. It is a phase of life, as real and necessary as any other.

The paradigm shift in May's work is subtle but profound. We have been trained to see difficulty as a problem to be solved — something to overcome, fix, or eliminate as quickly as possible. May invites us to see difficulty as a season to be lived through — something to experience, learn from, and allow to shape us. The difference is not in the events themselves but in the relationship we have to them. A problem demands a solution. A season demands patience. A problem makes you feel inadequate if you cannot fix it. A season makes you feel connected to the natural rhythms of existence. "We must learn to invite the winter in," May writes. "Not as a punishment, but as a time of growth."

The 48-hour implementation plan May would recommend is not about doing more but about doing less, and doing it more intentionally. Step one: find a window of time — even just two hours — when you can be completely alone, without your phone, without screens, without any agenda except to sit with yourself. This is not a meditation session with a timer and a goal. It is just sitting. Let your mind drift. Notice what comes up. Do not try to fix it. Step two: take a walk in whatever weather exists outside your door. Do not dress for comfort. Dress for the weather — feel the cold or the rain or the wind on your skin. Pay attention to the small things — the color of the sky, the movement of leaves, the sound of birds, the feel of the ground beneath your feet. Walk until you feel your mind slow down. Step three: identify one obligation in your life that you are carrying out of habit rather than necessity — a social engagement, a work commitment, a personal standard — and give yourself permission to drop it for the next week. Not forever, just for the next week. Wintering means conserving energy, not proving your strength. Step four: create a small winter ritual for the evening. Light a candle at the same time each night. Make a cup of tea and drink it without looking at a screen. Write a few sentences in a notebook — not a journal entry full of analysis and insight, just whatever is present in your mind. The ritual matters more than its content. It is a way of telling your nervous system that rest is allowed, that the dark is safe, that you do not need to be productive to be worthy.

May's book has honest limitations. It is a memoir, not a manual, and its gentle, essayistic style may frustrate readers who want a prescriptive program for getting through hard times. May does not offer strategies or techniques. She offers companionship. She walks beside you in winter rather than telling you how to get out of it. For someone in acute crisis, the book may feel too slow, too lyrical, too removed from the urgent practicalities of survival. And the book's premise — that winter is natural and necessary — can be hard to accept when the winter is caused by circumstances that are not natural at all: systemic injustice, violence, poverty, abandonment. May does not pretend that all winters are the same or that acceptance is always the right response. Some winters should be fought. Some darkness should be escaped. The art is knowing which is which.

The thought that lingers after reading May is that we have been told a story about life that is incomplete. We are told that life is about growth, achievement, progress, and the relentless pursuit of happiness. We are told that difficulty is an aberration, a detour from the proper course. May suggests the opposite. Difficulty is the proper course. Winter is not the exception but the rule. The people who live well are not the ones who avoid suffering, but the ones who have learned to move through the seasons of their lives with grace — who know when to push and when to rest, when to fight and when to surrender, when to light a candle against the dark and when to let the dark hold them. "Wintering will happen to all of us," May writes. "The trick is to learn how to do it well."
