There's a moment in *Untamed* where Glennon Doyle describes sitting in a parking lot, staring at her phone. On the screen is a text message that could end her marriage — because she's fallen in love with a woman, and she has to decide whether to tell her husband. She has three kids. She has a public image. She has a life that looks, from the outside, like everything she ever wanted. And she's about to blow it up because something inside her is screaming and she can't make it stop.

That's where this book starts — in the gap between the life you're supposed to want and the life your body is begging you to live. Glennon calls that gap the **Knowing**. It's the voice inside that tells you the truth before your brain catches up. Most of us spend our lives drowning it out. *Untamed* is an instruction manual for stopping.

> "The Knowing is not a thought. It's a sensation. It's a pull, a magnetism, a tug on your gut."

Glennon Doyle is not a subtle writer. She writes in short, punchy chapters that feel like sermons — or like she's sitting across from you at a coffee shop, gripping your wrist, refusing to let go until you hear her. Some people love this. Some find it exhausting. But everyone seems to remember what she said. She came to fame through her blog *Momastery*, then a memoir called *Carry On, Warrior*, then a marriage memoir called *Love Warrior*, which Oprah picked for her book club. *Untamed* is the third act — the one where she stops apologizing and starts telling the full truth.

The structure is built around a metaphor that runs through the entire book: **the cheetah**. Glennon went to a zoo and watched a cheetah pace in its cage — a prisoner of its own enclosure, because cheetahs in captivity don't know they're meant to run 70 miles per hour. They've never been taught. They pace, they eat, they lie down. That's all they know. And she realized: that's most women. Caged by expectations we didn't choose, internalizing limitations we think are natural. The cheetah doesn't know it's fast. And most women don't know they're free.

> "We are all born cheetahs. Then the world tells us: No. You are not a cheetah. You are a good girl. A good girl who sits still and is quiet and makes everyone comfortable."

The book is structured as a series of short, punchy chapters — each one a miniature essay on a single idea. Glennon calls them "knowings" — truths she arrived at not through logic but through paying attention to her body and her life. She writes in a style that's almost biblical: short sentences, rhythmic repetition, commands instead of suggestions. "You are a cheetah. You were born to run. Stop pacing."

**The Trap here** is confusion. Most of us don't feel caged. We have freedom — we vote, we work, we make choices. But Glennon is pointing at something deeper: the cage is the way we've learned to perform our lives. We say yes when we mean no. We stay in jobs, relationships, identities that don't fit because changing would be unthinkable. The cage is the voice in your head that says "What will people think?" before you even know what you want.

Glennon identifies several **Bucks** — the familiar promises we tell ourselves to stay safe. "Once I lose the weight, I'll be happy." "Once I get the promotion, I'll have enough." "Once the kids are older, I'll have time for myself." These bucks are the currency of the cage. They're not real goals — they're distractions from the terror of actually living uncontained.

> "The only thing that was ever wrong with me was my belief that there was something wrong with me."

**The Trap here** is believing the bucks. They feel like plans. They feel like hope. But they're actually deferrals — a way of putting your life on hold while you chase a condition that, when met, will only create another condition. The real work isn't achieving the buck. It's realizing you don't need any of them.

Glennon tells the story of a friend who, when asked what she wanted for her birthday, broke down crying — because she had no idea. She'd spent so long being what everyone else needed that she'd lost contact with her own desires. This is the disease *Untamed* is trying to cure: the erosion of the self through constant accommodation.

The most radical thing *Untamed* does is claim that **selfishness is a spiritual practice**. Glennon spends an entire chapter arguing that the word "selfish" has been used to keep women small. When a woman does what she needs — leaves a marriage, takes time for herself, prioritizes her own growth — she's called selfish. But here's the truth: the people who call you selfish are usually the ones who benefited from your selflessness. Glennon's argument is that the most generous thing you can do for everyone in your life is to become fully yourself. A hollowed-out, resentful woman is not actually helping anyone.

This is the foundation of her relationship advice, which runs counter to everything we're taught. She doesn't believe in compromise, at least not in the way we usually practice it — two people each giving up pieces of themselves to meet in the middle. She believes in two whole people staying whole and choosing each other every day. If you have to shrink to fit together, you're not in a relationship. You're in a cage with two people.

> "Relationships are not 50/50. They're 100/100. Two whole people showing up with all their parts."

The affair at the center of the book — her relationship with Abby Wambach, the soccer star — is handled not as a scandal but as a homecoming. Glennon doesn't apologize for it. She doesn't frame it as a mistake. She frames it as finally listening to the Knowing. This is where the book will lose some readers. If you believe marriage is a sacred, unbreakable covenant, *Untamed* will feel like a justification for selfishness dressed up as enlightenment. Glennon knows this. She doesn't care. Her answer: the marriage was already broken — they were both performing fidelity while dying inside. The affair wasn't the betrayal. The 20 years of pretending was the betrayal.

The writing is deliberately raw. There are no fancy sentences, no literary detours. Glennon writes like she's talking to a friend who's about to make a terrible decision — fast, direct, insistent. The short chapters (some are only a page) give the book a staccato rhythm. You can feel her urgency.

Let me be honest about the criticism. *Untamed* is polarizing by design, but some of the criticisms are fair. The book is repetitive — the same insights arrive in different packaging across the chapters. The cheetah metaphor, powerful at first, gets worn out by the third or fourth recurrence. Glennon's prose style, which some find electrifying, feels to others like an Instagram caption stretched to 300 pages. There's also a privilege problem: Glennon can blow up her life because she has a platform, a publisher advance, and a partner with resources. Her advice to "just listen to your Knowing" is easier to follow when you have a safety net.

And the biggest tension: the book frames personal liberation as a moral imperative — not just good for you, but the right thing to do. But what if your Knowing tells you to do something genuinely harmful? Glennon doesn't address this. She assumes the inner voice is always wise. A more skeptical reading would say that sometimes the voice inside is just your id, and the cage exists for good reason.

Women who feel trapped — in marriages, careers, identities, expectations — and need permission to leave. Also, anyone who's ever thought "Is this all there is?" and wanted someone to say "No, it's not."

People who value institutional commitment (marriage, religion, family loyalty) over personal authenticity. The book will frustrate you.

The cheetah. You'll see her pacing in her cage, and you'll wonder about your own enclosure.

1. **Name your cage.** Write down one expectation you're living up to that you didn't choose — something you do because you're "supposed to." Just naming it is the first step.

2. **Feel the Knowing.** For 24 hours, pay attention to your body's reactions — the yes and the no. When does your gut tighten? When does it relax? Write down two moments.

3. **One selfish act.** Do one thing today that you want to do, that serves no one but you, without apology. See if the world ends. It won't.
