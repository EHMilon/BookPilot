Thomas Merton (adaptation/translation)

A Trappist monk, living in silent contemplation in a Kentucky monastery, reading the words of a Chinese philosopher from two thousand years ago — and discovering that they already speak the same language. **The Way of Chuang Tzu** is not a translation in the normal sense. Thomas Merton didn't know Chinese. He worked from existing English translations by Arthur Waley, James Legge, and others, and *re-wrote* them, distilling each passage to its essence, adding nothing and subtracting everything that didn't resonate. The result is Zhuangzi through a Christian mystic's heart — filtered, yes, but filtered through a sensibility so finely tuned that the filter becomes part of the art.

Merton discovered Zhuangzi late in his life, in the 1960s, during a period of profound spiritual ferment. He had already written his classics — *The Seven Storey Mountain*, *New Seeds of Contemplation* — and was increasingly drawn to dialogue with Eastern traditions. What he found in Zhuangzi was a confirmation of something he had known from his own monastic practice: that the deepest truth cannot be forced, that silence is a better teacher than words, and that the "true person" is not someone who achieves special status but someone who *stops needing status*. Merton writes in his introduction: "Chuang Tzu is a philosopher of the concrete, who concerns himself primarily with human acts and attitudes, rather than with speculative systems." The same could be said of Merton himself.

Merton's rendering of Cook Ding is one of the finest in English. His version runs: "Prince Hui's cook was cutting up an ox. Every blow of his hand, every heave of his shoulders, every tread of his foot, every thrust of his knee — *whoosh!* — the perfect rhythm of the dance." The joy in the prose is unmistakable. Merton is not just translating — he's *inhabiting* the Cook's experience. The cook says at the end: "I have used this same knife for nineteen years, and it is still as sharp as the day it was new. Because the joints have spaces, and the blade has no thickness. To insert what has no thickness into a space that is no-space — there is plenty of room." Merton, who spent years in a Trappist cell learning that the spirit moves best through the path of least resistance, understood this passage in his bones.

Merton's Zhuangzi is beautiful, wise, and sometimes *too gentle*. The original Zhuangzi is spiky — he mocks, he mocks the mockers, he writes passages that intentionally gross you out. "The Way of Chuang Tzu" smooths over some of this. The scatological humor is minimized. The philosophical anarchism — Zhuangzi's claim that government is a kind of mutilation — is softened into a gentler critique of ambition. The trap is reading Merton's version and thinking you've gotten the full Zhuangzi. You've gotten the part of Zhuangzi that a Christian monk could responsibly integrate. The rest — the radical skepticism, the intentional weirdness, the cruelty of some of the jokes — is waiting for you in the complete text.

Merton selects thirty-one passages, and his selection itself is a commentary. He opens with the book's most accessible parables — the useless tree, the empty boat, the drunk man who falls from a cart and doesn't get hurt — before deepening into the more philosophically challenging passages about the equality of things and the true man. This sequencing is deliberate pedagogy. Merton knew his Western readers would be lost if he started with Zhuangzi's radical ontology. So he begins where everyone can enter: a story about a tree that survived being chopped down because it was too crooked to be useful. Then, slowly, he leads you toward the places where your assumptions about self, death, and knowledge are quietly dismantled. He favors passages about non-action, about the useless tree that survives because it's useless, about the empty boat that doesn't get angry when it's hit. He downplays the intellectual showmanship — Zhuangzi's demolition of the logician Huizi — and emphasizes the practical wisdom. The passage "Three in the Morning" (about a monkey keeper who teaches his monkeys to accept three acorns in the morning and four in the evening, then reverses it when they complain) becomes, in Merton's hands, a parable about how our sense of fairness is just a story we've gotten used to.

One of Merton's most striking choices is how he handles the passage about the debate between Zhuangzi and Huizi on the Hao River bridge. In Merton's version, the dialogue feels almost like a Zen koan — two friends teasing each other into insight. Merton renders Zhuangzi's final line as: "I know it from here — standing here on the Hao River." The emphasis on the \*standing here\*, the immediacy of the moment, is pure Merton. He spent his whole monastic life trying to be \*here\* — present to God, present to silence, present to the moment — and he recognized in Zhuangzi's reply the same priority of direct experience over abstract reasoning that his own tradition called "the sacrament of the present moment."

The most powerful section is Merton's rendering of the death of Zhuangzi's wife. Where the original has Zhuangzi drumming and singing, Merton's version adds poignancy: "When she died, I was affected... But I reflected on the beginning. She was not yet alive, not yet formed, not yet embodied. The intermingling of the formless produced a change, and there she was in her body. Another change, and she was alive. Now another change, and she is dead. These four stages are like the four seasons. Now she lies asleep in the great house of the universe." Merton's own experience of lost loved ones — and his own sense of approaching death (he would die accidentally in 1968, just years after this book was published) — gives the passage a weight it wouldn't have from a purely academic translator.

Merton was a Christian monk, and it shows. He translates *Tao* as "Way" (which is fine) but his introduction frames the whole text in terms of Christian apophatic mysticism — the via negativa, the cloud of unknowing. Zhuangzi is not a mystic in the Christian sense. He doesn't seek union with a personal God. He doesn't seek anything. Merton's reading is an *interpretation*, and while it's a rich and respectful one, it's not neutral. The trap is reading Merton's Zhuangzi and assuming the Chinese original is trying to say the same things as Meister Eckhart. There's overlap, but there's also crucial difference.

The idea I couldn't shake is that Merton's Zhuangzi does something that few academic translations achieve: it makes the philosophy feel *practicable*. When you read Burton Watson's excellent scholarly translation, you're reading a text. When you read Merton's version, you're reading someone who *tried to live these words*. The passage about the empty boat — the boat that floats down the river and hits another boat; if the other boat is empty, nobody gets angry, but if there's a person in it, the collision is a provocation — is rendered by Merton with such clarity that you close the book and feel the shift in your own perception. The next time someone annoys you, you think: *empty boat*.

The book is thin — thirty-one passages, barely a hundred sixty pages of text. It's an appetizer, not a meal. Merton's lack of Chinese means his "translation" is really a poetic adaptation of other people's translations, which creates a double distance from the original. The introduction, while beautiful, sometimes veers into Merton talking about Merton rather than about Zhuangzi. And the book's gentle, wise tone can be misleading — it makes Zhuangzi feel safe and welcoming, when the original text is deliberately destabilizing.
