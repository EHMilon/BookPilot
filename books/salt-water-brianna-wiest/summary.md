Here's a confession that most books won't make: sometimes you do everything right and still feel like you're drowning. You go to therapy, you meditate, you set boundaries, you do the work — and the heaviness doesn't lift. *Salt Water* is for those moments. Not when you need a solution, but when you need something saltier — something that doesn't try to fix you but simply stays with you in the deep.

Brianna Wiest wrote *Salt Water* as a companion to *Ceremony* — a second collection of prose-poetry that leans even further into the metaphor of the ocean and the body. Where *Ceremony* was about the rituals of becoming, *Salt Water* is about the grief that comes before the becoming. The stuckness. The feeling that you're carrying something too heavy and nobody can see it. It's less about healing and more about the permission to hurt without rushing to fix it.

The book's central metaphor runs through every page: salt water as tears, as the ocean, as sweat, as blood. Wiest is interested in the things that come out of us when we can't hold them anymore. And her argument is that there's no way around grief — only through it, and through means learning to swim in waters that will never be fully calm.

> "You cannot bypass the grief. You can only walk through it until you find yourself on the other side, salt-stained and different."

This is the book's hardest truth, delivered without softening. Most self-help is about getting *out* of pain. *Salt Water* is about learning to live *in* it. Not forever, but long enough for it to change you. Wiest is suspicious of quick fixes, and the book is an argument against the idea that you can heal by reading the right thing or doing the right exercise. Some grief just needs time — and time needs to be felt, not managed.

She describes grief as a landscape you have to cross — not a condition you need to cure. There are days when the water is calm and you can see the bottom. There are days when the current is too strong to fight and you just have to float. The book's emotional architecture respects both states. It doesn't tell you to be stronger or to try harder. It tells you that the water is deep, and that's not your fault.

**The Trap:** It's dangerously easy to interpret this as "pain is permanent and you should just accept it." That's not what Wiest is saying. She's saying that the resistance to grief — the frantic search for a way out — is often more painful than the grief itself. Surrender to the process isn't giving up. It's giving in to the only path through. The difference between resignation and surrender is subtle but crucial: resignation says "nothing matters," surrender says "this matters, and I will feel it fully so I can eventually let it go."

One of the book's strongest threads is the connection between emotional pain and physical sensation. Wiest writes about grief in the chest, about the tightness in the throat that comes before crying, about the exhaustion that has no physical cause. She's trying to give language to the experience of being *embodied* in pain — of not being able to think your way out of something because it lives in your bones.

> "Your body is not betraying you. It is holding what you would not let go."

This is a profound reframe. When your body hurts without medical cause — when you're tired, when you feel heavy, when you can't get out of bed — it's easy to feel cursed. Wiest suggests something gentler: your body is doing its job. It's holding the pain you haven't been able to process. The solution isn't to force it out. It's to make space for it, to thank your tired arms and aching back for their patience, and to slowly, gently, let the grief move.

She writes about the particular loneliness of grief that has no visible cause — when your life looks fine from the outside but feels unbearable from the inside. The shame of saying "I'm struggling" when nothing is technically wrong. *Salt Water* gives language to that shame and then gently dismantles it. You don't need a reason to hurt. You're human. That's reason enough.

Wiest dedicates significant space to the terror of transition — not the dramatic, visible changes, but the quiet ones where you don't know who you are anymore. She describes this as standing on a threshold, one foot in the life you're leaving and one foot in the life you haven't reached yet. The worst part isn't the sadness. It's the vertigo of having no solid ground.

This vertigo is the subject of some of the book's best passages. Wiest writes about the moments when you can't recognize yourself — when the person in the mirror feels like a stranger, when the decisions that used to be easy now feel impossible. She calls this the **threshold of becoming**, and her advice is counterintuitive: don't try to find yourself. Let yourself be lost for a while. The finding comes later.

> "You are not lost. You are between worlds. And the only way out is all the way through."

This is the book's most comforting assertion, and it's true in a way that most platitudes aren't. When you're in the middle of a genuine transition, the feeling of being lost isn't a sign of failure — it's the defining feature of the territory. Wiest gives you permission to be lost without calling it a problem, without rushing to find your way, without pretending you know where you're going.

A significant portion of *Salt Water* is about love — specifically, the realization that love is not rescue. Wiest writes about the fantasy that someone else will arrive and make the heaviness go away, and then the harder truth: no one can. The people who love you can sit with you in the deep water, but they can't lift you out of it.

> "The love you are looking for is not someone who will save you. It is someone who will stay with you while you save yourself."

This is the book at its most mature. It refuses the romantic fantasy of salvation while honoring the genuine power of companionship. You can be loved *and* still be in pain. Love doesn't fix that. It just makes it less lonely. Wiest writes about the kind of love that doesn't try to solve you, doesn't panic at your sadness, doesn't demand that you be okay before you're ready. That kind of love, she suggests, is rarer and more valuable than the dramatic rescues we're taught to want.

The book doesn't end with a triumphant breakthrough. It ends with a gradual return — the way you eventually, imperceptibly, start to feel less heavy. Not because the grief is gone, but because you've grown strong enough to carry it. The salt water recedes, not because the tide stopped coming in, but because you learned to breathe even when it did.

*Salt Water* shares most of its format and tone with *Ceremony*, which means it suffers from the same structural limitations: one-note cadence, repetitive syntax, a format that some readers will find too sparse. More than *Ceremony*, this book feels like it exists in a specific emotional register — deep grief, quiet despair — and if you're not in that register, it can feel unrelenting. The metaphor of salt water is stretched thin in places, and some passages land with less impact because the imagery has been worked over too many times. It's also worth asking whether reading about grief starts to feel like an indulgence in it — whether the book's comfort with pain makes it harder to move on from the place it describes. Wiest doesn't fully address this tension.
