If you practice yoga and want to stay comfortable in your assumptions about where it came from, do not read this book. I'm being serious. *Yoga Body: The Origins of Modern Posture Practice* by Mark Singleton is the academic equivalent of pulling back the curtain in Oz — and what's behind it is not ancient Indian sages passing down 5,000-year-old secrets. It's a 20th-century mashup of British gymnastics, Danish calisthenics, Indian nationalism, and a handful of truly brilliant innovators who changed the course of the practice in a single lifetime.

Singleton's thesis is simple and devastating: the physical postures (asanas) that most of the world now calls "yoga" are not, for the most part, ancient. They're modern. They emerged in the early 20th century, synthesized from Indian wrestling, Western physical culture, and the visionary work of a handful of Indian teachers — most notably Krishnamacharya and his students (Iyengar, Jois, Devi). What we call "yoga" in a studio in 2025 is, in large part, a post-colonial construction.

The book opens with a historical puzzle. In the 17th to 19th centuries, European travelers and colonial administrators documented the yogis they encountered in India. These figures were described as emaciated, naked, sitting in extreme postures, often covered in ashes, living in caves or forests. They were not doing sun salutations. They were not doing 90-minute flow classes. They were doing *hatha yoga* — a radical, ascetic practice involving a tiny handful of seated postures, breath retention, and esoteric rituals. The texts of medieval hatha yoga mention about 84 asanas, but most are variations of seated meditation poses. There's no Downward Dog, no Warrior, no Triangle — none of the standing postures that make up 90% of a modern class.

So where did the standing poses come from? Singleton traces them to an unlikely source: early 20th-century European physical culture. The "Danish gymnastics" of Niels Bukh, which featured dynamic, flowing sequences of standing postures, look strikingly similar to what we now call Vinyasa. Bukh's system was designed to build strength, flexibility, and national pride — and it spread across Europe and into India through British colonial institutions.

In India, this Western physical culture was blended with indigenous traditions to create something new. Indian nationalists like Vivekananda, Aurobindo, and eventually Krishnamarcharya wanted to create a system of physical practice that was both modern (to counter colonial narratives that Indians were weak and decadent) and Indian (to assert cultural pride and identity). The result was a hybrid: Western calisthenics fused with Indian philosophy and renamed as "yoga."

**The Trap:** Many yoga practitioners react to this history with either denial (it must be wrong) or despair (so it's all fake?). Singleton's purpose is neither to debunk nor to validate but to *understand*. The modern yoga tradition is not "fake" because it's modern. It's a living tradition that evolved — like every other tradition in history. The question is not "is it authentic?" but "does it work, and for whom?" If your practice is grounded in the physical body and you find meaning in the philosophy, knowing its history shouldn't change that.

The middle section of the book covers the story of Krishnamacharya in detail — and it's genuinely fascinating. Krishnamacharya was a Brahmin scholar who spent years learning from a yogi in a cave, then returned to teach in Mysore under the patronage of the Maharaja. He developed a system of asana practice that was designed to build the bodies of young boys — literal children — into strong, flexible warriors. The famous sequences that Ashtanga practitioners now do three hours a day were originally designed for adolescent boys in a palace gymnasium. They were not intended for 40-year-old office workers with desk backs.

Singleton also covers the transmission of yoga to the West. The book tracks the key figures: Swami Vivekananda, who brought meditation yoga to the 1893 World's Parliament of Religions; theosophists like Helena Blavatsky; and eventually Indra Devi, Krishnamacharya's first female student, who opened a yoga studio in Hollywood and taught celebrities. Each transmission involved adaptation — sometimes radical — to the new context. The yoga that arrived in California in the 1960s was already several generations removed from its Indian sources.

The story of Indra Devi deserves more attention than it gets. She was a Russian-born, German-raised woman who ended up in India, convinced Krishnamacharya to teach her when he was refusing to teach women, and then brought the practice to Hollywood. Her students included Greta Garbo, Gloria Swanson, and other stars. She softened the practice, made it accessible, removed the militaristic edge, and framed it as health and beauty rather than spiritual discipline. Singleton treats her ambivalently — she was both a pioneer and a popularizer who sanded off the rough edges. But her influence on modern yoga is incalculable.

What I found most valuable was Singleton's analysis of how asana practice became *feminized* in the West. In India, yoga was primarily practiced by men. In the West, it became seen as a "women's practice" — soft, stretchy, spiritual, not "real" exercise. Singleton shows how this happened through a combination of media representation, marketing decisions, and cultural assumptions about gender. The effect has been lasting: yoga studios in the West are still overwhelmingly women's spaces, and men who practice often feel the need to justify it.

**The Trap of Purity Culture:** The book has been used by some to argue that only "traditional" yoga is real yoga — and that modern postural yoga is a degraded, Westernized corruption. Singleton himself doesn't make this argument. He's a scholar, not a gatekeeper. The real lesson is that *all* traditions evolve, and purity narratives are usually nationalist or colonial in origin. The practice that feels ancient and sacred to you is probably 80-100 years old in its current form. That doesn't make it less valuable. It makes it *human*.

**Honest Criticism:** This is an academic text. Singleton writes clearly, but the level of detail — the footnoting, the methodology discussions, the historiographical debates — can be heavy for a general reader. The book assumes a level of familiarity with both Indian history and Western physical culture that most casual readers won't have.

Also: the book focuses almost entirely on the physical practice of asana. It says very little about meditation, breathwork, philosophy, or the other limbs of yoga. Singleton is clear that this is deliberate — he's writing about one specific strand of the tradition — but the narrow focus can give the impression that the book is claiming more than it is. The "yoga body" of the title refers specifically to the modern postural practice, not to yoga as a whole system.

And the book is, inevitably, somewhat dated. Published in 2010, it doesn't cover the explosion of hot yoga, acro-yoga, yoga therapy, or the billion-dollar global wellness industry that has emerged since. A reader in 2025 will need to supplement Singleton with more recent research.
