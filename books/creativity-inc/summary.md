In 1986, Ed Catmull bought a computer from Lucasfilm for $10 and used it to change cinema forever. As the co-founder of Pixar, he led a company that produced eleven consecutive box office hits — an unbroken streak that defied every law of the movie business. *Creativity, Inc.* is not a book about how Pixar made great movies. It's a book about how you build a culture that *keeps* making great movies, decade after decade, without the entropy and mediocrity that normally overtake successful organizations. Catmull is a computer scientist by training, a philosopher by temperament, and he treats the problem of sustained creativity as a systems engineering challenge: how do you design an organization where creative people can reliably do their best work, even as it grows from forty people to over a thousand?

Catmull's central insight is radically simple and radically uncomfortable: the biggest enemy of creativity is the fear of failure. Not lack of talent, not lack of resources, not bad luck. "Fear is the root of so many of the problems that I've seen in organizations," he writes. "Fear of failure. Fear of being exposed. Fear of being outdone." The problem is structural: creative work requires risk-taking, and risk-taking means failing. But organizations — especially successful ones — develop immune systems against failure. They create processes, approvals, and review systems designed to prevent mistakes. And in doing so, they destroy the very conditions that produce creative breakthroughs. Pixar's famous "Braintrust" meetings, where directors present works-in-progress to a group of trusted peers for brutal feedback, are a deliberate mechanism to short-circuit this fear. The rule is simple: everyone in the room is there to make the movie better, not to protect their ego. "You are not your idea," Catmull insists, "and if you identify too closely with your ideas, you will be unable to see when they need to change."

You build systems to prevent failure, and those systems prevent risk. The safer the organization feels, the less creative it actually becomes. Safety and creativity are not the same thing.

The Braintrust is Pixar's soul, and Catmull devotes a beautiful chapter to how it works. A director presents a rough cut — not a finished film, but something broken, ugly, and full of problems. Then ten or twelve trusted colleagues tell the director, with complete honesty, everything that isn't working. No authority hierarchy. No diplomatic softening. "Notes" are suggestions, not commands — the director has no obligation to implement them. But the honesty is non-negotiable. The Braintrust succeeds because it separates the critique from the critic. The goal is to solve problems, not to assign blame. "If you give a good note," Catmull writes, "it's because you're thinking about the problem from the filmmaker's perspective. You're trying to make them better, not prove that you're smarter."

The Braintrust model works because it inverts the normal power dynamics of creative organizations. In most companies, junior people are afraid to criticize senior people. At Pixar, the most junior Braintrust member can tell the most senior director that their second act doesn't work. Catmull engineered this deliberately: "Candor isn't cruel. It doesn't hurt. What hurts is when you're working on something that isn't working and nobody tells you."

You confuse politeness with respect. You think giving soft feedback is kind when it's actually a form of abandonment — you're letting someone waste months on a problem you could have helped them fix in a day.

Pixar is famous for its process — the intense rigors of storyboarding, the "reviews" at every stage, the relentless iteration. But Catmull warns against worshiping process. "Getting the team right is more important than getting the ideas right," he writes. A good team with a mediocre idea will make the idea great. A mediocre team with a great idea will ruin it. Process is necessary but not sufficient. The danger Catmull identifies is that process becomes a crutch — a way of avoiding the harder work of building trust, fostering candor, and empowering people. When Pixar had its first real failures (Cars 2, Brave went through painful development), Catmull realized the company had started leaning too heavily on its own machinery. Process was supposed to serve creativity, not replace it. The lesson is uncomfortable for managers who love frameworks: you can't systemically guarantee great work. You can only create the conditions for it.

You optimize your process until it runs perfectly — and then the process itself becomes a box that you can't think outside of. The process that enabled you can become the process that traps you.

Every Pixar movie starts ugly. The first screenings of Toy Story were famously unwatchable — Woody was a jerk, the story didn't work, and the animation was primitive. Catmull uses this to argue something many creatives resist: your first attempts will be bad, and this is not a sign that you're doing it wrong. "A movie is never good in its first form," he writes. "It starts out bad. Then the team makes it better." This might seem obvious, but most creative cultures punish the ugly baby phase. Deadlines, investor pressure, and internal expectations all conspire to make people want to skip the ugly part. Pixar's structure is designed to protect the ugly baby — to let a film be terrible for months while the team iterates toward something good. The principle is: fail early, fail often, fail cheaply, and most importantly — fail in private.

You show your rough first draft to someone who doesn't understand the process, they react badly, and you're crushed. You need an audience that understands that "bad" is the starting point, not the destination.

One of Catmull's most important contributions is his insistence on "postmortems" — systematic reviews of everything that went wrong, without blame. After every film, Pixar's teams conduct an exhaustive analysis of what worked and what didn't. The results are shared openly. Mistakes become institutional knowledge rather than personal shame. "Making mistakes is not the same as being a mistake," Catmull writes. "A failed project doesn't mean you are a failure." This sounds like corporate platitude until you see how rigorously Pixar lives it. When *The Good Dinosaur* struggled and ended up underperforming, the director stayed at Pixar and went on to make *Soul*. The culture absorbed the failure as information, not indictment. Most organizations do the opposite: failures are hidden, blamed, or forgotten — which means the same mistakes get made again down the line.

You do postmortems, but they're finger-pointing exercises disguised as reflection. People show up defensive. Real learning requires psychological safety, and psychological safety requires a leader who can admit their own mistakes first.

Catmull writes with sincerity and unusual transparency for a corporate leader, but the book has clear blind spots. He rarely addresses Pixar's failures in depth — *Cars 2* and *The Good Dinosaur* get mentioned but not dissected with the same rigor he applies to the successes. The book also glosses over the impact of Pixar's acquisition by Disney in 2006; Catmull's own role as president of both Pixar and Disney Animation meant he was writing from a position of power that few readers can relate to. The Braintrust model is brilliant but requires a level of trust and mutual respect that most organizations haven't built yet — Catmull doesn't fully address how to bootstrap that culture from scratch. The book also assumes a certain scale; the techniques for a 1,200-person studio may not translate to a startup or a solo creative practice. Some chapters feel padded, and Catmull's willingness to tell long stories about specific films can lose readers who aren't animation enthusiasts.
