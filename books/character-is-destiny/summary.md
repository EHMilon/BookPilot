The room was freezing, and they wouldn't stop asking. In a North Vietnamese prison camp, a young Navy pilot — beaten, starved, isolated — was offered early release. His father was a high-ranking admiral; the Vietnamese wanted the propaganda win. All he had to do was accept. He refused. "You don't take early release," the POWs' code went, "the ones shot down after you are the only ones who get to go home first." John McCain spent five and a half years in the Hoa Lo prison — the "Hanoi Hilton" — much of it in solitary, much of it under torture. And when he was finally broken, signing a confession his captors demanded, he came out of it with a conviction that most of us will never be tested on: character isn't how you act when everything's fine. It's what's left when everything's been stripped away.

McCain, co-writing with Mark Salter, frames *Character Is Destiny* not as a memoir but as a gallery of moral portraits — twenty-three historical figures whose lives demonstrate specific virtues like courage, loyalty, honesty, humility, and purpose. The structure is simple and almost old-fashioned: a virtue, a story, a lesson. The effect, if you let it land, is anything but simple. This is a book that believes goodness is not vague or relative or "whatever works for you." Goodness is specific, visible, and learnable — and it has a shape you can recognize across centuries and cultures.

The book opens with courage, and McCain wastes no time picking his exemplar: Sophie Scholl, the German university student who, as part of the White Rose resistance, distributed anti-Nazi leaflets knowing full well what the cost would be. She was caught, interrogated, and executed by guillotine at age twenty-one. "How can we expect righteousness to prevail when there is hardly anyone willing to give himself up individually to a righteous cause?" she wrote. "It is such a splendid sunny day, and I have to go."

**The Trap** with courage is that we fantasize about it as a single dramatic act — the big speech, the last stand. In reality, courage is almost always cumulative. Scholl didn't wake up one morning and decide to defy the Third Reich. She started with doubt, then discussion, then small acts of conscience. By the time the leaflets were printed, the line had already been crossed. The book's quiet argument is that courage is less about *finding* it in a crisis than about *building* it before the crisis comes.

McCain's choice for honesty is the English martyr Thomas More, who refused to sign the Act of Supremacy recognizing Henry VIII as head of the Church of England. More didn't lead a rebellion. He didn't even speak loudly against the king. He simply — stubbornly, quietly — would not say something he didn't believe. He was imprisoned, and eventually beheaded.

It would be easy to read this as a medieval curiosity, but McCain drags it into modern light with a sharp point: "Honesty is not merely the avoidance of lying. It is the refusal to pretend." The people More was up against weren't cartoon villains. They were pragmatists, people who said "just sign it, everyone knows you don't mean it." That pressure — the pressure to go along, to be reasonable, to not make a scene — is something most of us face in milder forms every day. The lie you tell on a resume. The opinion you nod along with. The silence when someone says something cruel.

We think dishonesty is about big scandals. It's actually about the small, daily erosion of saying what you mean. By the time the big moment comes, your capacity for truth has already been shaped by a thousand small compromises.

McCain's treatment of loyalty is among the book's most personal passages, because he uses it to talk about the POWs he served with. He devotes a chapter to the prisoners who maintained the chain of command even in captivity, who protected the weakest among them, who refused to exploit opportunities that would leave others behind. "Loyalty is a pledge of constancy," he writes, "not to the preferences of others but to the responsibilities of our relationships."

This is where McCain distinguishes himself from the kind of self-help that is really about optimizing your own life. This book is not about making your life better. It's about making your life matter — and those are different things. Loyalty, in his telling, is what stops you from treating other people as instruments of your own success. It's the virtue that makes you stay when staying is hard, not because it feels good but because you promised.

Modern culture treats loyalty as slightly suspect — a sign of naivete or dependency. We celebrate the person who "moves on," who "outgrows" relationships, who makes the strategic choice. McCain quietly argues this is a corruption of freedom. True freedom is the ability to bind yourself to something and keep the promise.

This section is a masterclass in irony: a United States Senator writing a chapter about humility. But McCain earned the right through his own documented failures — his arrogance as a young man, his role in the Keating Five scandal, his temper. The figure he holds up is Abraham Lincoln, whose humility was not self-deprecation but the absence of self-importance. Lincoln could mock himself, could take advice from rivals, could appoint his political enemies to his cabinet because he cared more about the outcome than the credit.

"Humility is not thinking less of yourself," McCain writes, echoing C.S. Lewis. "It is thinking of yourself less." It sounds like a neat aphorism. But the book's deeper point is that humility is the precondition for every other virtue. Without it, you can't learn courage because you're too worried about your image. You can't be honest because your ego has too much invested in the story you tell about yourself. You can't be loyal because loyalty demands you subordinate your interests to something larger.

We confuse humility with weakness or low self-esteem. The genuinely humble person is not the one who can't take a compliment. They're the one who doesn't need the world to arrange itself around their importance.

McCain's writing sharpens when he talks about purpose, perhaps because it's the virtue whose absence he saw most clearly in Washington. He profiles Winston Churchill — not the icon, but the man who spent years in the political wilderness, dismissed as a has-been, writing and waiting. Churchill's greatness, McCain argues, was not his tactical brilliance or his oratory. It was the fact that he had a purpose so deeply internalized that he could endure humiliation and irrelevance and still show up ready.

The book makes an argument that is almost antimodern: purpose is not something you *find* by looking inward. It's something you *choose* by looking outward. "We are all... the subjects of our own attention," McCain observes, "and there is no worse tyranny than a self-absorbed life." The happiest people he's known are not the ones who got everything they wanted. They're the ones who wanted something bigger than themselves.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book was the connection between virtue and freedom. We tend to think of freedom as the absence of constraint — being able to do whatever you want. McCain's argument, running through every chapter, is that real freedom is the ability to do what you *should* do, even when you don't want to. The disciplined person is freer than the impulsive one because their options aren't narrowed by their appetites. The honest person is freer than the liar because they don't have to remember their stories. The courageous person is freer than the coward because they don't spend their energy avoiding the thing they fear.

The book's main weakness is its structure. Twenty-three short biographical sketches, each tied to a virtue, creates a repetitive rhythm that can feel like a sermon series. Some of the figures — George Washington, Mother Teresa — are so familiar that the profiles don't add much beyond the conventional wisdom. McCain's biographical summaries are clean but rarely surprising; he's not a subtle enough writer to find the crack in a well-known story. There's also a distinctly military and masculine tilt to the virtue selection — temperance gets a chapter, but empathy and tenderness are largely absent. The book's moral universe is one of duty, honor, and struggle, with less room for the quieter virtues of care and connection. Readers outside the American conservative tradition may also find the tone occasionally self-congratulatory, as if the book is less a meditation on virtue than a defense of a particular kind of character — the soldier-statesman.

If you're in a position of leadership — formal or informal — especially if you're young and ambitious and feel the tug of expediency, this book will work on you like cold water. If you're already suspicious of virtue language as moralistic or dated, this book probably won't change your mind. But if you're open to an unfashionable argument — that being good is a skill, not a disposition, and that other people's lives are the evidence — it has a quiet power.

1. **Identify your most recent small compromise.** Think of one moment in the last week where you said something you didn't believe or stayed silent when you should have spoken. Write it down. Name what you traded for comfort.
2. **Read the chapter on Sophie Scholl.** It's short — fifteen minutes. Before you read it, ask yourself: who in my life right now needs me to be braver than I am?
3. **Pick one virtue from the book and spend one day practicing it deliberately.** Not all of them. One. If it's honesty, go a full day without a single social lie — no "I'm fine" when you're not, no "I'll call you" when you won't. Notice how hard it is.
4. **Ask someone you trust: "When have you seen me compromise my character?"** Don't defend. Don't explain. Just listen.
