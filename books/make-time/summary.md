Nobody ever looked at an empty calendar and said, "The best way to spend this time is by cramming it full of meetings." Nobody woke up and thought, "Today I'll spend hours scrolling social media." And yet that is exactly how most days get spent — not by conscious choice but by default. Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky know this intimately. As former Google designers, they helped build products like Gmail and YouTube — tools that billions of people use every day. They also saw firsthand how those same tools were making it impossible to focus. So they set out to build a framework not of productivity, but of intentionality. *Make Time* is the result: a four-step daily system for reclaiming your attention from the forces that have been designed to steal it.

The book opens with a diagnosis disguised as a question. The problem is not that you lack willpower. The problem is that you are swimming against a current engineered to exhaust you. The "Busy Bandwagon" — the endless stream of notifications, meetings, email pings, and social media feeds — is not an accident. It is the default operating system of the modern world, designed by the smartest minds in technology to maximize your engagement. Your attention is a battlefield, and you are losing. The solution is not to try harder. It is to redesign your day.

Knapp and Zeratsky's framework has four steps: **Highlight**, **Laser**, **Energize**, and **Reflect**. Each addresses one part of the attention problem.

The **Highlight** is the single most important thing you want to do today. Not the most urgent. Not the thing everyone else demands. The thing that will make this day feel worthwhile. It could be a work task, but it could also be reading with your child, going for a run, or working on a creative project. The authors ask you to choose your Highlight each morning before your inbox gets a chance to choose it for you. The criteria are simple: it should be urgent (something with a deadline), satisfying (something you look forward to), or important (something that moves a meaningful project forward). If none of those fit, ask yourself what you would do if you had no other demands on your time today. That is your Highlight. The trap here is the feeling that the Highlight must be the "most important" thing by some external standard. It does not. It is the most important thing to you, right now. Choosing it is an act of sovereignty.

The **Laser** is how you protect your Highlight from the forces of distraction. Knapp and Zeratsky offer dozens of tactics, but the philosophy behind them is simple: make distraction harder and focus easier. This means removing friction from the thing you want to do and adding friction to the things you want to avoid. They suggest making your phone boring — delete social media apps, switch to grayscale, turn off all notifications except from actual humans. They suggest creating a "distraction-free phone" by moving time-wasting apps off your home screen and hiding them in a folder you have to search for. They advocate for blocking time on your calendar — not just for the Highlight but for the preparation and recovery that surrounds it. The most counter-intuitive tactic is the "drink the glass" method: set a time limit for your Highlight and race against it. Parkinson's Law applies here — if you give yourself thirty minutes to write the proposal, you will write the proposal. The resistance you feel to starting is not a sign that you need a break. It is a sign that you need to start. The only way out is through.

The **Energize** step addresses the physical foundation of focus. This is the part of the book that most resembles a health manual, but the connection to attention is direct: you cannot focus if you are tired, hungry, and sedentary. Knapp and Zeratsky draw on their own experience with the **Design Sprint** — a five-day process for solving big problems that demands intense concentration. They learned that the sprint worked best when participants moved, ate, and slept well. The same is true for everyday focus. Energize tactics include: getting morning sunlight (before looking at your phone), moving your body during the day (even a ten-minute walk changes your cognitive state), eating real food instead of processed snacks, and using caffeine strategically (delay your first coffee by ninety minutes after waking to avoid the afternoon crash). The trap here is seeing exercise and sleep as optional — things you will get to when you have time. But time for focus is not separate from energy for focus. They are the same thing. A tired brain cannot laser.

The **Reflect** step closes the loop. At the end of each day, ask yourself simple questions: Did I do my Highlight? How did the Laser and Energize tactics work? What will I do differently tomorrow? The authors are clear that this is not about judgment. It is about data. Some days you will nail your Highlight. Other days you will get derailed. The point of reflection is not to feel bad about the failures but to learn what works for you. Over time, you build a personalized system — a menu of tactics you know work for your particular brain, your particular schedule, your particular challenges.

What makes Make Time different from other productivity books is its humility. Knapp and Zeratsky do not pretend to have found the one true system. They offer a "tactical menu" — dozens of small experiments you can try. If one does not work, try another. The framework is not a prescription but a laboratory. You are the scientist of your own attention. This flexibility is the book's greatest strength and its most subversive idea: there is no perfect system. There is only the ongoing practice of reclaiming your focus, one day at a time.

The paradigm shift is subtle but deep. Most productivity advice asks you to do more. Make Time asks you to do less — by design. It says you should pick one thing that matters and give it your full attention, not because you are lazy, but because focus is the only way to do anything well. Scattered attention is not a badge of honor. It is a guarantee of mediocrity. The authors want you to stop congratulating yourself for being busy and start measuring yourself by what you actually produce and experience.

**Tonight:** Before going to bed, pick your Highlight for tomorrow. Write it on a sticky note and put it on your phone screen so you see it first thing in the morning. Do not check your calendar, your email, or any notifications before choosing. The Highlight is yours to pick, not theirs.

**Tomorrow morning:** Do not look at your phone for the first thirty minutes after waking. Go outside or sit by a window for ten minutes of sunlight exposure. Delay your coffee by ninety minutes. Then, before opening email, spend your first sixty minutes of the day on your Highlight. Block it on your calendar if that helps.

**Tomorrow afternoon:** Audit your phone. Move every social media and video app into a single folder on the last page. Turn off all non-human notifications. Switch your display to grayscale in your accessibility settings. If you catch yourself reaching for a time-wasting app today, do ten pushups or walk around the block instead.

**Day 2 evening:** Spend five minutes reflecting: Did you do your Highlight? What got in the way? Pick one Laser tactic you will try tomorrow — either a new one or a repeat from today. Make a small adjustment based on what you learned. Write tomorrow's Highlight on a fresh sticky note.

Make Time is the most practical and least dogmatic of the major productivity books, but that pragmatism comes with limitations. The emphasis on daily experimentation means the book never offers a single clear path — which some readers find frustrating. It is a cookbook, not a recipe. The tactics also assume a baseline level of control over your schedule that not everyone has. If your job demands constant availability, or if you are caring for young children, the advice to "block time for your Highlight" can feel like a luxury rather than a strategy. And the authors' background in Silicon Valley tech — the very industry that created the attention economy they critique — casts an ironic shadow over the enterprise. They are offering you tools to escape a prison their former colleagues designed. Still, for anyone who has felt the vague unease of reaching the end of the day having done nothing that mattered, Make Time offers a way back. Not through guilt or shame or Herculean willpower, but through the quiet, daily practice of choosing.
