For over a year, the whole world knew her story and no one knew her name. She was "Emily Doe" — the anonymous victim in the Brock Turner sexual assault case, the woman whose eleven-page victim impact statement was read by millions, translated into multiple languages, and debated in courtrooms, dorm rooms, and living rooms across America. Her words were everywhere. But she was a ghost. And being a ghost was the only way she could survive.

*Know My Name* is Chanel Miller's account of what happened before, during, and after that case. It is not a courtroom drama, though there is plenty of court. It is not a trauma narrative, though trauma is everywhere in it. It is, in its deepest structure, a story about what happens when your life is taken from you and turned into a symbol — and what it takes to claim it back. Chanel Miller was reduced to a victim statement. This book makes her a person again.

> "I am not a victim. I am a survivor. And I want my name back."

**The Trap here** is thinking you know this story. You read the statement. You saw the headlines. You know Brock Turner was a Stanford swimmer, that he got six months in county jail (served three), that the judge was criticized, that the case became a flashpoint in the #MeToo movement. But the news coverage flattened Chanel into an archetype: the Innocent Victim, the Brave Survivor, the Anonymous Heroine. She was never any of those things. She was a 22-year-old woman who liked drawing, had a sister she adored, drank too much at a party, woke up in a hospital, and spent the next three years trying to remember what happened to her. The truth is messier and more specific than any archetype.

The book opens with the night of the assault. Chanel and her younger sister Tiffany went to a Stanford frat party. She doesn't remember much after a certain point — she blacked out from alcohol. She woke up in a hospital bed, disoriented, her body scraped and bruised, with no memory of how she got there. Two Swedish graduate students had found a man on top of an unconscious woman behind a dumpster. They chased him and held him until police arrived. That man was Brock Turner, a Stanford student and swimming star.

The slow realization of what happened — pieced together from police reports, witness statements, and medical records — is the book's most difficult passage. Chanel reads about her own assault in a police file like she's reading about a stranger. She doesn't recognize the girl in the report. That dissociation is the first wound. The assault happened, but she wasn't there for it. She missed her own violation.

> "To be a sexual assault victim is to be the sole owner of a memory that is not your own."

**The Trap here** is expecting a linear healing narrative. Chanel doesn't recover. She doesn't have a breakthrough moment where everything clicks into place. Her healing is jagged, circular, full of setbacks. She has panic attacks. She can't focus at work. She breaks down at unexpected moments. The trap is thinking victims should be inspirational, that their pain should have a narrative arc. Chanel's recovery is real precisely because it's not tidy.

The trial section is devastating not because of the prosecution's arguments but because of the defense's. Brock Turner's lawyers argued that Chanel was "promiscuous" (she had a boyfriend at the time, which was used to imply consent). They argued that she was "unreliable" because she couldn't remember the assault. They argued that Brock's life would be ruined by a conviction — as if the sentencing of a privileged white athlete was the real tragedy. The judge, Aaron Persky, agreed. He sentenced Turner to six months, citing his "lack of criminal history" and the "severe impact" a longer sentence would have on him.

> "Brock Turner's life was a house made of cards, and I was the wind. He would have to deal with the aftermath. So would I."

Chanel's victim impact statement was her chance to speak directly to the court, to Brock, to the world. She read it in fragments — she kept breaking down. The full text was published later and went viral. If you've read it, you know the lines: "You don't know me, but you've been inside me." "The damage is done. No one can undo it." But reading it again in the context of the memoir, surrounded by the events that led to it and followed it, is different. It's not just a document of pain. It's a document of Chanel discovering her own voice in real time.

The aftermath of the trial is where the book becomes something unexpected: a story about sisterhood, about art, about rebuilding a life. Chanel doesn't stay in the courtroom. She quits her job and moves to New York. She falls in love. She starts drawing again. Tiffany, her younger sister, becomes her anchor — the person who was at the party that night, who feels guilty for not protecting Chanel, who carries her own trauma from the experience. The book is dedicated: "For Tiff."

> "In my deepest, darkest moment, I found my voice. And that voice was not small. It was not weak. It was a roar."

**The Trap here** is minimizing the toll on secondary victims. Tiffany's story — the sister who was there, who feels responsible, who witnesses the aftermath — is a reminder that sexual violence ripples outward. The person who held Chanel's hair back while she vomited after the assault, the friend who found her, the sister who couldn't stop her — they all carry pieces of this too.

One of the most striking sections is about namelessness. Chanel describes what it felt like to be known only by a pseudonym. On one hand, the anonymity protected her from the worst of the public scrutiny. On the other hand, it erased her. She watched articles about her own life being written, her own trauma being analyzed, by people who had no idea who she was. She was a character in a story everyone felt entitled to tell — except her. Choosing to reclaim her name, to write this book, was the final act of the trial. Not the verdict. Not the sentence. The naming.

> "I had been the unnamed for too long. I needed to say: this happened to me, Chanel Miller, the daughter of parents who love me, the sister of a woman who saved my life."

Let me be honest about the criticism. *Know My Name* is long — 368 pages of dense, careful prose — and some sections feel repetitive. The aftermath is told in granular detail, and readers who are here for the legal drama may lose patience during the chapters about Chanel's day-to-day life. The book also doesn't engage deeply with structural questions about race, class, and the criminal justice system. Chanel's case became a symbol of systemic injustice, but the memoir stays close to her personal experience. That's her choice — and it's the right choice for a memoir — but readers looking for a broader analysis will need to look elsewhere.

There's also the question of the writing itself. Chanel is clearly talented — her prose is precise, emotional without being sentimental — but the book was written with a co-writer/editor, and sometimes the polish works against the rawness of the subject. The eleven-page victim impact statement is more powerful than any chapter in the book, precisely because it was not refined. The memoir, for all its craft, can't quite match the lightning of that moment.

Anyone who wants to understand what it actually feels like to survive a sexual assault — not the news story, not the statistics, but the lived texture of it. Also, anyone who's ever shared or read that victim impact statement and wondered who the woman behind it was.

Readers who can't handle detailed descriptions of sexual violence or the legal process. The book is explicit in parts and will be triggering.

The image of Chanel, years later, walking into a room and saying her own name. Not "Emily Doe." Chanel. The simple, radical act of being seen.

1. **Write your name.** On a piece of paper, write your full name. Think about what it means to claim it. Chanel had to fight for the right to attach her name to her experience. What would it mean to fully attach yours to yours?

2. **Read the victim impact statement.** It's online. Read it with the context of this book. Notice how specific it is — the details, the metaphors, the particularity. That's what makes it universal.

3. **Believe someone.** If you know someone — or ever know someone — who tells you they've been assaulted, believe them. Don't ask what they were wearing, how much they drank, why they were there. Just believe them. That's the bare minimum. It's also everything.
