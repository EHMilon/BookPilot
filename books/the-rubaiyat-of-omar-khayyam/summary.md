Edward FitzGerald's **Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam** is the most famous translation that is not really a translation, of a poet who was not really a poet, that became one of the most beloved books of the Victorian era and then one of the most mocked. The cultural impact of FitzGerald's Rubaiyat is hard to overstate. It was quoted in parliamentary debates, illustrated by the greatest artists of the era, and carried by British soldiers into the trenches of World War I. It influenced everyone from T.S. Eliot to the counterculture of the 1960s. Omar Khayyam Societies sprang up in England and America. The poem became shorthand for a certain kind of elegant fatalism — a civilized acceptance of life's brevity that appealed to Victorians who had lost their faith in God but not their taste for beauty. The story is almost as good as the poems. Omar Khayyam was an eleventh-century Persian mathematician, astronomer, and philosopher — the man who calculated the length of the solar year to remarkable precision and reformed the Persian calendar. He wrote quatrains (*rubaiyat*) in his spare time, apparently, and they circulated obscurely until FitzGerald, an eccentric Englishman, discovered a manuscript in the Bodleian Library and decided to turn them into English verse. The result, published in 1859, sold almost nothing until it was discovered by the Pre-Raphaelites, championed by Dante Gabriel Rossetti, and eventually became a fixture on every Victorian bookshelf. What they loved was a poem that seemed to say: life is short, the afterlife is uncertain, so drink wine, be happy, and do not take the theologians too seriously.

But the **Rubaiyat** is not that simple, and neither is the experience of reading it. The poem — and FitzGerald's version is effectively a single poem, not a collection — moves through a cycle of moods that mirrors the movement of a single day. It begins at dawn with the call to wake up and seize the moment: "The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ, / Moves on." Then it rises into a celebration of wine, love, and the garden. Then it darkens into meditation on death, the brevity of life, and the silence of the universe. Then it ends with the funeral of the day, the closing of the book, and the strange suggestion that what looked like a poem about despair is actually a poem about the only kind of freedom that is available to us.

The famous opening sets the tone: "Awake! for Morning in the Bowl of Night / Has flung the Stone that puts the Stars to Flight." But it is a serious call, not a chirpy one. The sun rising is also the day dying. Every moment of beauty is also a moment of loss. This is the first idea that FitzGerald's Omar plants and never lets go of: **the present is all we have, and the present is already gone.** "The Bird of Time has but a little way / To flutter — and the Bird is on the Wing." The Victorians who loved this poem read it as a call to pleasure, and they were not entirely wrong. But they were also not entirely right. The pleasure the poem offers is not hedonism. It is the specific pleasure of *looking at reality without flinching*. If the universe is meaningless and brief, then the only sane response is to love what is in front of you. The wine is not escape. The wine is the courage to be present.

**The potency of the moment** is the poem's deepest theme. "The grape that can with Logic absolute / The Two-and-Seventy jarring Sects confute." The grape — the wine — can do what theology cannot: end the arguments. Not by winning them, but by making them irrelevant. When you are truly present — truly drunk on the beauty of a single moment — you do not need to know whether the universe was created or eternal, whether God exists or not, whether paradise is real or invented. Those questions are for people who are not paying attention. The quatrains return again and again to this refusal: "Myself when young did eagerly frequent / Doctor and Saint, and heard great Argument / About it and about: but evermore / Came out by the same Door as in I went." The door of birth. The door of death. Everything in between is argument, and the arguments do not change the door.

Then comes the darkness. The middle of the poem is haunted by transience. "The Worldly Hope men set their Hearts upon / Turns Ashes — or it prospers; and anon, / Like Snow upon the Desert's dusty Face / Lighting a little Hour or two — is gone." The metaphysical horror here is not just that we die. It is that the universe is indifferent. The Potter's Wheel — a recurring image — turns out pots that it then breaks, without concern for what the pots wanted. "The Ball no Question makes of Ayes and Noes, / But Here or There as strikes the Player goes." This is not a comforting vision. It is the vision of a mathematician who calculated the stars and found that the stars do not care. The Rubaiyat is, among other things, a poem about what it feels like to live after the death of cosmic meaning.

The trap that the poem sets for its readers is the temptation to read it as a simple **carpe diem** message. It is not. The poem does not say "seize the day because life is short." It says "seize the day because there is nothing else to seize, and even the seizing is a form of letting go." The difference is subtle and crucial. A carpe diem poem tells you to maximize pleasure. The Rubaiyat tells you to be present to pleasure and to pain, to the rose and to the grave, because they are the same thing seen from different angles. "I came like Water, and like Wind I go." There is no clinging in that line. There is only acceptance.

The honest criticism that follows this book into the present is that it is **Orientalist kitsch**. FitzGerald did not translate Omar Khayyam. He used a few dozen of the several hundred known quatrains as raw material and composed an original poem in the voice of a Persian philosopher. Scholars have found that the original Omar was more pious, more philosophical, and less wine-soaked than FitzGerald's version. The quatrains that celebrate wine in Persian are often allegories for spiritual intoxication, not literal calls to drink. FitzGerald stripped the allegory and left the bottle. Additionally, many of the most famous lines — "A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou" — are FitzGerald's invention with no Persian equivalent. If you come to this poem looking for authentic eleventh-century Persian thought, you will be disappointed. If you come looking for a great English poem inspired by Persian sources, you will find one.

Who should read this? Anyone who has ever looked at a beautiful sunset and felt sad at the same time. Anyone who has struggled with religious doubt and wondered what is left when belief falls away. Anyone who loves poetry that sounds effortless but repays a hundred readings. Who should not? People who need their philosophy to be systematic, their spirituality to be certain, or their literature to avoid the question of death. The Rubaiyat is a poem about death that is disguised as a poem about wine. If you are not ready to think about your own mortality, this book will not let you avoid it. It will hand you a cup and ask you to drink to the end of things. The question the poem leaves you with is not "What is the meaning of life?" It is a better question: "Is it possible to love life without needing it to mean something?" FitzGerald's Omar thinks the answer is yes. The quatrains are the proof — a proof made of rhythm, rhyme, and the courage to say: the rose blooms, the rose dies, and the rose was enough.
