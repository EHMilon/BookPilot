Shunmyo Masuno is a rare combination: a Zen Buddhist priest and a celebrated garden designer. He's built some of Japan's most famous rock gardens, and he brings that same sensibility to his writing — sparse, deliberate, and designed to reveal something you missed because you were looking too fast. *The Art of Simple Living* is a collection of one hundred short lessons, each roughly a page long, each organized around a single principle. It's the kind of book you could read in an afternoon or stretch across months.

But don't mistake its brevity for shallowness. Masuno is working with a Zen tradition that has been refining these ideas for a thousand years. The simplicity of the presentation is the whole point. He's not telling you anything new. He's reminding you of what you already know but have forgotten in the noise of daily life.

Masuno's diagnosis is straightforward: modern life is not hard because the problems are complex. It's hard because we've made everything more complicated than it needs to be. We fill our schedules, our homes, our minds — and then wonder why we feel drained. The solution is not a radical overhaul. It's a series of micro-adjustments that add up to a lighter life.

*"It is not the simple life that is difficult. It is the complex one."*

Masuno's most powerful teaching comes from his work as a garden designer. A Zen rock garden — *karesansui* — uses stones, gravel, and moss to represent mountains, rivers, and islands. The arrangement looks effortless. It isn't. Months of consideration go into the placement of a single stone. But the final result *looks* inevitable — as if the rock had always been there.

His lesson: our lives should be the same. The appearance of effortlessness requires enormous attention to what we include and, more importantly, what we leave out. Every stone that's not there, every space left empty, is a deliberate choice.

Masuno devotes several lessons to the morning. He suggests that how you start your day sets the tone for everything that follows. His recommended morning is almost comically simple: wake up, open the window, greet the morning air, drink tea — slowly — and do absolutely nothing for five minutes before checking your phone or starting your tasks. This is not about productivity. It's about establishing a relationship with the day before you start using it.

**The Trap of "Doing More"** — When we feel overwhelmed, our instinct is to optimize. More productivity tools, better time management, tighter schedules. Masuno suggests the opposite. When you're overwhelmed, the problem isn't efficiency — it's the volume of commitments itself. The solution isn't doing faster. It's doing less.

The book is built around short, immediately applicable insights. Here are the ones that land hardest:

Gratitude is not a feeling. It's a practice. Masuno suggests thanking your chopsticks before you eat — not because the chopsticks are sentient but because the gesture of gratitude reorients your relationship to receiving. When you're grateful for what you have, you stop needing what you don't.

He calls worry "borrowing trouble from the future." Most of what we're anxious about will never happen. The energy we spend in anticipation of disaster is energy we could spend on the present. He suggests a simple technique: when you catch yourself worrying, ask *"Is this happening right now?"* The answer is almost always no.

*"The weight of worry comes from carrying tomorrow's burdens today."*

One of the book's most touching chapters suggests spending time with a tree — the same tree — over the course of a year. Notice it in spring, summer, autumn, winter. The tree doesn't struggle to be what it is. It simply grows, sheds, rests, and grows again. We could do the same if we trusted the cycle.

One of the book's most surprising chapters is about walking. Masuno says we've forgotten how to walk. We walk to get somewhere, while looking at our phones, while thinking about what's next. He suggests a different approach: walk as if walking is the whole point. Feel your feet. Notice the ground. The ancient monks who walked from temple to temple weren't commuting. They were practicing. Every step was a meditation.

*"Walking is not a means of transportation. It is a means of being present."*

Masuno has a chapter on *ichigo ichie* — a concept from the tea ceremony meaning "one time, one meeting." Every encounter, every conversation, every moment is unique and will never happen again. If you treat work meetings as *ichigo ichie* — fully present, fully engaged — they change quality. You listen differently. You respond differently. You stop multitasking because you understand that dividing attention divides life.

**The Trap of Efficiency** — He warns against the modern obsession with multitasking. Zen monks do one thing at a time. They fold their robes with the same attention they'd give to meditating. When you chop vegetables, you chop vegetables. When you answer an email, you answer an email. The fragmentation of attention is the fragmentation of self.

*"Your life is the sum total of the moments you give your full attention to."*

Masuno dedicates several lessons to the concept of *ma* — the meaningful pause or empty space. In Japanese aesthetics, the space between things is as important as the things themselves. The silence between musical notes. The empty space on a scroll. The gap between thoughts.

Modern life has no *ma*. We fill every silence with a podcast, every waiting moment with a scroll, every pause with productivity. Masuno suggests deliberately leaving emptiness in your day — a blank hour with nothing scheduled, a meal eaten alone in silence, a walk without a destination. These empty spaces are not wasted. They're where your life happens.

*"In the space between thoughts, there is peace."*

One of the more subtle lessons is about not fixing things that aren't broken. Masuno observes that modern people have a compulsion to improve, optimize, and intervene. A crack in the pavement, a wilting plant, a slightly off routine — we can't stand it. But Zen teaches that imperfection is not a flaw. It's a feature. The crack in the teacup is its identity. The plant that doesn't bloom perfectly is still alive.

This connects to *wabi-sabi* — the Japanese aesthetic of finding beauty in imperfection, impermanence, and incompleteness. Nothing lasts. Nothing is finished. Nothing is perfect. And that's precisely why it's beautiful.

Masuno also addresses how to end a day. He recommends a closing ritual — a deliberate act of completion. Put your shoes away neatly. Prepare your clothes for tomorrow. Thank the day for what it gave you, including its struggles. The ritual signals to your mind that the day is done, that nothing more is required of you until morning. This is not trivial. The inability to close the day is one of the main causes of insomnia and restless sleep.

He also advises against bringing your work or phone into your bedroom. The bedroom is for rest. If you bring your laptop into bed, your mind doesn't know whether to sleep or solve problems. A clean separation between domains is the foundation of a simple life.

The book's biggest weakness is also its strength. The hundred-lesson format means nothing is explored deeply. A truly profound insight — like *ichigo ichie* — gets the same page count as "tidy your desk every evening." The reader who wants depth will feel unsatisfied. The book is also deliberately, almost aggressively, gentle. If you're looking for a radical life overhaul or a challenging perspective, this isn't it. Masuno is offering comfort, not confrontation.

Some of the advice is culturally specific in ways that are hard to translate. "Thank your chopsticks" works differently in a culture without chopsticks. "Spend time with a tree" is harder to sell to someone in a high-rise apartment. The book's Zen Buddhism is lightly worn but still present — readers who are allergic to any spiritual framing may find the occasional reference to Buddha-nature off-putting.

Also, the sheer number of lessons means some are forgettable. The one-hundred-item format inevitably includes filler. A condensed version of the twenty best lessons would be a stronger book.
