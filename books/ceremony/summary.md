Start here: what if the reason your life feels hollow isn't that you're doing it wrong, but that you've forgotten how to treat everyday moments as sacred? That's the provocation at the heart of Brianna Wiest's *Ceremony* — a book that makes a quiet but forceful case for intentional living in an age that has stripped ritual of its meaning. Wiest isn't selling religion, and she's not asking you to adopt anyone else's traditions. She's asking you to build your own.

Wiest established herself as a distinct voice in the self-help landscape with *The Mountain Is You* and *101 Essays That Will Change the Way You Think*. But *Ceremony* represents something different — a turn toward the poetic and the liturgical. The book is structured as a collection of ceremonies, each designed to mark a transition, honor a wound, or create meaning out of ordinary experience. There are ceremonies for heartbreak and success, for endings and beginnings, for forgiveness and letting go. But more than the ceremonies themselves, the book is an argument for why ceremony matters at all in a world that has forgotten how to create it.

The core wound Wiest is trying to heal is a modern one: we have lost the language and practice of transition. In traditional societies, every major life passage had a ritual attached — birth, death, marriage, coming of age, harvest, solstice. These rituals served a psychological function that we now lack: they gave structure to emotional experience, created shared meaning, and made the invisible visible. We still have the transitions — the ends of relationships, the changes in career, the deaths, the births — but we have lost the containers for processing them. Wiest argues that this absence is not trivial. Without ceremony, we drift through life's most significant moments without fully inhabiting them. We accumulate experiences without integrating them. "You have been through so much that you have not yet honored," she writes. "The ceremony is how you honor it."

**The Trap:** The most common mistake is to confuse ceremony with performance. This is a subtle and pervasive trap in an age of social media. A true ceremony doesn't need an audience, a social media post, or even anyone else's understanding. Wiest is careful to distinguish between ritual for the sake of appearance and ritual for the sake of the soul. The trap is making your ceremony look meaningful rather than *feel* meaningful. The book's emphasis on private, personal, sometimes solitary practice is a deliberate corrective to a culture that has turned even spiritual growth into content. If no one knows about your ceremony, does it still count? Yes, Wiest argues. That's the only kind that counts.

What makes *Ceremony* different from the flood of books about mindfulness and intentional living is its concreteness. Wiest doesn't just tell you to build rituals — she gives you the architecture. Each chapter follows a structure: the reason for the ceremony, the emotional state it addresses, the materials you might need (and they're always simple — a candle, a piece of paper, a few minutes of silence), and the actual steps. There's a ceremony for releasing resentment that involves writing down the grievance on paper you burn in a safe dish. There's one for success that involves listing the people who told you it was impossible and then thanking them — not sarcastically, but genuinely, because their doubt clarified your intention. The concreteness is the point. "Abstract intention is just a wish," Wiest says. "Ceremony is how you turn a wish into a commitment."

One of the most powerful ceremonies in the book is for heartbreak. Wiest asks you to gather objects from the relationship — photographs, gifts, letters — and arrange them in front of you. You light a candle. You speak aloud what you are releasing and what you are keeping. Not everything from an ended relationship needs to be discarded; some lessons and loves stay with you. The ceremony is about discernment, not destruction. You then place the objects you're keeping in one box and the objects you're releasing in another. The box you release goes somewhere you won't see it for a year. The ceremony gives you permission to move forward without pretending the past didn't matter.

Another ceremony that demonstrates Wiest's practical wisdom is the one for welcoming success. She argues that many people sabotage success not because they don't want it but because they haven't created a container for it — the success arrives and they don't know how to *be* in it. Her ceremony involves physically clearing a space in your home (a shelf, a corner, a room), placing objects that symbolize the new chapter, and speaking aloud what you're making room for. The physical act of clearing space creates an energetic opening. Whether or not you believe in energy, the act of physically preparing for something makes it more likely to arrive.

Wiest also gives ceremonies for daily life — for the start of the workday, for the transition from work to home, for difficult conversations, for forgiving yourself. Each one is designed to be short, simple, and repeatable. The beauty of the system is that it doesn't require a special occasion. The mundane becomes meaningful when you choose to mark it. The book includes a ceremony for the full moon, one for the new moon, one for seasonal changes, and one simply for the act of starting something new. The cumulative effect is a life lived with more intention, more presence, and more meaning.

Wiest's writing in *Ceremony* is her most restrained and most powerful. She avoids the breathless inspirational tone that plagues the genre. Instead, the prose is measured, almost solemn, fitting the subject. The book reads like someone walking you through a ritual you didn't know you needed, speaking in a voice that doesn't need to convince you because it trusts the experience itself will do the work. There's a respect for the reader's intelligence and a refusal to oversell.

**The Shift:** What changes when you practice ceremony is not your circumstances but your attention. You begin to notice the thresholds you cross every day — the moment you start work, the moment you come home, the moment before sleep — as opportunities for intention rather than automatic transitions. Wiest's deeper argument is that meaning is not something you find but something you create through repeated, deliberate acts of attention. Ceremony is the technology for that creation. Over time, the practice reshapes your relationship to time itself. Life stops being a blur of undifferentiated experiences and becomes a sequence of moments you have chosen to mark, to honor, to remember.

**Honest Criticism:** *Ceremony* operates on assumptions that won't work for everyone. The book presumes a level of psychological safety that not all readers have. If you're in survival mode — dealing with trauma, abuse, acute crisis — creating a ceremony for your healing might feel premature or even impossible. Wiest acknowledges this obliquely but doesn't give it the weight it deserves. The book also leans heavily on a Western, individualistic framework of self-actualization. Readers from collectivist cultures or religious traditions that already have rich ceremonial practices might find Wiest's DIY approach redundant or even culturally naive. And for skeptics who find ritual performance inherently uncomfortable, the book may feel like an ask you simply can't give.
