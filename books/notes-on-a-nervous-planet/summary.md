After *Reasons to Stay Alive*, Matt Haig had a problem. He'd written a book about surviving depression, and thousands of people wrote to him saying it helped. He was happy — genuinely, cautiously, non-manically happy. But the world was getting harder to live in. The news cycle was a siren. Social media was a pressure cooker. Climate change, political chaos, constant comparison, infinite scrolling — the world itself seemed designed to make you anxious. So he wrote a sequel. Not to the personal story, but to the *diagnosis*. What's wrong with the world, and how do you live in it without losing your mind?

*Notes on a Nervous Planet* is a collection of short essays, reflections, lists, and observations about the relationship between modern life and mental health. Where *Reasons to Stay Alive* was about surviving yourself, this one is about surviving *everything else*.

> "The world is making us more anxious, and we are making the world more anxious. It is a feedback loop, and we need to understand it to break it."

Haig's central claim is simple: **your brain did not evolve for this environment.** It evolved for a small tribe, a predictable territory, and a limited information flow. Now it's flooded with news from everywhere, notifications from everyone, and expectations from every direction. The anxiety isn't a malfunction — it's a normal response to an abnormal environment.

He opens with a vivid metaphor: imagine you're driving a car, and your windshield suddenly shows you every possible accident you could have on your route. Every fender-bender, every pothole, every wrong turn — all displayed in real time. You'd become paralyzed. You'd grip the wheel and sweat. You'd want to stop driving. That's what modern news does to your brain. It shows you every possible catastrophe without giving you any sense of how *likely* each one is. The brain registers the information but loses the probability.

People think staying informed is a civic duty. Haig says there's a difference between informed and *flooded*. Most news is not actionable. If you can't do anything about a story, consuming it is just generating anxiety without purpose. The price of "staying informed" can be your mental health. Choose your sources intentionally. Limit your exposure.

The book's structure is deliberately fragmented — short chapters that can be read in the two minutes between doom-scrolling sessions. Haig uses lists to powerful effect. "Seven ways the news is making you anxious." "Things the mind needs that the internet doesn't provide." "How to read a scary headline." The format is the message: modern anxiety is scattered, and the antidote is scattered too — small practices, not grand philosophies.

One of the most affecting sections is Haig's exploration of **comparison culture**. Social media shows you everyone's highlight reel while you're living your behind-the-scenes. You compare your most anxious moments to someone else's most curated vacation. The gap is what hurts — not the gap between where you are and where you want to be, but the gap between your real life and the AI-filtered illusion of everyone else's.

> "You are not seeing their reality. You are seeing their carefully selected highlights. And you are comparing them to your full-length, unedited, director's-cut version of your own life."

Haig doesn't suggest deleting social media entirely — he's too realistic for that. Instead, he offers a middle path: curate ruthlessly. Mute the accounts that make you feel small. Follow the ones that add depth. Turn off notifications. Schedule your usage. Use the tool without letting it use you.

People think digital detoxes are the answer. Haig says they're a temporary fix that creates a rebound effect. You can't opt out of modern life entirely. The real skill is learning to be *selective* — to use technology intentionally rather than reactively. A digital detox is training wheels. Intentional use is the bike.

Haig writes movingly about **the body as an anchor**. When your mind is spinning about the state of the world, your body is always in the present. The feel of rain on your skin. The weight of a blanket. The rhythm of your own breathing. These sensations are the simplest form of grounding — not a technique to master, but a return to what you already have.

He recalls a moment of utter overwhelm: sitting in a cafe, phone buzzing, brain buzzing, unable to think. He put down the phone, picked up a pen, and wrote: *I am sitting in a cafe. There is a cup of coffee in front of me. The coffee is warm. I can smell it. I can hold the cup. This is real. Everything else is just thoughts about things that aren't here.*

This isn't therapy. It's survival. Haig's approach is deeply unglamorous: you don't transcend anxiety through a breakthrough. You manage it through a thousand small returns to the present.

> "Part of the cure is to face the fact that there is no cure. Anxiety is part of being human. The goal is to make it manageable, not to eliminate it."

The self-improvement industry sells you the dream of a fixed self — a version of you that doesn't feel anxious. Haig says this is a fantasy. Anxiety is part of being alive in a complex world. The goal isn't to eliminate it. The goal is to develop a relationship with it where it doesn't run your life.

One of the most useful chapters is **"Things That Are Better Than The Internet,"** which reads like a manifesto for the analog life. Walking. Reading a physical book. Cooking a meal from scratch. A conversation with no screens. Looking at the sky. Holding someone's hand. Playing an instrument. Gardening.

The list isn't nostalgic — these aren't "better times" complaints. Haig knows the internet is indispensable. But he also knows that your brain needs non-digital input to regulate itself. The absence of analog experience might be the single biggest contributor to modern anxiety. Your brain doesn't know what to do with a world that's all signal, no embodiment.

The honest criticism of this book is that it's uneven. The list format works in places and feels lazy in others — some chapters are barely a paragraph, raising the question of whether they needed to be a chapter at all. The book can feel like a blog collection rather than a considered whole.

There's also a kind of privilege in Haig's advice that he doesn't fully own. "Just take a walk" or "spend less time online" assumes you live in a walkable area, have free time, and aren't dependent on the internet for your livelihood or social connection. For someone working two jobs, living in a high-crime area, or isolated from real-world community, Haig's recommendations can sound like a luxury good.

And the book shares the same limitation as most advice about modern life: it's better at diagnosing the problem than solving it. Yes, the news cycle is toxic. Yes, social media breeds comparison. But what do you do when your entire social life, job, and source of information are online? Telling someone to "log off" is like telling someone in a desert to "go drink water." They know. The question is how.

Turn off all phone notifications for 24 hours. Not just the social ones — all of them. See what happens to the background hum of urgency. Then add one analog practice: fifteen minutes a day with no screens, doing something that engages your senses. A walk without headphones. Cooking without a podcast. Sitting outside without your phone. The world will not collapse if you miss a notification. Your nervous system might quiet down if you let it.
