You don't get to call yourself the happiest man on earth unless you've walked through the worst it has to offer and come out the other side without your humanity burned away. Eddie Jaku survived Auschwitz, survived Buchenwald, survived a death march, survived losing his entire family and his country and every material thing he owned. And then, at ninety-nine years old, he sat down to write a book — not about revenge, not about bitterness, but about *happiness*. Not the shallow kind. The kind you have to earn, shard by shard, in the dark.

*The Happiest Man on Earth* is Jaku's account of a life that touched absolute bottom and somehow kept rising. Born in Leipzig, Germany in 1920 to a Jewish family, he grew up comfortable, assimilated, proud to be German. The Nazis took all of that in stages — first his dignity, then his family, then his name. He became prisoner 172600. He watched his father die in his arms. He survived by becoming invisible, by learning every trick of survival the camps taught, and by making a single, unshakeable decision: that the Nazis would not take his spirit.

---

The book opens with Jaku's childhood in Weimar-era Germany, and its lightness is almost painful to read because you know what's coming. He was a boy who loved machines, who built radios, who fell in love with a girl named Inge who would later become his wife. His family was not particularly religious. They were Germans who happened to be Jewish — a distinction that became irrelevant after 1933. The slow creep of fascism is rendered with chilling detail: the schoolyard taunts that became official policy, the shop windows smashed, the friends who stopped speaking to him. Then came November 10, 1938 — Kristallnacht.

*On November 10, 1938, the world changed forever. I was eighteen years old. I remember the date well because it was the morning after Kristallnacht.*

Jaku's family home was destroyed. He was arrested and sent to Buchenwald. The book's first half is a masterclass in survival under conditions designed to strip you of every trace of humanity. He describes the starvation diet, the forced labor, the casual beatings, the way prisoners became "muscle men" — strong on the outside, hollowed out within. What saved him, he says, was his training as a mechanic. He made himself useful, and usefulness was currency. He learned to repair engines, to fix things no one else could fix, to become indispensable in a system that otherwise would have killed him without noticing. This is the first of his essential tenets: **make yourself useful.** Not successful. Not loved. Useful.

*In life, it is much better to be a repairman than a watchmaker. A watchmaker has only one job. A repairman has a thousand.*

This idea — that adaptability and usefulness are the core of survival — runs through the entire book. Jaku survived Auschwitz by trading mechanical work for food. On the death march from Auschwitz to Buchenwald in the winter of 1945, he survived by tying rags around his feet to protect them from frostbite, by hiding his weakness, by refusing to stop walking even when the man beside him collapsed. He watched thousands die on that march. He buried his own father in a mass grave, digged with bare hands, too exhausted to even weep. The numbness of survival, he writes, was its own kind of death — but it was a death you could come back from.

The book's emotional turning point is liberation. Jaku weighed thirty-five kilograms. He had tuberculosis. His parents were dead, his sister was dead, his extended family — sixty people — had been erased. He had nothing. And yet, in the moment of liberation, he made a decision that would define the rest of his life.

*I decided that day that I would never hate. Hate is a disease that destroys your enemy's humanity, but it also destroys your own. I chose life, and life means love.*

This decision — and he insists it was a *decision*, not a feeling — gives the book its title. Jaku doesn't claim happiness as a gift. He claims it as a deliberate act of defiance against everything the Nazis wanted to reduce him to. Happiness, for Jaku, is not a feeling. It's a practice. It's the decision to wake up grateful for another day, to help others, to find beauty in small things, to refuse bitterness even when bitterness is justified. He calls this **"the secret of happiness"** and it's disarmingly simple: be grateful, be kind, be of service. Every single day.

*If someone does you harm, do not seek revenge. That is the worst possible thing you can do. Instead, live well and be happy.*

The second half of the book follows Jaku's postwar life: emigrating to Australia, building a business, raising a family, living to see great-grandchildren. He speaks at schools and museums all over the world, and his message is always the same. He describes the joy of simple things — a cup of coffee in the morning, a walk in the park, the sound of his grandchildren laughing — with a reverence that could only come from someone who spent years without any of them. He doesn't take a single day for granted. He means it when he says that every morning he wakes up is a bonus.

The book also introduces what Jaku calls **"Eddie's Rules for a Good Life"**: 1) You have a choice to be happy. 2) Never waste time on anger or resentment. 3) Help others. 4) Be grateful. 5) Keep learning. 6) Exercise your body and mind. 7) Have a sense of humor. It's the kind of list you've seen a hundred times in Instagram posts — except Jaku earned every item on it at gunpoint. When he says "choose happiness," he means it in a context where the alternative was literal annihilation.

**The Trap** is the "toxic positivity" reading — the idea that Jaku is papering over horror with platitudes. But the book doesn't earn that criticism. Jaku doesn't skip the horror. He describes it in detail, without euphemism, without making it neat. The happiness he describes is not the absence of pain. It's the *transformation* of it. He doesn't say "just be happy." He says he *chose* to be happy because the alternative was letting the Nazis win twice — once in the camps, and once in his soul.

One genuine weakness: Jaku's frame can feel almost too resilient. The reader who hasn't endured trauma might find his advice accessible, but what about someone with clinical depression? What about the survivor who can't "choose" their way out? Modern trauma science would dispute the idea that willpower alone is sufficient to overcome severe psychological wounds. Jaku also glosses over the moral complexity of survival — he was a prisoner, but he also worked as a mechanic *for the camp*, a position that gave him slightly better conditions. What does it mean to have been useful to the machine that was killing you? The book doesn't explore this. And Jaku's faith in human goodness, while admirable, sometimes reads as a protective blindness — as if refusing to see evil clearly were the same as refusing to hate.

Still, the book's core is undeniable. Jaku is not a philosopher. He's a man who lived the extremes and came to a simple, earned conclusion: kindness matters. Friendship matters. Gratitude is not naive — it's a survival skill. When he says, at ninety-nine, that he is "the happiest man on earth," you believe him not because life was easy, but because he worked for that happiness with his whole life.

*Happiness is not a destination. It is a way of traveling.*
