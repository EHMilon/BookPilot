A friend asks Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie: "How do I raise my daughter as a feminist?" Fifteen suggestions arrive in reply. *Dear Ijeawele* is the result — a letter that became a book, written to a friend who was about to become a mother and needed a guide that didn't exist. It's short, direct, and deceptively radical. Not because any single suggestion is shocking, but because taking them all together requires unlearning almost everything we've been taught about raising children.

The book is structured as fifteen specific proposals. Each one is a paragraph or two — concrete, actionable, grounded in Adichie's own experience growing up in Nigeria and navigating the world as a woman who refused to shrink. She's writing to one person about one baby girl, but she knows the letter will reach millions. That intimacy is the book's secret weapon. It's not a manifesto. It's advice from one friend to another, overheard.

> "Your feminist premise should be: I matter. I matter equally. Not 'if only.' Not 'as long as.' I matter equally. Full stop."

**Suggestion 1: Be a full person.** Don't raise your daughter as a project — a human you're building to meet external standards. Raise her as a person who will inherit a world she can shape. This means being a full person yourself. A mother who sacrifices everything for her children is not noble. She's modeling self-erasure. Your daughter will learn how to be a woman by watching you. If you dim your light, she will learn to dim hers.

**Suggestion 2: Do it together.** This one is for both parents. Adichie rejects the idea that childcare is "helping." It's not helping when a father changes a diaper. It's parenting. The language matters: "help" implies the primary responsibility belongs to the mother. "I'm watching the kids tonight" is not a favor. It's parenthood. Adichie suggests specific, practical shifts — the father should know the pediatrician's number, should pack the diaper bag, should be equally responsible for the mental load.

> "We teach girls that they can have ambition, but not too much ... to be successful, but not too successful, or they'll threaten men. We should teach girls ambition is not a dirty word."

**The Trap here** is thinking equality in parenting will sort itself out. It won't. The default setting of every heterosexual household is the mother doing more. You have to consciously, repeatedly, uncomfortably fight against that default. Adichie's advice: don't praise your husband for doing basic parenting. That praise reinforces the idea that he's doing you a favor.

**Suggestion 4: Reject the language of gender roles.** "Because you're a girl" should never be a reason for anything. Chijioke (the father) should not "help" with cooking. The child should not be told to be "ladylike" or to defer to boys. Every time you attach "because you're a girl" to a command, you're building the cage.

**Suggestion 6: Teach her to question language.** Adichie is especially sharp on this. Language is not neutral — it's the vehicle for all the assumptions we inherit. When someone says a woman is "bossy" and a man is "assertive," your daughter should learn to hear the double standard. When fairy tales end with marriage as the reward, she should learn to ask: "What else could she want?"

**Suggestion 10: Teach her about difference — make difference ordinary.** Don't teach her that difference doesn't matter. That's colorblindness, and it's a lie. Instead, teach her that difference exists and is not a hierarchy. Some people are gay. Some are religious. Some are not. Some have more money. These facts are not judgments. The goal is not to pretend everyone is the same. It's to make difference unremarkable.

> "Teach her that the idea of 'gender roles' is absolute nonsense. Do not ever tell her that she should or should not do something because she is a girl."

**The Trap here** is overcorrecting — raising a girl who is "not like other girls" or who looks down on femininity. Adichie is clear: feminism is not about rejecting feminine things. It's about having the freedom to choose them without penalty. If your daughter loves pink and princesses, that's fine — as long as she also knows she can love trucks and mud. The enemy is not femininity. The enemy is the box.

**Suggestion 13: Teach her to love books.** This is Adichie's most personal suggestion. Books were her liberation. They showed her worlds beyond her own, taught her empathy, gave her language for her experience. A girl who reads is a girl who knows there are other ways to live. A girl who reads has models of resistance, of complexity, of freedom. A girl who reads is harder to control.

> "Teach your child that books are a way to escape, to imagine, to learn. If she can read, she can travel anywhere. If she can read, no one can trap her in a narrow version of the world."

**Suggestion 14: Teach her about sexuality.** This is the most direct chapter. Adichie urges Ijeawele to teach her daughter that her body belongs to her — that no one has the right to touch her without her consent, that pleasure is not shameful, that sex is not dirty. She argues that the "purity" narrative that cultures impose on girls is a form of control, not protection. A girl who knows her own body is less likely to be victimized and more likely to have healthy relationships.

> "Do not ever tell her that her body is something to be ashamed of, or that she should cover it for the comfort of others. Her body is hers. Full stop." Books were her liberation. They showed her worlds beyond her own, taught her empathy, gave her language for her experience. A girl who reads is a girl who knows there are other ways to live. Adichie also addresses the problem of well-meaning relatives. The aunt who says "you're so pretty" instead of "you're so smart." The uncle who tells the girl to help with dishes while the boys play. The grandmother who comments on her weight. Adichie's advice: correct them. Not aggressively, but consistently. "We don't say that in our house." It's exhausting, but necessary. Every uncorrected comment is a teachable moment for the wrong lesson.

> "Teach her that if you criticize X, that does not mean you hate X. The ability to critique something is a sign of thoughtfulness."

One of the most radical suggestions is the simplest: **Let her be herself.** Adichie tells Ijeawele that the goal is not to raise a "feminist" in any prescriptive sense. It's to raise a human who knows her own worth, who can say no, who can say yes without guilt, who can choose her own path. The feminism is in the space you give her to discover who she is without the weight of gender expectations.

> "Your daughter will be whoever she is. Your job is not to shape her into your image of a feminist. Your job is to give her the tools to become herself."

This is harder than it sounds. Every parent has hopes for their child. Every parent projects. Adichie's invitation is to hold those projections lightly — to let the child teach you who she is, rather than the other way around.

Let me be honest about the criticism. *Dear Ijeawele* is very short and, for anyone who's read feminist literature, not especially original. The suggestions are sensible but not novel — you could find most of them in any parenting-while-feminist blog. The book's value is not its originality but its synthesis: it packages decades of feminist thought into a 90-minute read that you can hand to any new parent.

The book also assumes a specific kind of family — two-parent, heterosexual, middle-class, with enough leisure time to be deliberate about parenting. A single mother working two jobs doesn't have the bandwidth to micro-analyze her language choices. A family dealing with poverty doesn't have the luxury of correcting every aunt's comment. Adichie knows this, and the book is addressed to Ijeawele specifically, not to everyone. But the mass publication makes the assumption visible.

And there's the fundamental tension: raising a feminist daughter in a non-feminist world is painful. You're equipping her to see injustice more clearly without being able to protect her from it. The book doesn't sit with that pain long enough. It's relentlessly hopeful, which is a strategy but also a limitation.

New parents — especially parents of daughters, but honestly, parents of sons too. Also, anyone who's ever wanted a simple, warm, practical answer to "how do I do feminism in everyday life?"

People looking for deep feminist theory or structural analysis. This is a practical guide, not a scholarly work.

The phrase "I matter equally. Full stop." — a complete feminist philosophy in five words.

1. **Delete "help."** For one day, notice every time you or someone else says a man is "helping" with childcare or housework. Re-frame it in your mind. He's not helping. He's parenting. He's cleaning. Shift the language.

2. **Catch one "because you're a girl."** Notice it in media, in conversation, in your own head. Just catch it. That's the beginning of dismantling it.

3. **Buy a child a book.** A girl, if you can. A book where the heroine doesn't need rescuing. Send it with a note: "I matter equally. Full stop."
