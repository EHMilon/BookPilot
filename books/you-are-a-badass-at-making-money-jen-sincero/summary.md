You probably have a story about money. Maybe it's "money is the root of all evil." Maybe it's "rich people are greedy." Maybe it's "I'm just not good with money." Whatever your story is, Jen Sincero wants you to know one thing: it's a lie you've been telling yourself, and it's the only thing standing between you and the life you actually want. **You Are a Badass at Making Money** is not a personal finance book. It doesn't teach you how to budget, invest, or save. It teaches you something harder: how to believe you deserve to be wealthy.

Jen Sincero became famous for her profane, irreverent, straight-talking self-help with *You Are a Badass*, and **You Are a Badass at Making Money** applies the same approach to the most loaded topic in most people's lives. Sincero herself went from sleeping on friends' couches in her forties to making millions as a coach and author. The book is her personal testimony plus her coaching framework, and its central claim is as simple as it is uncomfortable: your financial situation is a direct reflection of what you believe you deserve. Change the belief, and the money follows.

Sincero begins with the wound — the specific point in your childhood or young adulthood where you learned that money is bad, scarce, or not for people like you. Maybe your parents fought about money. Maybe you were told that rich people are corrupt. Maybe you internalized the idea that spiritual people shouldn't care about material wealth. Whatever it was, that wound created a belief, and that belief has been running your financial life ever since.

> "Your problem isn't that you don't know how to make money. Your problem is that you don't believe you deserve to have it."

Sincero tells the story of her own money wound — growing up in an upper-middle-class family that suddenly lost everything, watching her parents struggle, internalizing the message that money is unreliable and dangerous. She carried that belief into adulthood and built a financial life that confirmed it: she was broke in her forties, working jobs she hated, terrified of never having enough. The breakthrough came when she realized that no amount of financial strategy could fix a belief system that was rigged against her.

**The Trap:** The "money wound" framework can become another excuse — "I can't be wealthy because of my past." Sincero addresses this by insisting that the wound is real but not permanent. You can heal it. But first you have to face it.

The heart of the book is a series of contrasts between the "rich mindset" and the "broke mindset." Sincero is not subtle about this. She calls it like she sees it:

- Broke people focus on saving. Rich people focus on earning.
- Broke people play it safe. Rich people take calculated risks.
- Broke people wait for the perfect moment. Rich people start before they're ready.
- Broke people think small. Rich people think big.
- Broke people resent success in others. Rich people celebrate it.
- Broke people say "I can't afford it." Rich people ask "How can I afford it?"

The tone is blunt — sometimes abrasive — but Sincero's point is that your financial reality is not separate from your psychology. If you think like a poor person, you will be a poor person, no matter how much money you make. If you think like a rich person, wealth will find you.

> "The only difference between rich people and everyone else is that rich people have made peace with the idea of having money. They don't think it's bad. They don't think they don't deserve it. They just go out and get it."

**The Trap of Scarcity Thinking:** Sincero argues that scarcity is a self-fulfilling prophecy. If you believe there's never enough, you'll behave in ways that ensure there's never enough — by hoarding, by being afraid to invest, by shrinking instead of growing. The shift to abundance doesn't mean being reckless. It means believing that more is possible, and acting accordingly.

This is where the book gets its most controversial. Sincero is a believer in the Law of Attraction — the idea that your thoughts and emotions create your reality. She argues that money is a form of energy, and that you attract wealth by vibrating at the "frequency" of wealth.

If this sounds like new-age woo to you, you're not alone. Sincero knows it sounds woo. She addresses the skepticism directly: call it whatever you want — the Law of Attraction, the power of positive thinking, psychology — the evidence, she says, is that people who believe they can be wealthy are more likely to become wealthy. Whether it's metaphysics or mindset, the practical result is the same.

> "The universe doesn't speak English. It speaks vibration. If you're vibrating at the frequency of lack, you'll attract more lack. If you vibrate at the frequency of abundance, you'll attract abundance."

The frequency framework translates into practical action: monitoring your emotions, catching yourself when you're in a scarcity spiral, deliberately choosing thoughts that feel expansive rather than contractive. Sincero's advice is less about sitting on a mountaintop visualizing money and more about noticing when you're in a fear state and choosing to shift out of it.

**The Trap of Spiritual Bypassing:** The Law of Attraction can easily become a way of blaming people for their circumstances — suggesting that people in poverty simply aren't thinking the right thoughts. Sincero doesn't fully address this criticism, and it's a significant weakness of the book. The framework works best for people who already have some resources; for people in genuine survival mode, "just change your thoughts" can feel insulting.

Beyond the mindset work, Sincero offers practical exercises:

- **The Money Mirror Exercise:** Look at yourself in the mirror and say "I am a moneymaking genius." Feel the resistance. That resistance is the belief you need to break.
- **The Abundance Log:** Every day, write down three things you already have in abundance. Train your brain to see abundance rather than scarcity.
- **The Income Doubling Challenge:** Pick one income stream and figure out how to double it. Don't worry about how right now. Just declare it. Then figure it out.
- **The Fear List:** Write down everything you're afraid of about making more money. Then ask: is this fear rational? What's the worst that could happen? What's the best?

A significant section of the book is about pricing — specifically, the terror of charging what you're worth. Sincero argues that many people undercharge because they don't believe their work is valuable, and this undercharging is a form of self-sabotage.

> "Your prices are a reflection of what you believe you're worth. If you're charging too little, it's not a marketing problem. It's a self-worth problem."

She tells stories of clients who raised their prices dramatically and saw their businesses transform — not because the work changed but because the energy changed. When you charge what you're worth, you attract clients who value what you offer. When you charge too little, you attract clients who don't.

**You Are a Badass at Making Money** is an effective book if you connect with Sincero's voice and are open to her framework. It's alienating if you're not. The Law of Attraction material will lose readers who want science-based, evidence-backed approaches. The book is almost entirely about mindset and has very little practical financial advice — no budgeting, no investing, no debt management. For readers who need actual financial skills, this book will feel incomplete. And the tone, while entertaining to some, can be exhausting — Sincero's relentless cheerleading style doesn't leave much room for nuance, grief, or the genuine complexity of financial struggle.
