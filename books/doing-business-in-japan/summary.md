Every year, thousands of Western businesspeople fly to Tokyo with a briefcase full of spreadsheets and a head full of assumptions. They've read the market analyses, studied the demographics, prepared the PowerPoint. And then they sit down across a table from their Japanese counterparts, and within the first five minutes, they've already made three mistakes they don't know about. The handshake was too firm. They sat in the wrong seat. They started talking business before the formal tea was served. The meeting isn't going badly from their perspective — but from the Japanese side, the negotiation has already entered a different phase that the Westerner doesn't even know exists.

*Doing Business in Japan: A Guide to Success in the World's Third-Largest Economy* — an anthology of contributions from business consultants, expatriate executives, and Japanese cultural specialists — exists to prevent these failures. It's not a deep cultural analysis. It's a field manual: practical, specific, and oriented entirely toward getting results.

The book's first and most important lesson is that Japanese business relationships run on a fundamentally different model than Western ones. In the West, business relationships are transactional: you negotiate terms, sign a contract, and then execute. Trust is a byproduct of successful transactions. In Japan, the order is reversed. You build trust first, over a long period of social interaction, and only then do you discuss business. The contract, when it comes, is almost secondary — the relationship is what matters, and the contract is just a formal acknowledgment of a relationship that already exists.

"The worst mistake a Western executive can make," one contributor writes, "is to hand over a contract at the first meeting. It signals that you value paperwork over people. The Japanese side will be polite, but they will write you off as someone who doesn't understand how things work here."

The Western response to this is often to double down on relationship-building — dinners, gifts, golf outings — without understanding that the relationship they're building is being evaluated by completely different criteria. You can't buy trust in Japan with entertainment. Trust is built through consistency, reliability, and demonstrated respect over time. One lavish dinner means less than six months of showing up on time.

The book dedicates an entire chapter to the *meishi* (business card) exchange, which sounds absurd until you've witnessed its importance. In Japan, your card is not just contact information. It's your identity — your company, your title, your rank, your place in the hierarchy. The way you exchange cards communicates how you understand the social structure of the meeting.

The rules are specific: present your card with both hands, Japanese side facing up. Receive the card with both hands. Read it carefully before putting it away. Never write on a card in the presence of the giver. Never put a card in your back pocket (you'd be sitting on their identity). These aren't formalities. They're signals that communicate whether you understand how hierarchy works in Japan. Get them wrong, and you're revealing that you don't respect the structure that Japanese business depends on.

Western egalitarianism is a liability in Japanese business. Japanese meetings have explicit hierarchies that determine who speaks, when, and to whom. A junior person who speaks out of turn damages not just their own standing but their company's. A Western manager who treats everyone as equals — addressing the junior engineer with the same deference as the department head — introduces chaos into the system without meaning to.

A crucial chapter explains *nemawashi* — the informal consensus-building process that precedes any formal decision. Literally "digging around the roots," nemawashi is the practice of consulting everyone who will be affected by a decision before the decision is made. It's slow. It's indirect. It involves countless conversations that feel like social calls but are actually the real work of governance.

From the Western perspective, nemawashi looks like inefficiency. Why not just call a meeting and decide? But the Japanese perspective is that a decision made without consensus is a decision that will be undermined by everyone who wasn't consulted. The meeting is not where decisions happen. The meeting is where decisions are *announced*. The actual decision happened in the weeks of quiet conversations beforehand.

"The Western executive who pushes for a decision in the first meeting," the book warns, "is asking for something that cannot be given. You cannot get a decision at that stage because the decision hasn't been made yet. It hasn't even begun the process of being made."

Westerners who understand nemawashi often try to speed it up. They think "I'll just talk to the key people myself and build consensus faster." This backfires because you're inserting yourself into a social process you don't fully understand. The consensus isn't just about the decision — it's about maintaining the network of relationships that make future decisions possible. Speed damages the network.

A substantial section of the book is devoted to after-hours business entertainment — *nomikai* (drinking parties) and *enkai* (banquets). These are not optional social events. They are extensions of the workplace where relationships are deepened and hierarchy is both observed and relaxed. The book provides detailed protocols: pour drinks for others rather than yourself, wait for the senior person's toast before drinking, never pour your own drink, match the pace of the senior person.

"The nomikai is where real business happens," one contributor notes. "Not because deals are made there, but because trust is built there. Japanese business partners need to see that you can relax, that you're a human being they can work with over the long term. The meeting is the test. The dinner is where you pass or fail." The book warns against drinking too much — the goal is conviviality, not intoxication — and advises Westerners to pace themselves carefully.

The section on gift-giving is essential reading. In Japanese business culture, gifts create obligations, and the exchange is governed by precise rules about value, timing, and reciprocity. A gift that's too valuable creates an obligation the recipient can't reciprocate, which is an insult. A gift that's too cheap signals that you don't value the relationship. The book provides specific guidelines — gifts from your home region, good quality but not extravagant, wrapped properly — and warns against common mistakes like giving sets of four (the number four is associated with death).

The timing of gift exchange is equally significant. *Oseibo* (year-end gift-giving) and *ochūgen* (mid-year gift-giving) are seasonal traditions that Westerners doing business in Japan are expected to participate in. The book provides a calendar of these gift-giving seasons with practical advice on what to send and how much to spend. The rule of thumb: send something that represents your home region or country, priced at a level that signals respect without creating excessive obligation.

A full chapter is devoted to meeting etiquette, and it's worth memorizing. The seniority seating arrangement — *kamiza* (the seat of honor) farthest from the door — must be observed. The order of entry matters: the most senior person enters first. The order of speech follows hierarchy: the most senior person speaks first, then the next, and so on. A junior person who speaks before a senior violates the social order in a way that Japanese colleagues will notice even if they don't mention it.

The book also covers the role of silence in Japanese meetings — a phenomenon that disorients most Westerners. "Silence does not mean agreement or disagreement," one contributor explains. "It means the person is thinking, and you should give them time to think. Don't fill the silence. Don't rephrase your question. Wait."

The book's practical focus is both its strength and its limitation. It tells you what to do but doesn't always explain why the rules exist. A reader could follow every instruction and still miss the underlying logic, which means they'll be lost when the situation deviates from the script.

The advice is also geared toward a specific kind of business — large corporations, formal negotiations, long-term partnerships. For entrepreneurs, startups, or people working in creative industries, the rigid etiquette described here might not apply with the same force. Japan's business culture is changing, especially in tech and creative fields, and the book doesn't fully capture those shifts.

Some of the advice feels like it's written for the 1980s rather than the present. The section on fax etiquette, for example, has not aged well.

Essential reading for anyone who will represent a company in face-to-face negotiations in Japan. Useful for anyone who will work in a Japanese-owned company outside Japan, as many of the cultural norms transfer. Skip it if you're dealing with Japanese companies entirely through email or remote calls — many of the face-to-face protocols don't apply in the same way to digital communication.

1. **Get your cards right.** Order Japanese-style business cards with English on one side and Japanese on the other. Practice the exchange with a friend: both hands, read the card, place it on the table. It feels awkward until you've done it ten times.

2. **Learn the seating hierarchy.** In any Japanese meeting, the most senior person sits farthest from the door, with their back to the wall. Learn to identify who's senior not by their title but by where they sit. In your own meetings, note the seating and you'll see the hierarchy instantly.

3. **Practice nemawashi in miniature.** For your next non-urgent decision at work, don't call a meeting. Instead, have one-on-one conversations with everyone affected before the group discussion. See how much faster the "meeting" goes when the actual decision has already been made.
