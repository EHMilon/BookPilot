In Buddhist monasteries, there is a tradition of *jirye* — a period of total rest that follows an intensive meditation retreat. After days or weeks of disciplined practice, the monks are told to do nothing. Not meditate, not read sutras, not clean the temple. Just be. Haemin Sunim opens **The Art of Resting** with this image not because he expects his readers to become monastics but because the concept of intentional, uninterrupted rest is so foreign to modern life that it needs a cultural translation. We are people who rest when we collapse, not when we choose to.

**The Art of Resting** is a companion to Sunim's earlier work, but it is not a sequel. It is a deeper dive into a single idea that threads through his philosophy: rest is not a withdrawal from life. It is a way of meeting life more fully. The book is structured as a series of short meditations — on sleep, on silence, on boredom, on the difficulty of stopping when there is still work to be done. Each chapter is a door into the same room: the discovery that our frantic activity is often a way of avoiding something that feels more frightening than exhaustion.

Sunim begins with a confession: he once struggled with rest himself. As a young monk, he would fill his days with study, practice, teaching, and service, believing that spiritual progress required constant effort. It was his teacher who intervened, telling him that he was mistaking agitation for diligence. "You move fast," the teacher said, "but where are you going?" The question stopped Sunim cold. He realized that his busyness was not taking him anywhere — it was simply a way of feeling productive while avoiding the deeper question of what his life was for.

The Trap is that modern culture has turned busyness into a virtue signal. We announce our exhaustion as if it were a badge of honor. We compare our schedule to others' as a measure of importance. Sunim names this for what it is: a form of spiritual bypassing. When you keep moving, you don't have to feel what you would feel if you stopped — the grief, the uncertainty, the emptiness that modern life is designed to keep at bay. The problem is not that you are tired. The problem is that you are using tiredness to avoid yourself.

> "When you are busy, you feel important. But what you are actually feeling is the anesthetic that keeps you from facing your inner life."

Sunim devotes a surprising amount of space to sleep — not as a health optimization topic but as a spiritual practice. He writes that sleep is the body's way of saying: *your efforts are not what sustain you. Something larger sustains you.* Every night, you surrender control. You close your eyes. You let the world continue without you. And in the morning, you wake up to find that the world did not collapse in your absence. Sleep is a daily lesson in trust.

He tells the story of a man who came to him suffering from chronic insomnia. The man was a high-achieving professional who prided himself on his discipline. He could not sleep because his mind kept running through the day's unfinished tasks, tomorrow's meetings, the next quarter's goals. Sunim asked him a question that shifted everything: "Do you believe the world can function without your constant attention?" The man paused. He had never considered that his vigilance might not be needed. Sunim prescribed not a sleep medication but a nightly ritual: before bed, write down everything you are holding in your mind, then speak aloud the words, *I release this. It will be here tomorrow. I am not needed right now.* The insomnia did not disappear overnight, but the relationship to it changed. The struggle became a practice rather than a problem.

> "Sleep is not a waste of time. It is a return to the source from which your energy comes."

One of the book's most counterintuitive arguments is that boredom is not something to escape but something to cultivate. Sunim observes that the moment we feel bored, we reach for stimulation — a phone, a conversation, a snack, a distraction. But boredom, in Sunim's view, is the mind's way of telling you that you have become disconnected from the present moment. The answer is not more stimulation. The answer is deeper attention.

He describes watching a Zen master sit in a garden for hours, apparently doing nothing. A student approached and asked, "Aren't you bored?" The master replied, "How can I be bored when I am meeting the world with fresh eyes every moment? Boredom is what happens when you expect the world to entertain you instead of being present to it." The Trap is that we have outsourced our attention to devices that are designed to prevent boredom at all costs. And in doing so, we have lost the capacity for the kind of quiet attention that reveals the depth of ordinary life. "The opposite of boredom is not entertainment," Sunim writes. "It is interest. And interest is a choice."

Silence, for many people, is terrifying. Not because it is painful in itself but because it removes the noise that keeps us from hearing our own doubts, fears, and longings. Sunim writes about sitting in silence with a group of students during a retreat. Within the first few minutes, several people were crying. The silence had not caused the tears — it had simply removed the distractions that kept the tears at bay.

Sunim's advice is not to seek a silent retreat immediately. It is to find small pockets of silence in daily life — five minutes in the morning before checking your phone, a walk without headphones, a meal eaten in quiet. The goal is not to "achieve" silence but to become familiar with it. Over time, silence shifts from being a space you endure to a space you inhabit. "Silence is not empty," Sunim writes. "It is full of things that your ears cannot hear."

A recurring theme in the book is the danger of turning rest into another performance. Sunim observes that even spiritual practices can become a form of busyness — a checklist of meditation, gratitude journaling, yoga, and self-care that leaves you just as exhausted as the work you were originally fleeing. The Trap is that you optimize your rest, measure your mindfulness, track your sleep quality, and turn the act of slowing down into another project.

Sunim's antidote is brutal in its simplicity: true rest has no goal. You do not rest to be more productive tomorrow. You do not rest to reduce your stress score. You do not rest because it is good for you. You rest because rest is intrinsically valuable — a return to your own being, without purpose, without performance, without measurement. "The moment you rest for a reason, you have turned rest into work."

> "Do not turn your rest into another project. Let it be useless. That is its power."

The book has a subtle but persistent thread about the body. Sunim writes that we live mostly in our heads — planning, analyzing, worrying, remembering. The body becomes a vehicle for carrying the mind around rather than a source of wisdom in its own right. Rest, in Sunim's view, is not just about stopping mental activity. It is about returning to the body — feeling your breath, sensing your weight on the chair, noticing the temperature of the air on your skin.

He describes a practice called "body resting" that is distinct from "mind resting." In mind resting, you try to stop thinking. In body resting, you simply pay attention to physical sensation. The effect is often more powerful because the body exists in the present moment in a way the mind does not. Your thoughts may be in the past or future, but your body is always here, always now. Resting is not about making the mind quiet. It is about joining the body in the only moment that exists.

The book's brevity and aphoristic style mean that it lacks a sustained argument. Readers who want a structured program for rest will find the book more suggestive than prescriptive. Sunim's Buddhist framework is present but lightly applied — the book avoids the deeper philosophical questions about why rest is so difficult in a capitalist economy, focusing instead on the individual's internal experience. The advice can feel, at times, like the perspective of someone whose material needs are securely met. Telling someone who is working two jobs to "embrace boredom" or "find silence" may land differently than Sunim intends. Additionally, the book repeats themes from *The Things You Can See Only When You Slow Down* without adding enough new depth to justify a separate volume. It is more of a variation on a theme than a new argument.

For people who know they need rest but cannot seem to take it. For the perpetually busy who suspect that their busyness is a shield. For anyone who has ever said "I'll rest when I have time" — and never found the time.

Before sleep, write down everything on your mind. Then say aloud: "I release this until morning. My rest is my practice."

Sit for five minutes with no phone, no music, no book, no agenda. If boredom arises, stay with it. Notice what the mind reaches for. Do not reach back.

Take one 10-minute break today that serves no purpose. Do not use it for walking, meditating, stretching, or "self-care." Just sit. Let it be useless. This is the hardest exercise in the book.
