Let's start with an uncomfortable question: when you picture the face of modern feminism, who do you see? If you're being honest with yourself, it's probably a white woman. College-educated. Middle-class. Articulate. She's marching for reproductive rights, posting about the pay gap on Instagram, maybe leaning in at a corporate job. Now ask yourself: does that feminism feel like it was built for you? And if it wasn't—if you're Black, poor, rural, trans, disabled, or any combination of the above—what has feminism actually done for you?

Mikki Kendall's **Hood Feminism** is not a polite correction to mainstream feminism. It's a demolition. And it needed to be written.

> "Feminism isn't just about the right to choose. It's about having choices in the first place."

**The core argument** is devastating in its simplicity: mainstream feminism has become obsessed with the issues that affect privileged women (breaking the glass ceiling, reproductive choice, equal pay for white-collar work) while ignoring the survival issues that affect everyone else (food insecurity, access to healthcare, housing, gun violence, police brutality). Kendall calls this the **One Percent Feminism**—a movement led by and for women who have already secured the basics and are now fighting for luxuries of equality.

The book is structured as a series of essays, each one targeting a blind spot of mainstream feminism. The tone is urgent, angry, and meticulously argued. Kendall isn't interested in finding common ground with the Sheryl Sandbergs of the world. She's interested in showing how their feminism actively harms the women it claims to represent.

**Food insecurity** gets one of the book's most powerful chapters. Kendall argues that you cannot call yourself a feminist while ignoring that millions of women—disproportionately Black and brown—don't know where their next meal is coming from. The fight for a woman's right to be a CEO rings hollow when you're fighting for a woman's right to feed her children. The feminist movement's neglect of food access isn't an oversight. It's a class and race filter—a way of signaling whose problems count.

**Gun violence** is another blind spot. Kendall points out that gun violence is a feminist issue—women are more likely to be killed by a gun in a domestic violence situation than by a stranger. But mainstream feminism has largely avoided the gun control conversation, treating it as a separate issue. The silence, she argues, is a luxury only available to women who don't live in neighborhoods where gun violence is a daily reality.

> "The idea that feminism can be separate from the fight for racial justice is a fantasy."

**Housing, healthcare, education, police violence**—each chapter takes a survival issue and shows how mainstream feminism has either ignored it or made it worse. Kendall's critique of **slumlord feminism** is particularly sharp: the tendency of well-meaning white feminists to move into low-income neighborhoods, displace the existing residents, and then congratulate themselves for their progressive values. Gentrification is a feminist issue. The women being pushed out of their homes are disproportionately women of color. Where is the movement for them?

**The Trap** of mainstream feminism, in Kendall's analysis, is **respectability politics**—the belief that if women just behave well enough, achieve enough, dress and speak and live respectably enough, they'll be granted equality. This is the feminism of "lean in," of "ask for the raise," of "smile more." It works, barely, for a tiny percentage of women. For everyone else, it's a cruel joke. Respectability has never protected Black women from police violence. It has never fed a hungry child.

**The story** that grounds the book is Kendall's own—growing up poor and Black in Chicago, navigating a world where feminism was a word that didn't seem to belong to her. She writes about the women in her community who were doing the work of survival and care that feminism claims to value, but who were invisible to the movement. Her grandmother, her aunts, the women at her church—they were feminists in practice, not in name. The book is an argument for honoring that feminism, the one that shows up at the community kitchen and the housing authority office, not just the one that shows up at the Women's March.

**Why this is hard:** Because Kendall is not asking mainstream feminism to expand. She's asking it to fundamentally reorient. Not "add more diversity to the leadership." Not "include more intersectional language in the mission statement." She's saying: the issues you've been prioritizing are the wrong issues. You've been fighting for the right to compete in a rigged system instead of fighting for the people the system has already discarded. That's not a small adjustment. That's a revolution.

> "Feminism that only addresses the needs of the privileged is not feminism at all."

**The emotional arc** is one of frustration escalating into demand. The early chapters diagnose the problem with a controlled fury. The middle chapters get specific about what's been neglected. The later chapters shift into what solidarity actually looks like—not white women "helping" women of color, but a feminism where survival issues are the center, not the periphery.

**Honest criticism:** The book is more diagnosis than prescription. Kendall is brilliant at identifying what's broken—each chapter lands a blow against a different blind spot—but the solutions she offers are sometimes sketched rather than detailed, leaving readers with a clear sense of what's wrong but less clarity about what to do next. The essay format, while readable and powerful, means some arguments repeat across chapters, and the accumulation can feel relentless rather than constructive. The tone is confrontational by design, which means readers who are defensive about mainstream feminism will feel attacked and may disengage—that's the book's right and arguably its purpose, but it limits its reach to people who already agree. And the "hood feminism" framing, while powerful as a corrective, can occasionally romanticize struggle in a way that treats poverty as authentic and achievement as suspect. Kendall avoids this trap most of the time, but not always.

**What sticks:** The concept that neglect is a form of violence. When mainstream feminism doesn't fight for housing, food, safety, and healthcare for all women, it is not neutral—it is actively choosing which women matter. That reframe is uncomfortable and necessary. After reading this book, you can't look at a white feminist organization's priorities without asking: who are you leaving behind?
