Computer science has a dirty secret: it's not really about computers. It's about *information* — how to process it, store it, sort it, and act on it under constraints of time, memory, and uncertainty. And if that sounds like a description of being alive, you've already understood the premise.

*Algorithms to Live By* does something almost magical: it takes the mathematical tools that computer scientists use to solve abstract problems — scheduling, sorting, caching, game theory — and shows they're the same tools you need to decide when to marry, how to organize your inbox, where to park, and whether to commit or explore. The authors, Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths, are a writer and a cognitive scientist, and together they've written the most genuinely useful book about life I've ever encountered — without once falling into self-help cliché.

---

The book opens with its most famous problem: you're looking for an apartment, a parking spot, a spouse. You need to decide when to stop looking and commit. The mathematical answer, it turns out, is shockingly precise.

The **optimal stopping** problem has a solution known as the **37% rule**. Look at the first 37% of your options without choosing any. Then, take the next option that's better than everything you've seen so far. This gives you the highest probability of picking the best option — about 37%.

*"In any situation where you have a sequence of options and you need to pick one — whether it's a secretary, an apartment, or a parking spot — the optimal strategy is to look at the first 37% to calibrate your standards, then pick the first one that beats everything you've seen."*

**The Trap:** Most people decide too early or too late. The early decider commits to someone mediocre because they don't know better options exist. The late decider keeps searching forever, convinced the perfect option is just around the corner. The 37% rule gives you permission to stop — with the mathematical confidence that you're making the best possible bet given what you know.

Every decision in life is a choice between **exploration** (trying something new to gather information) and **exploitation** (using what you already know to get the best outcome). Should you try the new restaurant or go to your favorite? Stay in your current job or take a risk on a new one? Date new people or commit to the one you're with?

The computer science answer: it depends on your **time horizon**. When you have a long time left, explore. When time is short, exploit. The optimal strategy follows a curve: start with broad exploration, gradually tilt toward exploitation as your horizon shrinks.

*"The younger you are, the more you should explore. The older you are, the more you should exploit. But most people do the opposite — they lock into comfortable routines early and never explore, or they keep searching long after they should have committed."*

**The Trap:** Our culture glorifies exploration — "say yes," "keep your options open," "never settle." But endless exploration is a pathology. At some point, you need to exploit what you've found. The trick is knowing when that point is — and the math says it's when your remaining time is about 37% of the total.

We spend an enormous amount of time organizing things — email, files, books, tasks. Computer science has a crisp insight: **don't sort unless you need sorted order**. In most cases, keeping things roughly organized is good enough. The cost of perfect organization is time you could spend actually doing the work.

*"The time you spend sorting things is time you could spend using them. If you're sorting your book collection by color, you're making a decision that no one will ever thank you for."*

The most efficient approach: **caching** — keep your most-used items close at hand, and don't worry about the rest. Your computer's cache works this way: it stores the data you access most frequently, and lets everything else sit in slower memory. You should do the same. Your desk should hold only what you used in the last week. Everything else goes in a drawer.

**The Trap:** Perfect organization is a form of procrastination. You feel productive because you're doing *something* — but you're avoiding the actual work. A messy desk with a finished project is better than a pristine desk with nothing to show.

Scheduling theory has some brutal truths. The most important: **you can't do everything**. The only question is what to defer, what to do now, and what to cancel.

The optimal scheduling rule is simple: do the task with the earliest deadline first. This sounds obvious, but most people violate it constantly — they prioritize urgent tasks over important ones, or they multitask and destroy their own productivity.

More counterintuitive: **multitasking is a myth**. The computer science term is **context switching** — the overhead of switching between tasks. Every time you switch, you pay a cognitive cost to reload the context of the new task. The cost is high enough that a single focused hour is more productive than four hours of constant task-switching.

*"Context switching is not just inefficient — it's dangerous. The cognitive load of juggling multiple tasks makes you more likely to make mistakes, miss deadlines, and burn out."*

**The Trap:** We glorify busyness. Being "swamped" is a status symbol. But the math says: the more you switch, the less you accomplish. The best strategy is to batch your tasks, avoid interruptions, and — most importantly — learn to say no.

The book's final section deals with the social dimension: how do you cooperate when everyone is optimizing for themselves? The answer from game theory: **tit for tat**. Cooperate on the first move, then mirror what the other person does. If they cooperate, cooperate. If they defect, defect.

Tit for tat wins iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments consistently — not because it's clever, but because it's *nice*, *retaliatory*, *forgiving*, and *clear*. It doesn't start fights, it doesn't back down, it's willing to make peace, and it's simple enough that others can predict its behavior.

*"The best algorithm for social life is not the most sophisticated. It's the one that's clear, consistent, and cooperative first — but not a doormat."*

**The Trap:** People either cooperate too naively (and get exploited) or defect too aggressively (and create enemies). The optimal strategy is calibrated: generous enough to build trust, firm enough to deter exploitation, and simple enough that others can understand your rules.

---

*Algorithms to Live By* argues that the most profound lessons about how to live well come not from philosophy or religion, but from the mathematics of computation — a field that spent decades figuring out how to make optimal decisions under constraints of time, information, and uncertainty. The insights are practical: when to stop looking (37% rule), when to explore vs. exploit (time horizon), how to organize (cache, don't sort), how to schedule (earliest deadline first), and how to cooperate (tit for tat). The meta-lesson is humbling: many of the problems we treat as personal failings are actually universal computational challenges. You're not bad at life. You're just solving a hard problem without the right algorithms.

The 37% rule. I've used it for everything — from apartment hunting to deciding whether to keep reading a book. It's not perfect, but it's better than what most people do (which is either panic-commit or never-commit). The deeper lesson is even more useful: that there *is* a right answer to when to stop looking. Most of us oscillate between FOMO and settling. The 37% rule gives you a principled middle ground.

The book sometimes stretches the analogy too far. Not every computer science problem maps neatly onto human life. The scheduling chapter, while useful, feels like it was written for someone who manages a server, not a human life. And the 37% rule, elegant as it is, assumes you're sampling randomly from a known distribution — which life rarely cooperates with. The book is also relentlessly cognitive — it ignores emotion, intuition, and the messy human factors that override optimal algorithms. The authors would acknowledge this, but still: the book could use a chapter on "when to ignore the algorithm."
