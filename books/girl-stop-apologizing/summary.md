The opening chapter of **Girl, Stop Apologizing** features Rachel Hollis standing in the shower, late for a flight, asking herself why she is so exhausted by a life she chose. It's a disarmingly honest moment, and it sets the tone for a book that is both more focused and more limited than its predecessor. If **Girl, Wash Your Face** was about the lies you believe about yourself, this sequel is about the excuses you make for not pursuing your ambitions. The subtitle — "A Shame-Free Plan for Embracing and Achieving Your Goals" — gives away the structure. Hollis wants to help women stop apologizing for wanting things — for wanting a career, a business, a body they love, a life that doesn't fit the mold of self-sacrificing womanhood.

Hollis organizes the book around ten excuses women make for not going after what they want. The excuses are familiar to anyone who has ever talked themselves out of a dream: "I'm not enough." "I don't have time." "It's not the right season." "What will people think?" "I'm not a good leader." Each excuse gets its own chapter, with a personal story from Hollis's life, a reframing of the excuse as a choice, and a concrete plan for moving past it.

The book is divided into three sections: the excuses (what's stopping you), the behaviors (what you need to do instead), and the skills (how to actually get it done). This structure is cleaner than the first book's and reflects a more mature understanding of her audience — these are women who already know what they want; they need permission and tools, not more soul-searching.

*The Trap:* The "excuses" framework can feel like blaming the victim. Hollis is careful to say that not all barriers are mental — she acknowledges systemic sexism, lack of resources, and real-world constraints. But the emphasis is relentlessly on personal responsibility, and readers who are struggling with genuine structural obstacles will find the framework insufficient.

The chapter on "I'm not a good mom" is the strongest in the book, and it's the one that connects most directly with Hollis's core audience. She argues that the "good mom" standard is a moving target designed to make women feel inadequate. She writes about the night she told her husband she wanted to start a business, and how terrified she was that he would say she was prioritizing work over family. He didn't. But the fear was real, and she argues that the fear itself — the fear of being judged as a bad mother — is the thing that actually makes you a less present parent. The reframe is simple but effective: "You are not a bad mom for wanting more. You are a human being with dreams, and those dreams are part of what makes you a good role model for your children."

*The Trap:* The "good mom" chapter, like much of the book, assumes a nuclear family with a supportive partner. What about single mothers? What about women whose partners are not supportive? What about women who cannot afford childcare? Hollis addresses these questions in passing but does not center them. Her advice works best for the already-privileged.

Part two of the book is about behaviors — the daily habits that separate dreamers from doers. Hollis offers concrete practices:

**Planning backwards from the goal.** Most people plan forward: what can I do today? Hollis argues you should start with the goal and work backward. If you want to write a book in six months, that means one chapter every two weeks, which means five hundred words a day, which means blocking an hour each morning. The reverse-engineering makes the goal feel achievable rather than overwhelming.

**The three-hour morning.** Hollis is a proponent of the "morning miracle" concept — waking up early to work on your most important project before the demands of the day begin. She suggests three hours in the morning before anyone else is awake. Three hours of focused work on your dream, not your obligations.

*The Trap:* The three-hour morning is a classic hustle-culture prescription that assumes everyone has the same energy curve and family situation. Night owls, people with young children, shift workers, and people with chronic health conditions will find this advice not just irrelevant but alienating.

**The "ask for what you want" chapter.** This is perhaps the most practically useful section of the book. Hollis writes about the difference between asking and hinting, between stating your needs and hoping someone will guess them. She offers scripts for asking for a raise, asking for help, asking for what you need in a relationship. The instruction is simple: "You have to actually say the words. No one can read your mind. And if you ask and they say no, you are no worse off than you were before you asked."

The third part of the book covers practical skills: goal-setting, time management, networking, leadership, and dealing with criticism. This is where Hollis is at her best — she has built multiple businesses and she knows what she's talking about. Her advice on goal-setting is concrete: write the goal down, make it specific, put a deadline on it, tell someone else, and check your progress weekly. Her advice on networking is refreshingly honest: "People want to help people they like. Be someone worth helping." Her advice on dealing with criticism is the most useful: "Don't read the comments."

*The Trap:* The skills section is genuinely helpful, but it's also the place where the book's limitations as a one-size-fits-all guide are most visible. Hollis's advice works for building a lifestyle brand, a speaking career, a social media presence. It works less well for becoming a surgeon, a teacher, or a nonprofit director. The book is for entrepreneurs and creators, not for people in traditional careers.

The book's most valuable contribution is its refusal to apologize for ambition in women. Hollis is unapologetic about wanting money, success, recognition, and a beautiful life. She argues that women have been socialized to downplay their desires, to say "oh, this little thing" when someone asks about their business, to deflect compliments and minimize achievements. She writes: "I want to be a huge success. I want to make millions of dollars. I want to be on every bestseller list. I want to be a household name. And I'm not sorry for any of it." The shock value of this confession for many women — who have been taught that wanting is unfeminine — is its own kind of liberation.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is about the difference between wanting something and admitting you want it. Most of us have been trained to hide our desires — to say "I'd like to" instead of "I will," to preface our ambitions with caveats and self-deprecation. Hollis's argument is that the hiding itself is the barrier. You cannot pursue what you will not admit you want. The book made me take a hard look at the things I've been saying "someday" about and ask whether "someday" is just a comfortable lie.

The book is one long motivational speech, and motivational speeches are exhausting to read. There is no chapter where Hollis says "you might fail, and that's okay." The failure narratives are always reframed as learning experiences on the way to success, which means the book has no space for genuine loss, grief, or the possibility that your dream might not work out. The tone can be condescending — "girl, stop apologizing" is the voice of a friend who loves you but is tired of your complaining, and that voice works for some people but not others. The book is also relentlessly focused on outward achievement — money, fame, business growth — with almost no attention to what success costs. Hollis's own marriage ended in divorce a few years after this book was published, which casts a shadow on the "you can have it all" message that the book doesn't have to reckon with.
