You are standing at the door of a conversation that matters, and you don't know what to say. It could be a performance review where you need to ask for a raise, a difficult conversation with a neighbor about their barking dog, or a note to a friend whose father just died. Most people, when faced with these moments, do one of two things: they either blurt something out and regret it, or they say nothing and carry the weight of the unsaid. The "How to Say It" tradition — built on decades of communication research collected across multiple authors and editions — offers a third path. It gives you not just principles, but actual words. **Communication Templates** that you can adapt for almost any situation, from the mundane to the life-changing.

The central insight this body of work offers is that most of us fail at hard conversations not because we lack good intentions but because we lack good scripts. We assume that being authentic means being unscripted — that if we plan what to say, we're being manipulative. The authors argue the opposite: preparation is respect. When you show up unprepared, you are asking the other person to absorb your unfiltered emotions. When you prepare, you are treating the conversation and the person with the dignity of thoughtfulness. The **Communication Templates** are not about tricking anyone. They are about giving you a scaffold so you can focus on what really matters — listening, connecting, and responding — instead of fumbling for words.

At the heart of this framework sits **The Message Model**, a structure for any communication that matters. A good message, whether spoken or written, has three parts: what you see (the facts), what you feel (the emotional impact), and what you want (the request). Most people skip the first two and jump straight to the demand. "I need you to stop doing that." The problem is the other person has no context for why you're asking, so they experience it as an attack. When you start with what you see — "I've noticed that the garage door has been left open the last three nights" — you ground the conversation in observable reality. Then you share the impact: "I worry about the security of the house." Then you make your request: "Could you please make sure it's closed before bed?" The same message, delivered without the model, feels like an accusation. With the model, it feels like a partnership.

The friction most people experience with **The Message Model** is that it asks you to be vulnerable. Telling someone how you feel requires you to admit that you have feelings, which many of us have been trained to hide. The authors acknowledge this openly: the model works only if you are willing to be honest about your emotional experience. If you say "I feel frustrated" when you're actually furious, the mismatch will show in your voice and face, and the conversation will feel hollow. The template is not a mask. It's a map. You still have to walk the territory.

**Empathetic Listening** is the companion skill that makes the model work. You cannot craft an effective message if you haven't understood the other person's reality. And understanding someone else's reality requires more than hearing their words — it requires hearing their emotions, their context, and the needs behind their position. The authors offer a disarmingly simple technique: before you respond to anything someone says, summarize what you heard them say — in your own words, confirming that you understood. This does two things. It forces you to actually listen instead of preparing your rebuttal. And it makes the other person feel heard, which is the single fastest way to lower defensiveness. Most conflicts, the authors argue, are not about disagreement. They are about people feeling unheard. When someone feels truly heard, they stop fighting and start collaborating.

The trap hidden in **Empathetic Listening** is the seduction of "fixing." Many people, especially those who see themselves as helpers, listen just long enough to diagnose the problem and then start offering solutions. The authors are clear: empathetic listening is not about solving. It is about being present. Sometimes the most powerful thing you can say is, "That sounds really hard. I'm sorry you're going through that." No advice. No silver lining. Just presence. The urge to fix is often about your own discomfort with the other person's pain, not about their actual need. Learning to sit with someone else's discomfort without trying to resolve it is one of the hardest communication skills — and one of the most freeing.

The authors draw from a range of real-world scenarios, and the beauty of the template approach is its specificity. They don't say "be more assertive." They give you the exact words: "I appreciate your offer, but I'm going to pass this time." They don't say "set better boundaries." They give you: "I'm not able to take on additional work right now. Let me know if we can reprioritize something." The **Communication Templates** cover everything from declining invitations to delivering bad news, from writing a thank-you note to ending a relationship. Each template follows the same rhythm: acknowledge the context, state your position, offer an alternative or a way forward. The rhythm is not confining. It's liberating — because it means you don't have to invent the wheel every time you open your mouth. You just have to fill in the specifics.

The honest limitation of this approach is that templates cannot save you from the wrong intent. If you use the words of empathy without genuinely feeling it, people will sense the gap. The authors are careful to say that **The Message Model** and **Communication Templates** are tools for people who already want to communicate well. They are not weapons for manipulation. A perfectly crafted message delivered with condescension will still fail. A template used to avoid genuine emotion will feel hollow. The words work only when they are backed by real respect for the person you're speaking to.

The biggest shift this work asks of you is from reactive communication — where you say whatever comes to mind in the heat of the moment — to intentional communication, where you choose your words with the same care you'd choose your actions. You would never walk into a job interview and just "be authentic" without preparing. Why would you treat your relationships with less care? Intentionality is not manipulation. It is respect. It is saying: this conversation matters, so I am going to show up prepared.

Over the next 48 hours, you can make this shift concrete. First, write out one message you've been avoiding. Use **The Message Model**: one sentence for what you see (the facts), one for what you feel (your emotional response), and one for what you want (your request). Read it out loud. Adjust until it sounds like you — not a robot, not a textbook, but you at your most thoughtful. Second, practice **Empathetic Listening** in a conversation today that has no stakes. When someone tells you about their day, don't respond with advice or a similar story. Just say, "That sounds like it was really [adjective they used]. How did that feel?" Then stay quiet. Let them fill the space. Notice how differently they respond when they're not waiting for you to jump in. Third, choose one **Communication Template** from the book's catalog — the apology, the request for feedback, or the boundary statement — and commit it to memory. Use it tomorrow in a real situation. The goal is not perfection. The goal is to feel what it's like to have the right words ready when you need them. Fourth, record yourself saying the message from step one. Listen to your own voice. Does your tone match your words? If you sound angry when you're trying to sound concerned, adjust. The words and the music must match. That congruence is what makes a message land not just in someone's ears, but in their heart.
