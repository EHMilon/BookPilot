Elaine Welteroth became the youngest editor-in-chief in the history of Condé Nast at twenty-nine, running *Teen Vogue* when it transformed from a fashion magazine into one of the most surprising political voices of the Trump era. That transformation was not an accident. It was the result of a woman who had been told her whole career that she was "too much" — too ambitious, too outspoken, too Black for the white-marbled corridors of fashion media — and had finally stopped apologizing for it. **More Than Enough** is her memoir, but it's also a manifesto for anyone who has ever been told that their presence is a problem. The title is the rebuttal. Not "enough." More than enough.

Welteroth grew up in a mixed-race household — her mother is Black, her father is white — and the book opens with a childhood spent navigating the space between worlds. She was too white for the Black kids and too Black for the white kids, too ambitious for the small-town California expectations and not wealthy enough for the fashion world she wanted to enter. The early chapters trace her college years at Howard University, which she describes as a kind of homecoming — the first time she was in a majority-Black space where her intelligence was assumed, not questioned.

The Howard section is crucial because it's where Welteroth learns that she belongs in rooms that weren't built for her. Her professors push her. Her peers challenge her. She learns to code-switch, to navigate, to demand a seat at tables that don't want her. She also learns something more important: that the problem is never that you don't belong. The problem is that the table doesn't know how to include you, and that's not your fault to fix.

*The Trap:* The "Black excellence" narrative often implies that if you just work hard enough, the system will reward you. Welteroth is more honest. She works insanely hard — and she also gets lucky, knows the right people, and benefits from a few white gatekeepers who chose to open doors. Hard work is necessary; it is not sufficient. The book doesn't pretend otherwise.

The middle third of the book is a behind-the-scenes account of the *Teen Vogue* transformation, and it's gripping even if you don't care about fashion. Welteroth was brought in by Amy Astley, the outgoing editor-in-chief, and she inherited a magazine that was losing relevance. The old model — telling teenagers how to look pretty and what to buy — was dying. Welteroth bet on something different: treating young people as intelligent political actors who also liked clothes.

The story of how they landed the Hillary Clinton interview is a masterclass in negotiation and persistence. The story of how they published a piece explaining how to contact your elected representatives — and how the right-wing backlash was immediate and furious — is a study in what it costs to take a stand. Welteroth describes the threats, the hate mail, the panic from Condé Nast executives who weren't sure they'd made the right bet. She held the line.

One of the book's most powerful threads is her relationship with her white male boss, who did not always understand what she was trying to do but trusted her enough to let her try. She writes about the exhausting labor of translating your vision to people who don't share your context — explaining why a story about police brutality belongs in a fashion magazine, why a cover featuring a Black model matters, why political coverage is not a distraction from the brand but the future of it.

*The Trap:* It's tempting to read this as a triumph story — young Black woman conquers white institution. Welteroth is careful to show the cost. She cried in bathrooms. She almost quit. She lost friendships. The wins were real, but they came with a tax that is invisible to the people who only see the headlines.

Welteroth's relationship with her mother runs through the book like a bass line. Her mother raised her to believe she was capable of anything, and that belief is the foundation of everything Welteroth achieves. But the mother-daughter dynamic is not simple — her mother also carried her own fears about Elaine's safety in a world that punishes confident Black women. The chapter where Welteroth calls her mother to tell her about the *Teen Vogue* promotion, and her mother's first response is "Are you sure this is safe?" — that's the whole book in one exchange.

Mentorship is the other through-line. Welteroth dedicates significant space to the women who lifted her — Ava DuVernay, Oprah Winfrey, her professors at Howard — and the lessons she learned from each of them. She also writes about what it means to be a mentor in return, the responsibility she feels toward the young women of color who write to her asking for advice. Her philosophy is simple: if you're the only one in the room, reach back and pull someone else in. If you're one of two, make sure the other person has what they need. If you're one of many, build something that lasts.

*The Trap:* Mentorship talk can easily become cliché — "lift as you climb" and all that. Welteroth avoids this by being specific. She names names. She gives examples of what actual mentorship looks like: the late-night emails, the brutal feedback delivered with love, the recommendation given without being asked.

The book's most honest chapter is about impostor syndrome — but Welteroth reframes it. She doesn't see impostor syndrome as a personal failing or a mental block to be overcome. She sees it as a rational response to being in spaces that weren't designed for you. The feeling that you don't belong is not a delusion; it's a correct observation. The question is what you do with it.

Her answer is a kind of radical self-trust. She tells the story of walking into a meeting at Condé Nast where she was the only Black person, the youngest person, and the only woman who hadn't gone to an Ivy League school. She felt like an impostor. Then she looked around and realized: everyone in this room was admitted by someone. Someone opened the door. She was let in. And now she was the one who could open doors for others.

She writes: "You don't need permission to take up space. You don't need an invitation to belong. You just need to decide that you are worthy of the seat you are sitting in — and then find five other people to sit with you."

*The Trap:* This kind of language — "decide you are worthy" — can sound like hustle culture wrapped in a self-help bow. Welteroth earns the right to say it because she doesn't just say it; she shows the decades of work that made it possible. The self-trust came after the proof, not before.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is that being "more than enough" is not a feeling you arrive at but a decision you keep making. Welteroth didn't wake up one day believing she belonged. She made the decision to act as if she did, over and over, in rooms where everything told her she didn't. The impostor feeling never goes away entirely — she admits she still feels it. But she stopped letting it make her decisions. That distinction — between feelings and decisions — is the book's most useful insight. You can feel like an impostor and still take the meeting. You can feel like you don't belong and still speak. The feeling is information. The decision is action. They are not the same thing.

The book is long — nearly three hundred pages of memoir that could probably have been tightened by fifty. Some sections feel like extended magazine articles that were inserted rather than integrated. Welteroth's writing, while warm and engaging, can be repetitive, especially in the middle section where she seems to be telling the same story about her career from different angles. The book also leans heavily on the language of self-help and inspiration, which may not work for readers who prefer their memoir more literary and less prescriptive. And while she is honest about the costs of her ambition, she is less honest about the privileges — the beauty, the connections, the timing of her rise — that made it possible. The book would have been stronger with a chapter that fully acknowledged the luck involved.
