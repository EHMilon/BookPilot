Rod Judkins teaches creativity at Central Saint Martins in London, one of the world's most famous art schools, and he's watched hundreds of students paralyze themselves with the belief that creativity is a special gift for special people. *The Art of Creative Thinking* is a pugnacious, iconoclastic antidote — ninety short chapters, each built around a single principle, designed to shake you out of conventional thinking and into genuine originality. Judkins writes like a teacher who has told one too many students that they can't copy Van Gogh and calls them a cab. His examples range from Picasso to Einstein to Damien Hirst, and his tone is rude, funny, and deliberately provocative. The book doesn't want to be loved. It wants to make you uncomfortable enough to think differently.

Judkins opens with a grievance: schools, workplaces, and society reward us for being correct, consistent, and predictable. They punish us for being wrong, strange, or inconsistent. But creativity requires exactly the qualities that institutions discourage: the willingness to be wrong, the ability to hold contradictory ideas, and the courage to look foolish. "If you want to be creative," he writes, "you have to stop trying to be right all the time." He tells the story of architect Frank Gehry, who built his own house as a chaotic experiment in deconstruction — and was sued by his neighbors. Gehry's willingness to produce something ugly and disruptive was exactly what produced the aesthetic that later made him famous. Judkins's insight is that creativity isn't about having better ideas — it's about having the *nerve* to follow ideas that make you look like an idiot.

You edit your own ideas before they're even expressed. You think everything you produce needs to be good. Judkins argues the opposite: generate first, judge later. Good ideas come from bad ideas, not from nothing.

One of Judkins's best sections is on "bricolage" — the art of making something from whatever is at hand. He tells the story of Apollo 13: when the oxygen tank exploded, NASA engineers had to invent an air filter using only the materials available on the spacecraft — plastic bags, cardboard, tape. They didn't have time to order the right parts. Constraints forced creativity. Judkins argues that having too many resources is often worse than having too few. "When you have no materials, you have to use your mind." The bricoleur works with what's around them, improvising, adapting, combining. This isn't a second-rate approach to creativity — it's fundamental. The most creative people are not the ones with the best tools; they're the ones who can make something with the worst tools.

You wait for the perfect conditions — the right software, the right studio, the right training. You tell yourself you'd be creative if only you had the resources. Judkins says: use what you have. Now.

Judkins has no patience for the myth of originality. "There is no such thing as an original idea," he writes cheerfully. "Everything comes from somewhere." He points to Picasso, Shakespeare, and Richard Branson — all famous borrowers. The difference between creativity and theft is what you do with what you take. Picasso saw African masks in a museum and transformed them into Cubism. The Velvet Underground took the noise of New York streets and turned it into music. The principle isn't "copy" — it's "transform." Take something familiar, combine it with something unexpected, and produce something that feels new. Judkins's advice is practical: stop worrying about being original. Start worrying about being interesting. Interesting is better than original, and far more achievable.

You kill your ideas by comparing them to what's already been done. "Someone already thought of this" becomes an excuse to do nothing. Judkins says: good. Now make your version.

This is where Judkins gets genuinely interesting. He argues that creative thinking requires holding two contradictory ideas at the same time — and not resolving the tension too quickly. He cites physicist Niels Bohr, who said: "The opposite of a correct statement is a false statement. But the opposite of a profound truth may be another profound truth." The creative mind can live in contradiction: you want the project to be both chaotic and controlled, both simple and sophisticated, both personal and universal. The mediocre mind picks one pole and stays there. "The creative thinker," Judkins writes, "doesn't choose between opposites. They find a way to incorporate both." This is uncomfortable because we're taught to resolve tensions, not to intensify them. But the richest work comes from holding the tension long enough for something new to emerge.

You resolve creative dilemmas too quickly. You pick the safe option. You commit to one direction before you've explored the opposite. Give yourself permission to stay uncertain a little longer.

Judkins devotes multiple chapters to failure, and his treatment is refreshingly unsentimental. He doesn't romanticize failure as a learning experience; he argues that failure is simply part of the process, and that the most creative people fail more often — not because they're worse, but because they attempt more. He tells the story of James Dyson, who made 5,127 prototypes before his vacuum cleaner worked. Not five. Not fifty. Five thousand. Dyson's secret wasn't some special talent for invention; it was a willingness to produce 5,126 failures. The lesson is actuarial: increase your sample size. "The more you try," Judkins writes, "the more accidents happen. And accidents are where real discoveries come from."

You treat each failure as evidence that you're not good enough. You slow down. You get careful. You try to avoid mistakes. But that's exactly the behavior that shuts down creativity. The goal isn't to avoid failure—it's to increase your failure rate so that the wins, when they come, are worth the cost.

Judkins has a wonderful chapter on the creative value of negativity. We're told to be positive, to look on the bright side, to see opportunities instead of problems. But Judkins argues that negative emotions — anger, frustration, boredom, discontent — are powerful creative catalysts. The best art often comes from rage. The best inventions come from irritation with the way things are. "Creativity," he writes, "is often born from a refusal to accept things as they are." He tells the story of how the Dada movement was born from the horror of World War I — not from optimism but from a furious rejection of the civilization that produced the trenches. The principle is unexpected but true: don't always try to be positive. Let yourself be genuinely angry or dissatisfied with the way things are, and use that energy to make something different.

You suppress negative emotions because they feel uncreative. But anger and frustration are telling you that something needs to change — and that's exactly where creativity starts. Don't manage your moods. Listen to them.

The book's greatest strength — its scrappy, provocative energy — is also its limitation. The 90-chapter structure means no idea gets explored deeply. Just when a concept gets interesting, it's over and you're on to the next one. The tone can feel like a drill sergeant's monologue stretched to book length; readers who prefer nuance over aphorisms will find it exhausting. Judkins's examples, while vivid, are heavily skewed toward visual artists and entrepreneurs, and some of his advice assumes a level of boldness that genuinely shy or risk-averse people won't find actionable. The chapter on "how to be a genius" is three pages long and says essentially "be bold and work hard" — which is true but not especially helpful.
