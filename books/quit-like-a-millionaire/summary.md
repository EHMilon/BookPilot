Kristy Shen grew up in poverty in rural China. Her family had nothing — not money, not connections, not a safety net. She immigrated to Canada with her parents, watched them work themselves to exhaustion, and internalized a single, burning lesson: financial security is everything. By her mid-thirties, she had retired with a net worth of over a million dollars, traveling the world with her husband Bryce Leung. *Quit Like a Millionaire* is the story of how she did it, and it reads like a heist movie written by someone with a computer engineering degree and a deep grudge against the financial establishment.

Shen's central argument is that the traditional path — get a good degree, get a good job, buy a house, invest in a 401(k), retire at 65 — is a betrayal disguised as wisdom. She calls it "the standard script," and she argues that it was designed for a world that no longer exists. Houses are unaffordable. Wages are stagnant. Pensions are dying. And the advice your parents gave you about "safe" investing is silently destroying your purchasing power. She and Bryce didn't retire early by being geniuses. They did it by analyzing the math, rejecting the script, and having the audacity to bet on themselves.

Shen is an engineer, and her first gift to the reader is a mathematical framework that makes financial independence feel less like a dream and more like a physics problem. She introduces the concept of your "burn rate" — the total amount of money you spend each year — and argues that your goal should be to accumulate 25 times your burn rate invested in a stock-heavy portfolio. That's the famous 4% rule, popularized by the Trinity Study: if you withdraw 4% of your portfolio each year, historically, your money has a very high probability of outliving you.

The Trap here is that most people focus on the wrong variable. They try to increase their savings rate incrementally — save 10% instead of 5%, get a slightly better return, cut a few lattes. But Shen shows that the real lever is your savings rate as a percentage of your income. If you save 10% of your income, it takes about 50 years to reach financial independence. If you save 50%, it takes about 17 years. If you save 70%, it takes about 9 years. The relationship is nonlinear and explosive. The math doesn't care about your excuses. As Shen puts it, "Your savings rate is your freedom rate."

This is Shen's most controversial argument, and she makes it with data and a personal story. She and Bryce were renting a tiny apartment in Toronto, saving aggressively, when they felt the social pressure to buy a house. They crunched the numbers and reached a shocking conclusion: in most of North America, buying a house is a terrible investment. The costs of maintenance, property taxes, insurance, and interest eat your returns. And the mortgage itself — tens of thousands of dollars a year — could instead be invested in the stock market, where historically it would compound far more reliably.

Shen tells the story of her parents buying a house in rural China decades ago, believing it was the path to security, only to watch its value stagnate while the economy grew around them. She contrasts this with her own decision to rent cheaply, invest the difference, and eventually retire while her home-owning peers were still paying mortgages. The Trap is the cultural assumption that home ownership is inherently smart. It's not. It's a bet on a single, illiquid asset, leveraged with debt, that may or may not appreciate. Shen's argument isn't that you should never buy a house — it's that you should run the actual numbers before doing so, instead of assuming it's the default path.

Once Shen and Bryce hit their FI number, they didn't take up golf in the suburbs. They sold everything and moved to Southeast Asia, where their dollars went much further. Shen calls this "the geography of freedom" — the recognition that the cost of living varies by a factor of 10x between countries, and that you can effectively multiply your retirement income by moving to a lower-cost country.

This is where the book becomes genuinely radical. Shen doesn't just advocate for early retirement. She advocates for decoupling your life from the Western consumer economy. She describes living in Thailand on $1,200 a month — eating incredible food, traveling, building community — while her portfolio grew untouched. The Trap here is the assumption that "retirement" means staying in the same place, living the same lifestyle, just without a job. Shen sees it differently: financial independence is permission to design a completely different life, one that may look nothing like what you left behind.

Shen identifies three obstacles that keep people trapped in the standard script, and each one is worth studying.

**Wall One: The Job.** Most people feel they can't save aggressively because they don't earn enough. Shen's answer is brutal: earn more. She describes how she and Bryce maximized their income as software engineers — negotiating salaries, job-hopping every 2-3 years, freelancing on the side, and always looking for the next opportunity to increase earnings. The conventional advice is to cut expenses. Shen's advice is to increase income first, then cut expenses. That's the order that creates escape velocity.

**Wall Two: The Market.** Fear of investing — specifically, fear of stock market crashes — keeps people in cash or bonds, which quietly loses purchasing power to inflation. Shen's solution is what she calls "the four-way portfolio": a mix of US stocks, international stocks, bonds, and real estate investment trusts (REITs). She emphasizes that market crashes are normal, expected, and actually beneficial to the accumulating investor because you buy more shares at lower prices. Her personal story of living through the 2008 crash as a young investor, watching her portfolio drop 50%, and continuing to buy is one of the most powerful in the book.

**Wall Three: The Social Contract.** This is the hardest one. Your family thinks you're crazy. Your coworkers think you're cheap. Society has a script, and you're deviating from it. Shen recounts the conversations with her parents, who couldn't understand why their daughter — who had a high-paying job — was living like a student. The social pressure to spend is immense, and Shen doesn't pretend it's easy to resist. Her only antidote is clarity: knowing your numbers so well that the social pressure bounces off.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is Shen's calculation of what following the standard script actually costs you. She estimates that the average person who buys a house, pays a mortgage for 30 years, invests in actively managed mutual funds, and retires at 65 will spend their entire working life trading their time for money, and end up with less wealth at retirement than someone who rented cheaply, invested in index funds, and quit at 45. The difference isn't a few percentage points. It's millions of dollars and decades of freedom.

Shen's path required a specific combination of advantages: high-income tech jobs, no children (at the time of writing), a willingness to move to a different country, and a tolerance for risk that many people don't share. Her advice about earning more is correct but not universally applicable — not everyone can become a software engineer. And her dismissal of home ownership, while mathematically defensible, ignores the non-financial benefits of owning a home: stability, community, a sense of rootedness that no spreadsheet can capture.

The book can also feel overly focused on the accumulation phase. The "what now?" of early retirement — purpose, meaning, identity without work — gets less attention than it deserves. And the geographic arbitrage strategy, while brilliant for some, isn't realistic for people with family ties, medical needs, or visa limitations.
