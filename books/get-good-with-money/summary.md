Tiffany Aliche starts *Get Good with Money* with a confession: she lost everything. The recession hit, her school closed, her house went into foreclosure, and she ended up $36,000 in debt with ruined credit. If you're looking for a financial guru who never made a mistake, you're in the wrong place. Aliche — known as "The Budgetnista" — earned her credibility the hard way, crawling back from the bottom with a ten-step plan she wishes someone had given her before the fall.

What makes Aliche distinct in a crowded genre is her focus on the emotional and relational dimensions of money. She's not a spreadsheet person; she's a "heal your relationship with money" person, and she does it without veering into toxic positivity. The book grew out of her Live Richer Academy, an online course she built after realizing that financial literacy isn't just about knowing what a 401(k) is — it's about having enough emotional stability to actually open one.

Aliche organizes the book around ten steps, each building on the last. The structure is deliberate: you can't skip to step eight (investing) if you haven't done step two (budgeting) and step three (saving). She calls this "financial HEALing" — an acronym she constructs from the steps — and while the branding can feel forced, the sequence itself is sound.

Most people jump to the exciting part. They want to invest, or negotiate a raise, or buy real estate. They skip the boring foundational work — tracking expenses, paying off debt, understanding their insurance coverage. And then they wonder why their brilliant investment strategy gets derailed by an emergency they hadn't planned for.

*"You can't build a financial house on a cracked foundation. You have to fix the cracks first."*

Aliche forces you to do the unglamorous work first. Step one is "Figure Out Your Starting Point" — not a vague reflection but a literal net worth calculation. She provides the worksheet. She tells you to write down everything you owe. The act of facing the full picture, she argues, is itself healing. Most people avoid it because it hurts. But you can't fix what you refuse to see.

Aliche rejects the idea that there's one right way to budget. She categorizes people into "budget personalities" — the Saver, the Spender, the Avoider, the Debt-Hater — and helps each type build a system that accounts for their natural tendencies rather than fighting them.

Traditional budgeting advice assumes you're a rational actor. "Just spend less than you earn!" it says, as if the problem were arithmetic and not psychology. If you're an Avoider, a detailed monthly budget will overwhelm you and you'll abandon it by week two. If you're a Spender, a restrictive budget will feel like punishment and you'll rebel against it.

Aliche tells the story of a client who was a classic Spender. Every budget she'd ever tried failed within days. Instead of a traditional budget, Aliche had her set up an automated system: a fixed amount moved to savings and bills immediately on payday, and everything left in checking was hers to spend guilt-free. The constraint wasn't artificial — it was real but invisible. The client stopped feeling deprived because she never saw the savings money; it was just gone.

Match the system to the person, not the person to the system. Aliche gives you permission to be honest about who you are with money and design around your weaknesses instead of trying to eliminate them.

Aliche's approach to debt is familiar — she's a proponent of the debt snowball (smallest debts first) — but she adds a layer most debt advice misses: negotiation. She dedicates an entire chapter to showing readers how to call their creditors and ask for lower interest rates, reduced settlements, or "pay-to-delete" arrangements for credit reporting.

We assume the rules are fixed. Your interest rate is your interest rate. Your credit score is your credit score. But creditors are businesses, and businesses negotiate. The people who get better terms are the ones who ask.

*"The worst they can say is no. And if they say no, you hang up, take a breath, and call again until you get someone who says yes."*

Aliche walks through her own debt payoff journey — $36,000 — and shows how negotiating with creditors saved her thousands in interest and months of repayment time. She provides exact scripts: "Hi, I'm a loyal customer who's struggling with this interest rate. Can you lower it?" The answer is often yes, if you ask correctly.

Debt feels permanent and immovable. Aliche shows that it's actually flexible — the terms you have aren't the only terms available. The act of negotiating itself changes your relationship with debt from passive suffering to active management.

Unlike many personal finance books that assume the only variable is spending, Aliche gives significant attention to earning more. She introduces the concept of a "side hustle" not as a trendy lifestyle choice but as a necessary tool for anyone trying to build financial stability while starting from behind.

People think side hustles require special skills or capital. "I can't do anything," they say. But Aliche pulls examples from her community — dog-walking, tutoring, organizing closets, reselling thrift store finds — and shows that the barrier to entry is lower than most people think. The real barrier is fear of trying.

*"You can't save your way to wealth. You have to earn your way there."*

Aliche introduces the idea of a "financial fill-in" — a temporary side income stream you use to close the gap between where you are and where you need to be. It doesn't have to be your passion. It doesn't have to be forever. It just has to be enough to stop the bleeding and start building momentum.

Aliche devotes more space to insurance than most personal finance books, and it's one of her most valuable contributions. She walks through health insurance, renter's insurance, life insurance, disability insurance, and umbrella policies — not in a salesy way, but explaining what each covers and why you might need it.

Young, healthy people skip insurance because they think nothing bad will happen to them. Then an unexpected medical bill, a car accident, or a disability wipes out years of savings. Insurance isn't exciting, but it's the wall between you and financial catastrophe.

Aliche tells the story of a woman in her thirties who was diagnosed with cancer. She had excellent health insurance through her job but had never bought disability insurance. She survived, but her finances didn't — she couldn't work for a year, her savings evaporated, and she ended up in debt. Aliche's point isn't that you should expect the worst; it's that you should plan for it precisely because you don't expect it.

You stop treating insurance as optional and start treating it as a fundamental part of your financial architecture. Like the basement of a house: invisible, unglamorous, but catastrophic if absent.

What stayed with me was Aliche's insistence that you can't separate financial health from mental health. She calls it "financial wholeness" — the state where money serves your life instead of dominating your thoughts. The people who get rich fastest aren't always the ones who are happiest with their money. Aliche wants you to be both solvent and sane.

The book is long — 368 pages for material that could have been delivered in 250. Aliche's writing can be repetitive, and she leans heavily on her personal brand (The Budgetnista, Live Richer Academy) in ways that sometimes feel like product placement. The ten-step framework is helpful but also somewhat arbitrary — some steps could be combined, and others feel stretched to fill a chapter. Readers who prefer data-driven advice will find the anecdotal approach frustrating.

Anyone who's tried and failed at budgeting before. Anyone who feels shame or anxiety about money. Anyone who needs permission to start from zero — zero savings, zero knowledge, zero confidence.

If you're already financially literate and just need advanced strategies — tax optimization, real estate investing, portfolio management — this is the wrong book. It's a foundation-layer guide, and that's okay.
