**The Diabetes Code** by Dr. Jason Fung

You've been told that Type 2 diabetes is a chronic, progressive disease — that once you have it, you manage it with medication and hope it doesn't get worse. Dr. Jason Fung, a Toronto-based nephrologist, reads that medical consensus like a dare. *The Diabetes Code* is his challenge to everything we think we know about the disease: what causes it, how it progresses, and — most controversially — whether it's reversible. Fung argues that Type 2 diabetes is not a disease of "high blood sugar" that needs to be suppressed with drugs. It's a disease of *excess insulin* — and the treatment is not more medication but removing the cause. The book is as much a critique of the medical establishment as it is a guide for patients, and it rejects the incrementalism that has defined diabetes care for decades.

**The insulin hypothesis.** Fung's core argument is that Type 2 diabetes is driven by **hyperinsulinemia** — chronically high insulin levels. Insulin is the hormone that tells your cells to absorb glucose from the blood. When you constantly eat carbohydrates and sugar, your body pumps out more and more insulin to keep blood sugar down. Over time, your cells become resistant to the signal — they stop listening. Your pancreas cranks up production even further. The result is a vicious cycle: high insulin causes insulin resistance, which causes more insulin, which causes more resistance. The disease, Fung says, is not "high blood sugar." That's a symptom. The disease is "high insulin." Most diabetes treatment focuses on lowering blood sugar with medication — which often raises insulin even further. You're treating the measurement, not the root cause.

**The dangers of dietary guidelines.** Fung goes after the low-fat, high-carb dietary guidelines that have dominated Western health policy since the 1970s. He traces the history: how the US government's dietary recommendations (which influenced the entire world) pushed carbohydrates as the base of a healthy diet, demonized fat, and created the conditions for the obesity and diabetes epidemics. The science behind those guidelines was weak from the start — driven more by politics and personal ambition than by evidence. Ancel Keys, whose Seven Countries Study was used to build the case against fat, was later shown to have cherry-picked data. Meanwhile, the sugar industry paid scientists to downplay sugar's role in heart disease. "Healthy whole grains" is not a neutral statement. For someone with insulin resistance, whole grains spike blood sugar and insulin just like refined carbs, just a little more slowly. The medical advice to eat "small, frequent meals" to keep blood sugar stable is, in Fung's view, exactly wrong — it keeps insulin elevated all day, which is the exact opposite of what a diabetic needs.

**The fructose problem.** One of Fung's most illuminating chapters focuses on fructose — the sugar molecule that's metabolized differently from glucose. Glucose can be used by any cell in the body. Fructose is processed almost entirely in the liver, where it's converted to fat — a process called **de novo lipogenesis**. This liver fat drives **non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)** , which is strongly linked to insulin resistance. Fung argues that sugar (especially high-fructose corn syrup) is uniquely damaging because it simultaneously spikes blood glucose and forces the liver to produce fat. It's a double hit. We think of sugar as "empty calories." Fung says it's worse than empty — it's actively toxic through the fructose pathway.

*"Type 2 diabetes is not a chronic, progressive disease. It is a reversible condition caused by too much sugar in the diet."*

*"The problem is not that diabetics have high blood sugar. The problem is that they have too much insulin."*

*"Eating six times a day is the worst possible thing for someone with insulin resistance."*

*"Fructose is not a harmless sugar. It is alcohol without the buzz."*

*"We did not conquer infectious diseases by building better hospitals. We conquered them by understanding their cause and removing it."*

**Dietary interventions that work.** Fung doesn't leave you with the diagnosis. His prescription is threefold: reduce carbohydrates, incorporate intermittent fasting, and stop eating all the time. The most effective intervention, he argues, is **therapeutic fasting** — periods of zero or very low calorie intake that force the body to burn stored glucose and reset insulin sensitivity. His clinic has published case studies of patients reversing Type 2 diabetes entirely through fasting protocols. The mechanism is simple: when you stop eating, insulin drops, and your body starts using its stored energy. Over time, insulin resistance improves, and the pancreas can rest. People are terrified of fasting. We've been told that skipping a meal is dangerous, that the brain needs constant glucose, that breakfast is the most important meal of the day. Fung addresses each objection with data. The fear of fasting is cultural, not physiological.

**Low-carb vs. low-fat: the evidence.** Fung walks through the major dietary trials comparing low-fat and low-carb approaches, including the DIRECT study and the A TO Z weight loss trial. The consistent finding: low-carb diets produce better weight loss, better blood sugar control, and better cardiovascular markers than low-fat diets — despite the fact that low-carb diets include more fat and often more calories. This is the **calorie hypothesis** problem: if a calorie were a calorie, low-fat should work. It doesn't. The reason, Fung argues, is that not all calories are metabolized the same way. Carbohydrates trigger insulin; fat does not. Fung also highlights meta-analyses showing that low-carb diets consistently outperform low-fat diets for both weight loss and HbA1c reduction at 6 and 12 months, even when calorie intake is not restricted. The "calories in, calories out" model ignores the hormonal reality of how the body processes food. Doctors who advise "eat less, move more" are following an outdated model. It fails for the same reason that cutting your budget doesn't fix debt: it treats the symptom, not the structural imbalance.

The idea I couldn't shake after reading *The Diabetes Code* is the **insulin hypothesis** — not because it's new (the science has been there for decades), but because the implications are so radical for how we eat. The idea that "healthy whole grains" might be making people sick is hard to accept if you've been told your whole life that carbs are the foundation of a good diet. The second idea that stuck is **fructose as hepatotoxin**. The comparison to alcohol is powerful: both are processed by the liver, both cause fatty liver, both flood the reward system. We think of sugar as a treat. Fung makes a case that it's closer to a poison when consumed in modern quantities.

Dr. Fung writes with a crusader's energy, and that cuts both ways. He simplifies the medical establishment's position to the point of caricature — not every doctor is clueless, not every dietitian is captured by the sugar industry. The evidence for diabetes reversal through fasting is promising but still based on small studies and clinical cases, not large randomized controlled trials. Fung's tone can be strident, and he dismisses opposing views too quickly. Some of his recommendations (extended fasts of 24-42 hours) are not safe for people on certain diabetes medications without medical supervision, and the book doesn't emphasize this enough. Readers with Type 1 diabetes need completely different approaches that the book only briefly addresses. Also, the book is repetitive — Fung makes the same points about hyperinsulinemia and dietary history multiple times.

Anyone with Type 2 diabetes, pre-diabetes, or a family history of either. Also anyone who wants to understand why the low-fat advice of the last 50 years has failed. People on insulin or sulfonylureas should read this *with their doctor*, not alone — fasting can be dangerous without medication adjustment. Type 1 diabetics will find the recommendations largely inapplicable. Anyone satisfied with current dietary guidelines and unwilling to reconsider will resist this book at every turn. And if you're looking for a moderate, balanced approach to dietary change, Fung's intensity can feel overwhelming.

**Why this matters now.** The diabetes epidemic is accelerating, not slowing. Fung's argument — that the medical establishment is treating symptoms instead of causes — has only grown more urgent since the book was published. Whether or not you accept all his conclusions, the conversation he starts is one that anyone with metabolic disease needs to have. With an open mind.

1. For the next two days, eliminate all added sugar and refined carbohydrates. If it comes in a package and has more than 5g of sugar per serving, don't eat it.
2. Extend your overnight fast to 16 hours: finish dinner by 8 PM, don't eat again until noon the next day. Coffee, tea, and water are fine. Notice how you feel.
3. Identify the hidden sugar sources in your diet: breakfast cereal, yogurt, sauces, bread, "healthy" snacks. Write a list. This week, find replacements for the top three.
4. Look up your latest blood work. Find your fasting insulin if available (not just glucose) or your HbA1c. If your HbA1c is above 5.7% or your fasting glucose is above 100, take the dietary changes in this book seriously — not next year, this week.
