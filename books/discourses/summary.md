Your boss is incompetent. The government is corrupt. Your partner said something cruel. And you're furious — because you believe things should be different. Epictetus, the Stoic philosopher who started life as a crippled slave in first-century Rome, would tell you that this fury is the problem. **Discourses** is his collected teachings, preserved by his student Arrian, and it represents one of the most practical and confrontational philosophies ever written. It doesn't care about your feelings. It cares about whether you're living in alignment with reality.

Epictetus's central claim is simple enough to fit on a bumper sticker and disorienting enough to spend a lifetime grappling with: some things are up to us, and some things are not. What's up to us: our judgments, choices, desires, aversions. What's not up to us: our health, reputation, wealth, other people's opinions, and basically everything external. All human misery, Epictetus argues, comes from mistaking the second category for the first — trying to control what we cannot, and neglecting to take responsibility for what we can.

This is the cornerstone of Epictetus's philosophy. He opens the *Enchiridion* (his shorter manual) with it, but the *Discourses* develops it at length.

"Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in a word, whatever are not our own actions."

The implications are radical. If someone insults you, the insult is not the problem — your judgment about the insult is the problem. If you lose your job, the loss is external, but your response to the loss is yours to choose. If you're sick, the illness is not in your control, but your attitude toward it is.

"People are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them," Epictetus writes.

This is not a comforting philosophy — not in the way that says "everything will be okay." It's a demanding philosophy that says "you can handle anything, but only if you stop demanding that reality conform to your wishes."

Using the dichotomy of control as an excuse for passivity. Some modern readers interpret "some things are not up to me" as permission to stop trying. That's not what Epictetus means. You can act in the world — you can pursue goals, work for justice, love people. What you can't do is control the outcome. The distinction is between doing your best and being attached to the result.

Epictetus didn't see philosophy as abstract theorizing. He saw it as medicine for the soul — practical treatment for the disease of misdirected desire.

"Men's sins are not the product of their will but of their sick condition," he says. "Just as we do not become angry at the sick, so should we not become angry at those who err."

This medical metaphor pervades the *Discourses*. A student who comes to Epictetus wanting to learn philosophy is like a patient coming to a doctor wanting to feel better. They want relief from anxiety, anger, disappointment. Epictetus prescribes the same medicine: align your desires with reality.

His teaching sessions (the dialogues that make up the *Discourses*) are relentless in their refusal to offer comfort without confrontation. When a student says "I want to be a philosopher," Epictetus asks: "Then are you prepared to be laughed at? To be talked about behind your back? To be thought a fool? Because that's the price of admission."

He tells a story of a man who came to him distressed about what others thought. Epictetus's response: "If you seek to please everyone, you will please no one — least of all yourself."

Wanting the benefits of philosophy without the costs. People want to be Stoic without the social friction Stoicism creates. If your values genuinely differ from the culture around you, you will be seen as strange. Epictetus says: accept it. The alternative is to let other people's opinions run your life, which is slavery.

Epictetus developed the concept of **assent** as the critical moment between stimulus and response. An impression arrives — "That person insulted me." Before you react, you have a choice: do you assent to the impression? Do you accept it as true and let it dictate your response?

This is the gap where freedom lives.

"First, don't let the impression carry you away," Epictetus advises. "Say to it: 'Wait for me a little. Let me see what you are and what you represent. Let me put you to the test.'"

The practice is simple: when something happens, pause before reacting. Ask: "Is this under my control? Is my judgment about it accurate? Do I want to be carried away by this?" The pause is everything. Without it, you're a puppet of circumstance. With it, you have the possibility of choice.

Epictetus uses the metaphor of an actor on stage. You don't choose your role — you might be cast as a rich man or a beggar, a king or a slave. But you do choose how well you play your role. The excellence is not in the role but in the performance.

Thinking the pause means suppression. Epictetus is not asking you to repress your emotions. The Stoics didn't believe in suppressing feelings — they believed in examining them before you act on them. The distinction is crucial. Feelings are impressions. They happen to you. But your response is your choice.

Some of Epictetus's most challenging advice concerns love and attachment. He warns that loving anything external means handing your happiness over to chance.

"When you kiss your child or your wife, say to yourself that you are kissing a mortal," he writes. "If she dies, you will not be disturbed."

This sounds cold — and it is, to modern ears. But Epictetus's logic is consistent: if your peace depends on the continued existence of someone you love, you've made yourself vulnerable to the inevitable. Everyone you love will die. Every relationship ends, through loss or change or time. If your happiness is built on the permanence of things that are impermanent, you've built on sand.

The follow-up is the crucial part: this doesn't mean you shouldn't love. It means you should love fully, while recognizing the nature of what you're loving. The Stoic doesn't love less. They love without the illusion of permanent possession.

Taking Epictetus's advice as permission to be emotionally distant. The modern reader often uses Stoic ideas to avoid vulnerability — "I'm not attached, so I don't care." That's not Stoicism; that's avoidance. Epictetus loved his students, his friends, his family. He just did not make his happiness conditional on their presence.

The *Discourses* return repeatedly to the theme of **freedom**. Epictetus, who was literally enslaved for part of his life, had unique insight on this.

"Is freedom anything else than the power to live as we wish?" he asks. "Do you wish to live without error? Then be free — but that requires discipline."

The paradox at the heart of Stoicism: freedom comes through discipline. The person who cannot control their impulses, their desires, their reactions is not free — they're a slave to their own conditioning. The person who can choose their responses, who can say no to destructive desires, who can face difficulty without being broken — that person is free.

Epictetus frames discipline as training. Every difficult moment is an opportunity for practice. A rude remark is a chance to practice patience. A loss is a chance to practice acceptance. A temptation is a chance to practice self-control.

"Circumstances do not make the man," Epictetus says. "They reveal him to himself."

Thinking freedom means doing whatever you want. Epictetus inverts this: freedom means wanting what's actually good for you, not what your impulses demand. The person who can't say no to dessert, who can't resist checking their phone, who can't hold their tongue during an argument — that person is not free. They're driven by forces they haven't examined.

You cannot control what happens to you. You can control how you respond. All misery comes from confusing these two categories — from trying to control the uncontrollable and neglecting the one thing that is actually yours: your judgment. The discipline of assent, the acceptance of impermanence, the willingness to play whatever role you're given with excellence — these are the practices that lead to a life that cannot be broken by circumstance.

The line that refuses to leave my mind is from the *Enchiridion*, but it's the distillation of the entire *Discourses*: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them." I've tested this in small moments — a cancelled flight, a rude email, an unexpected expense. Every time, the disturbance came from my judgment ("this shouldn't be happening") rather than the event itself. Recognizing this doesn't make the disturbance disappear. But it creates a sliver of space between the event and my response, and that sliver is everything.

The *Discourses* is not a polished book — it's lecture notes, preserved by a student. The organization is loose, the topics repeat, and some sections feel like fragments. Epictetus's relentless style can be exhausting — he's always confronting, always demanding, and some readers will find his tone hectoring rather than inspiring. The philosophy's insistence on emotional self-control can be harmful when taken too far, especially for people who need to process genuine trauma rather than discipline their responses. The Stoic framework is also deeply individualistic — it's better at helping you endure injustice than at motivating you to fight it.
