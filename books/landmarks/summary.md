The premise of *Landmarks* is so simple you almost miss how radical it is: collect the words that people have used for centuries to describe the natural world — the specific, tactile, dialect-rich vocabulary of shepherds, farmers, fishermen, and hunters — and show how losing those words is a kind of ecological loss as real as deforestation. Robert Macfarlane's follow-up to *The Old Ways* is, on one level, a dictionary. It contains thousands of terms for landscape features, weather patterns, animal behaviors, and plant structures, drawn from the regional dialects of the British Isles and beyond. But it's also a lament, a love letter, and a piece of cultural conservation. It argues that language loss and ecological loss are the same wound.

The book is structured in thematic chapters — flatlands, uplands, waterlands, woodlands, coastlands — each bookended by an essay from Macfarlane that sets the context, then followed by a "word-hoard": a glossary of terms for that particular landscape. The word-hoards are where the book lives. You'll find out that *amphidromous* means a fish that migrates between fresh and salt water, that *crizzle* is the noise made by ice when it freezes, that *smeuse* is the hole in a hedge made by the repeated passage of an animal. You'll learn that the Scots have 100 words for snow but not the ones you think — not "powder" and "packed" but *flindrikin* (a brief snow shower), *skelf* (a large snowflake), *unbrak* (the beginning of a thaw). You'll learn that there's a word for the sound of wind in the rigging of a ship (*chirm*) and a word for the light that precedes dawn on a heathland (*larking*). 

> "We have lost a vast linguistic commons: a store of words for the natural world that once salted every regional dialect."

Macfarlane's argument is that this loss matters. Not because we should all start speaking like 18th-century shepherds, but because language shapes perception. When a thing has a name, you see it. When it doesn't, it becomes invisible. The decline of regional landscape vocabulary is not just a cultural curiosity — it's a perceptual catastrophe. If you don't have a word for the particular way light falls through winter branches, do you even notice it? If you've never heard the word *aquabob* (a dagger of icicle hanging from a rock), does that icicle exist for you?

The book's intellectual ancestor is the work of Jacqueline McMakin, Nan Shepherd, and the great nature writer Barry Lopez, but its closest relative is the *Old English* word-hoard tradition — the idea that a culture's relationship to its environment is encoded in its vocabulary. Macfarlane goes further: he argues that the loss of this vocabulary is not accidental but structural. Industrial agriculture, urbanization, and screen-based life have systematically dismantled the conditions in which this language could survive. You can't have a word for the particular texture of a ploughed field after rain if you never walk on a ploughed field.

**The Trap** is thinking that this book is about nostalgia. It's not. Macfarlane isn't saying the past was better. He's saying the past *knew things* that the present has forgotten, and that forgetting has consequences. The word-hoards are not museum pieces. They are tools. They are ways of paying attention that the modern world has made obsolete but which remain available to anyone willing to learn them. You can still learn to read a landscape the way a shepherd reads it. You just have to want to.

> "Words are stones, but they also have their own weight and resistance."

The personal essays that frame each section are quieter than Macfarlane's usual work — less narrative, more reflective — but they contain some of his best writing. The essay on waterlands describes a childhood spent by rivers, learning the names of ripples and eddies. The essay on coastlands recounts a walk along a tidal estuary where the boundary between land and sea kept dissolving, and with it, the boundary between self and world. The essay on woodlands is a meditation on canopies — the way a forest ceiling filters light and sound, creating a world beneath the world.

The book also includes short interludes on writers who have shaped Macfarlane's thinking — "guardians" he calls them: Nan Shepherd (the Scottish writer whose book *The Living Mountain* is a companion volume to this one), Roger Deakin (author of *Waterlog*), J.A. Baker (author of *The Peregrine*). These interludes are love letters to writers who understood that precise observation is a form of love.

The Nan Shepherd interlude is particularly moving. Shepherd spent her life walking the Cairngorm mountains and wrote *The Living Mountain* in the 1940s, but it wasn't published until 1977. Macfarlane describes her as someone who "had no interest in conquering mountains" but instead wanted "to be inside the mountain, to know it from within." Her language for the mountain — *the cupped stillness, the light that lifts, the body of the hill* — is exactly the kind of vocabulary *Landmarks* is trying to preserve. Macfarlane's guardians section is the book's secret heart: a reminder that the work of paying attention has always been done by someone, somewhere, and that we're all just adding words to a conversation that started long before us.

Macfarlane's own writing style changes in *Landmarks* — shorter sentences, more fragments, as if he's trying to match the compressed precision of the words he's collecting. In the coastal section, he describes walking a tidal causeway: "The path was scallop-shelled with the prints of oyster-catchers. The sea was a mile out. The air smelled of salt, mud, and the particular iodine of bladderwrack." It's craft, not accident — he's showing you what attention looks like when you have the language for it.

The weakness of *Landmarks* is structural. The word-hoards, while fascinating, are not really readable in sequence. You dip in, you marvel, you move on. But as a reading experience, the book can feel like a reference work trying to be a narrative. The essays are too short to develop full momentum, and the glossaries are too long to sustain attention. There's a tension between the book's two impulses — to tell a story and to preserve a record — that it never quite resolves.

The book also includes a moving coda on the death of Macfarlane's friend, the naturalist Roger Deakin, who died during the writing of *Landmarks*. Macfarlane's farewell to Deakin is also a farewell to a certain kind of English nature writing — personal, precise, deeply in love with the particular. "He taught me," Macfarlane writes, "that the best way to love a place is to learn its names." That line is the book in miniature.

Macfarlane also sidesteps the hard questions about who gets to use this language. Some of the dialect terms come from communities that no longer exist, or exist only as fragments. Is it appropriation to collect them? Or preservation? He gestures at the question but doesn't fully engage it.

The book's most luminous section is the chapter on waterlands — rivers, estuaries, fens, and coasts. Macfarlane describes the language of water with the kind of passionate precision that makes you want to immediately go find a river. Words like *aquabob* (a dagger of icicle), *whisht* (a sudden stillness in the reeds), *glaur* (wet clay or mud). He writes about the Norfolk Broads, the Somerset Levels, the Thames estuary, and in each place, he finds a local vocabulary that captures something universal: the way water shapes not just the landscape but the minds of the people who live near it.

But these are limitations of form, not of vision. *Landmarks* does something that very few books attempt: it changes the way you see the world. After reading it, you start noticing the *smeuse* in every hedge, the *crizzle* on every puddle. You start looking for words to describe what you're seeing, and in looking for words, you see more. And that, Macfarlane would argue, is the first step toward caring. Because you don't protect what you don't see. And you don't see what you can't name.
