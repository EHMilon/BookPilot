Here's a thought experiment: What if your anxiety isn't a malfunction? What if it's a habit — a loop your brain learned, automated, and now runs without asking permission? And what if the cure isn't fighting the habit, but understanding how habits work and using that knowledge against them?

Jud Brewer is a psychiatrist and neuroscientist at Brown University who studied under some of the top researchers in addiction and habit formation. Before writing about anxiety, he spent years treating addiction — cigarettes, food, opioids — using mindfulness-based approaches. The bridge between addiction and anxiety is his great insight: both are driven by the same neural mechanism, the same feedback loop of trigger, behavior, reward. Your anxiety is not a disorder. It's a habit your brain learned. And if you can learn a habit, you can unlearn it.

Brewer introduces the **anxiety habit loop**: you encounter a trigger (a work email, a social situation, a physical sensation), you react with worry (the behavior), and you get a reward (temporary relief from the uncertainty). Relief feels good, so the brain files it away: *trigger + worry = relief*. Next trigger, the brain automatically reaches for worry. Not because worry is productive — but because it's temporarily relieving.

> "Worrying is the brain's way of trying to feel in control. The problem is, it's an illusion of control that keeps you stuck."

The genius of this framing: it removes blame. You're not "an anxious person." You're not broken. Your brain learned a strategy that worked in the short term and generalized it. The problem is that the long-term cost — chronic anxiety, sleepless nights, depleted energy — is enormous.

Most people try to stop worrying by *telling themselves to stop worrying*. This never works because you're trying to suppress a habit. Suppression makes the habit stronger. Think of the last time someone told you not to think about a pink elephant. What did you think about? Brewer's approach doesn't involve suppression. It involves *seeing the loop clearly enough that you lose interest in running it.*

Brewer draws heavily on **habit reversal training**, the gold standard for breaking unwanted habits. The process has three steps:

1. **Map the loop** — identify trigger, behavior, reward
2. **Generate alternative rewards** — find something more satisfying than worry
3. **Practice the new loop** — repeatedly, until it becomes automatic

The first step is deceptively powerful. Brewer has patients create an anxiety map: a simple diagram of what triggers their worry, what the worry behavior looks like (rumination, checking, reassurance-seeking, avoidance), and what the immediate reward is. Most people discover that their worry is driven by a desire for *certainty*. Worrying feels productive even when it produces nothing.

> "The brain learns by reward. If you can find a bigger, better reward for curiosity than for worry, you can rewire the loop."

Step two is where Brewer makes his most original contribution. He introduces **curiosity as an alternative reward** to worry. When the anxiety trigger appears, instead of defaulting into the worry loop, you practice being curious about the physical sensation of anxiety. Where do you feel it in your body? What shape is it? Does it change with your breath?

Curiosity breaks the automaticity of the worry loop. You can't be curious and panicked at the same time — they use different neural circuits. And curiosity is inherently rewarding, which means it can compete with the reward of "relief from uncertainty" that worry provides.

Brewer tells the story of a patient named Sarah who had crippling health anxiety — every bodily sensation triggered a frantic Google search. Her anxiety loop: sensation → "this could be cancer" → Google → temporary reassurance → more searching an hour later. The reward was a brief dip in fear. But the cost was hours of daily compulsive searching and escalating dread.

Brewer had her create a loop map. Then he introduced the curiosity step: when the urge to Google appeared, she would sit with the sensation in her body for 60 seconds and describe it without judgment. Tightness in her chest. Heat. A sense of pressure behind her eyes. She wasn't trying to make the sensation go away — she was just curious about it. The urge to Google faded within two minutes. Over time, the loop weakened.

People think mindfulness is about becoming calm. Brewer says mindfulness is about *seeing clearly*. Calm is a byproduct, not the goal. If you try to be calm, you're still struggling against your experience. If you're just curious about what's happening, you've dropped the struggle. That's the true exit from the anxiety loop.

Brewer also introduces the concept of **reward-based learning** as the engine of all behavior change. Your brain doesn't care about your long-term goals. It cares about what feels good or relieving *right now*. Understanding this is the key to changing any behavior. You don't fight the brain's reward system — you work with it. You find a reward that's more satisfying than worry.

What reward could possibly compete with the temporary relief of "I've figured out what's wrong"? Brewer's answer: **present-moment awareness**. When you're fully present, you're not worried about the past or the future. And being present, even for a few seconds, feels *good*. It's a subtle reward — but it's real. And it doesn't come with the hangover of anxiety.

This is not abstract philosophy. Brewer ran clinical trials showing that a mindfulness-based app (his own, called Unwinding Anxiety) reduced anxiety levels significantly in medical patients. The app uses exactly the loop-mapping + curiosity approach described in the book. The results were comparable to standard CBT and medication — but with no side effects and no therapist required.

The book extends the framework beyond worry to **emotional eating, substance use, and relationship patterns**. Brewer argues that all addictive and compulsive behaviors follow the same neural architecture: trigger → behavior → reward. The specific reward differs — relief from anxiety for worriers, pleasure for eaters, escape for drinkers — but the loop is identical. Changing any of them requires mapping the loop, finding a better reward, and practicing.

> "Once you clearly see the mechanism of your anxiety, it's hard to keep falling for it. The same way a magician can't fool you once you know the trick."

Brewer's approach works beautifully for habit-based anxiety — the kind that's triggered by specific cues and maintained by reward. But not all anxiety is habit-based. Trauma-based anxiety, for example, can't be mapped into a neat loop and "curiosity-ed" away. Trauma lives in the body differently. Brewer acknowledges this briefly but doesn't address it with the same depth.

There's also a risk of **spiritual bypass** with the curiosity approach. "Just be curious" is great advice for moderate anxiety. For a full-blown panic attack where your amygdala is screaming DANGER, being curious about your tight chest isn't going to cut it. You need the amygdala-specific approaches from Pittman's work alongside Brewer's framework.

The book is heavily focused on the app-based research, which can feel like a long sales pitch. The science is real, but the constant references to "in our studies with our app" can make you wonder how much is genuine insight and how much is marketing.

Where the book shines: mapping your anxiety loops is an activity you can do right now, without the app. Draw three columns on paper. Triggers. Worry behaviors. Short-term reward. Do this for three different anxiety episodes. You'll likely see a pattern you've never noticed. That's the first step. Then, next time you feel the urge to worry, pause for 60 seconds and get curious about the physical sensation in your body. Don't try to change it. Just observe. See if the urge to worry weakens.

Brewer's great gift is his reframing of anxiety from an enemy to a teacher. Your anxious habits are not signs of weakness — they're your brain's best attempt to solve a problem with the tools it had. The problem is that the tool (worry) was meant for temporary use, and your brain got stuck with it on. Brewer shows you how to see that you're the one holding the tool. And you can put it down.
