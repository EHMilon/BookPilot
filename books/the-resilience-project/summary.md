Hugh van Cuylenburg had a sister who was dying.

Not in the dramatic, movie-of-the-week sense. His younger sister Georgia was in the grip of a severe eating disorder, and Hugh — a primary school teacher in Australia — was watching her disappear. He'd tried everything to help her. Nothing was working. And somewhere in the middle of that helplessness, he stumbled onto something that would become his life's work.

*The Resilience Project* isn't a textbook. It's a story — Hugh's story — of how he went from a teacher who felt useless in the face of his sister's suffering to someone who has taught resilience to hundreds of thousands of students, athletes, and corporate leaders. The book opens on a trip to rural India, where Hugh spent time volunteering in a village called Ladakh. The people there had almost nothing in material terms — no electricity, no running water, no possessions worth protecting. But they were, by any measure, happier than the people he knew back home in Australia. They laughed more. They worried less. They took care of each other.

What was their secret?

Hugh came back from India obsessed with this question, and the answer he arrived at forms the core of his resilience framework: three pillars. **Gratitude. Empathy. Mindfulness.** GEM.

It sounds simple, even reductive. But the power of the book is in how Hugh unpacks each one — not as abstract concepts, but as daily practices that are deceptively hard to sustain.

**Gratitude** is first, and Hugh doesn't mean the gratitude of a journal entry (though he recommends that too). He means the active, muscle-building practice of noticing what's good when everything around you suggests otherwise. He tells the story of a man named Danny, a pastor he met in Queensland whose son died of a drug overdose at seventeen. The grief nearly destroyed Danny. But he started a practice: every morning, he'd name one thing he was grateful for. Not about his son's death — that would be absurd — but about his son's life. The way he laughed. The camping trips. The small moments. Over time, the practice didn't erase the grief, but it created space next to it for something else. Danny describes it as "building a muscle you didn't know you had."

Hugh's own practice is simpler. Every night at dinner, his family goes around the table and says one thing they're grateful for from the day. The rule: you can't repeat something someone else said. He admits the first few weeks felt forced. But after a year, it became the thing everyone in the family looked forward to. The kids started noticing moments during the day — "that's going to be my gratitude tonight" — which means they were training their brains to scan for the positive.

**The Trap with Gratitude:** Hugh is careful to avoid the toxic positivity trap. Gratitude isn't about pretending bad things aren't happening. It's not about comparing your suffering to someone else's and concluding you have no right to complain. Real gratitude practice, he emphasizes, involves the *coexistence* of pain and appreciation. You can be grieving and still grateful. You can be anxious and still grateful. The one doesn't cancel the other.

**Empathy** is the second pillar, and this is where Hugh's experience as a teacher shines. He writes about a student named Ben who was being bullied relentlessly. The school's response was punishment for the bullies, which made things worse. Hugh's approach was different: he worked with Ben's entire class on empathy — not just "how would you feel if that was you?" but exercises designed to *produce* the feeling of connection. Eye contact without talking. Sitting in silence next to someone who's sad. Learning to listen without preparing your response.

There's a story about Hugh visiting a football team — the Melbourne Football Club, which was at rock bottom when he started working with them. The players were talented but fractured. They didn't trust each other. Hugh introduced a simple exercise: each player had to share something vulnerable with the group. Something they were ashamed of or embarrassed by. The first few sessions were awkward, but something shifted when the captain went first. He talked about his fear of failure, his anxiety before games, the pressure he felt to be perfect. After that, the whole team opened up. They went from a group of individuals protecting themselves to a group of people who knew each other's struggles. They went on to win the premiership.

The story works because it's not about football. It's about what happens when you trade performance for connection: you get more of both.

**Mindfulness** is the third pillar, and Hugh is aware that this one has been commercialized to death. He approaches it practically. For him, mindfulness isn't sitting cross-legged on a cushion for twenty minutes. It's the pause between a trigger and a reaction. It's noticing when your mind has wandered and gently bringing it back — not once, but a thousand times a day. It's the breath before you respond to a difficult email. It's the sixty seconds you take before walking into your house after a bad day, so you don't bring the stress to your family.

He shares a story from his own life: a period of intense anxiety where he couldn't sleep, couldn't focus, couldn't stop the spiral of negative thoughts. The mindfulness practice that helped wasn't meditation in the formal sense. It was a simple body scan — lying in bed and noticing the physical sensations of anxiety without trying to change them. The heat in his chest. The tightness in his throat. Just noticing. And something shifted — not because the anxiety disappeared, but because he stopped fighting it.

**Honest Criticism:** The book is written for a general audience, and researchers may find it frustratingly light on citations. Hugh is a storyteller, not a scientist, and the framework doesn't engage with the academic literature beyond a surface level. The tone can be a bit earnest — there's a lot of "you can do it!" energy that might grate on readers who prefer their self-help with an edge.

The GEM framework also simplifies what is actually a complex set of psychological skills. Gratitude, empathy, and mindfulness are each the subject of hundreds of studies, and the book treats them more as character virtues than as trainable competencies. The implementation advice is solid but brief — you'll finish the book knowing *what* to do but wishing for more on *how* to do it when it's hard.

**What Will Actually Stick With Me:** The image of Hugh's family dinner table, with everyone naming one good thing from their day, is deceptively powerful. I tried it and found that the real benefit wasn't the gratitude itself — it was that I spent the day scanning for things to be grateful for, which changed my attention more than the evening ritual did. The Danny story — the father who lost his son and built a gratitude muscle anyway — is the one I think about when gratitude feels hollow or forced.
