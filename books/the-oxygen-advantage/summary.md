The first thing *The Oxygen Advantage* did was make me feel stupid for having a mouth. Not permanently — just for the 30 years I'd been breathing through it without a second thought. Patrick McKeown, a protégé of the Russian breathing pioneer Konstantin Buteyko, argues that most of us are chronically over-breathing, and that this single habit — mouth breathing — is quietly dismantling our health, our sleep, our athletic performance, and our mental state.

I came to this book skeptical. I've read a lot of breathing books, and most of them land somewhere between "just breathe, man" and a 400-page dissertation on why your exhalation should be exactly 1.7 times longer than your inhalation. McKeown is different. He's a former sufferer of asthma who stumbled into Buteyko's work after exhausting every conventional treatment. His writing has the urgency of someone who was saved and wants to pass the rope.

The core insight is counterintuitive: you don't need more oxygen. Your blood is already 95–99% saturated with oxygen most of the time. What you need is more carbon dioxide. Yes — the gas we've been taught is waste. McKeown explains that CO₂ is not a waste product; it's a primary regulator of how oxygen is actually *released* from your hemoglobin into your cells. This is called the Bohr Effect. When CO₂ levels drop because you're over-breathing, your blood holds onto oxygen tighter — less reaches your brain, your muscles, your organs. You're breathing *more* and oxygenating *less.*

**The Trap of "Take a Deep Breath":** That thing everyone says when you're anxious — "just take a deep breath" — might be making things worse. A big gulp of air triggers your sympathetic nervous system, the fight-or-flight response. McKeown argues that small, soft, nasal breaths are what actually calm you down, because they gently increase CO₂, which relaxes smooth muscle tissue and signals safety to the nervous system.

McKeown breaks his program into three layers: the mechanics of nasal breathing, the principle of "light breathing," and the advanced protocols for athletes.

Before getting into the mechanics, he spends crucial time explaining why we breathe the way we do. The modern human breathes about 12–15 liters of air per minute at rest. The healthy norm, according to Buteyko's research, is 4–6 liters. That's more than double the volume we actually need. And here's the kicker: this over-breathing isn't neutral. It's actively harmful. When you blow off too much CO₂, your blood vessels constrict (including the arteries feeding your brain), your hemoglobin clings to oxygen instead of releasing it, and your nervous system interprets the low CO₂ as a threat signal. You end up in a low-grade state of physiological panic without knowing why.

Nasal breathing is the foundation. The nose is not a backup air intake for when your mouth is busy eating. It's a sophisticated organ designed to filter, warm, and pressurize air. It produces nitric oxide, a molecule that opens blood vessels and kills pathogens. Mouth-bypassing this system, especially during exercise, is like running your car without an air filter. McKeown's most practical instruction is simple: tape your mouth shut at night. Seriously — surgical tape over the lips, just a small strip. He presents research showing that mouth taping reduces snoring, improves sleep quality, and, for some people, eliminates sleep apnea. I tried it. It's weird for three nights. By night four, I couldn't go back.

**The "Light Breathing" concept** is where it gets interesting. McKeown says most people chronically over-breathe at rest — taking 12–18 breaths per minute when 6–8 is optimal. The fix isn't to breathe *deeper* but to breathe *less*. He introduces the BOLT score (Body Oxygen Level Test) — a simple measure of how many seconds you can comfortably hold your breath after a normal exhalation. A BOLT score under 20 seconds indicates chronic over-breathing. An elite athlete might be at 40–60 seconds. The whole program is designed around increasing your BOLT score through simulated altitude training — periods of reduced breathing that nudge your system toward CO₂ tolerance.

He tells a story about a runner who came to him with exercise-induced asthma. The runner would start wheezing halfway through a 5K, reach for the inhaler, and limp home. McKeown taught him to switch to nasal breathing during runs, to reduce his breathing volume, and to do breath holds at specific points in his training. Within three months, the runner dropped his 5K time by 90 seconds and stopped using the inhaler. The asthma wasn't cured — the *breathing pattern* was corrected, and the asthma symptoms resolved.

There's a whole section on the relationship between breathing and posture that I haven't seen elsewhere. McKeown argues that chronic mouth breathing leads to a forward head posture, which compresses the airway and reinforces the bad breathing habit. It's a loop: you can't breathe well because your posture is bad, and your posture is bad because you're breathing poorly. Fixing one helps the other. He recommends specific exercises — chin tucks, diaphragmatic breathing in constructive rest position — to break the cycle.

**The Trap of Over-Engineering:** McKeown's program can become another thing to obsess about. Checking your BOLT score every hour, worrying about nighttime mouth breathing, counting your breaths per minute. The book does acknowledge this but doesn't fully solve it. The paradox is that the system designed to relax you can itself become a source of tension if you approach it like a perfectionist.

The athlete-specific chapters are detailed and practical — McKeown maps breathing patterns to different running paces, explains why you shouldn't breathe through your mouth during maximal efforts, and provides specific warm-up protocols that mimic altitude exposure. But the same principles apply to anyone who moves: the better your breathing economy, the more you get out of every breath.

There's a section on high-altitude simulation that I found unexpectedly profound. McKeown describes techniques — breath holds during walking, reduced breathing during exercise — that simulate the conditions of high altitude. This triggers your body to produce more red blood cells, improve oxygen utilization, and build CO₂ tolerance. The effect is similar to sleeping in an altitude tent, but it costs nothing and uses only your own biology. He shares the story of a mountaineer who used these techniques to prepare for an Everest ascent, reporting that his sustained breath hold time at sea level went from 25 seconds to 60 seconds after 8 weeks of practice. "The body adapts to what you ask of it," McKeown writes, "and very few of us ask it to breathe less."

McKeown ends with a striking claim: that our modern environment has systematically destroyed our natural breathing patterns. Soft food, sedentary lifestyles, central heating, high stress — all of these have trained us to breathe too much, too fast, and through the wrong orifice. The fix isn't complex. It's literally closing your mouth and slowing down.

I want to mention one more thing that gets buried in the middle of the book but deserves more attention: the connection McKeown draws between breathing and spinal alignment. He explains that the diaphragm's movement is directly connected to the health of the spine via the thoracolumbar fascia. Poor breathing mechanics — shallow chest breathing — starve the spine of the hydraulic movement it needs. This means chronic back pain, stiffness, and degeneration may have a breathing component. I'd never heard anyone connect those dots before.

**Honest Criticism:** McKeown occasionally overstates his case. The Bohr Effect is real and well-documented, but he sometimes writes as if CO₂ is a miracle molecule that fixes everything — he stretches the evidence in places. Not all research supports the strong claims about BOLT scores and athletic performance. The book can feel formulaic in its middle third, cycling through the same advice with different examples. And the program requires consistent, daily practice — not everyone will have the discipline to do breath holds every morning for six weeks.

Also: the mouth taping recommendation, while backed by some evidence, isn't suitable for everyone. People with severe sleep apnea, nasal obstructions, or certain respiratory conditions should consult a doctor first. McKeown mentions this in passing but could be more emphatic.
