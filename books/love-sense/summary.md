Gottman said you need to learn conflict resolution. Chapman said love languages. Gray said men are from Mars. Sue Johnson says they were all asking the wrong question. The real question isn't how to fight better or speak the right dialect. It's whether you can turn to your partner and know, in your bones, that they'll be there when you need them. **Love Sense** is Johnson's argument that romantic love is not an emotion that happens to us — it's an attachment bond, wired by evolution, as essential to survival as food or shelter. And once you see it that way, everything you thought you knew about relationships stops making sense.

Johnson is a clinical psychologist who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), the most empirically validated approach to couple therapy. **Love Sense** is her attempt to rewrite the cultural script: love is not mysterious, not a crapshoot, and definitely not something you can fix with a spreadsheet of strengths and weaknesses. It's an ancient biological program for survival, and when it goes wrong, the solution is not more techniques — it's rebuilding a secure bond.

The book's opening move is to demolish the conventional wisdom. Johnson argues that Western culture has sold us three bad ideas about love. First, that love is a mystery — something that happens by magic and can't be understood. Second, that love is a feeling that fades naturally, and "realistic" couples accept this. Third, that love is a transaction — you give X, you get Y, you balance the books.

None of these hold up to the attachment science Johnson has spent decades studying. The real story: humans are wired for emotional connection from birth. Infants attach to caregivers for survival. Adults attach to romantic partners for the same reason. The drive is biological, not cultural. It's as real as hunger or thirst.

"The need for safe emotional connection is wired into our genes," Johnson writes. "It is not a weakness or a luxury. It's our basic survival code."

This is Johnson's central claim and it changes everything. If love is an attachment bond, then the goal of a relationship is not to negotiate better or communicate more clearly. The goal is to create a relationship where both partners feel **safe** — where you can reach out and know someone will be there.

Thinking love is about finding the "right person." Johnson says it's about building a secure bond with the person you're with. Attachment research shows that relationship quality matters more than partner characteristics. You don't need a perfect partner. You need a responsive one.

When an attachment bond is threatened — when you reach out and don't get a response — the brain reacts with primal panic. This is not a "communication problem." It's a survival alarm.

Johnson names the three patterns couples fall into when this alarm sounds:

One partner attacks, criticizes, blames. The message is "I'm angry because you're not there for me." But it comes out as "What's wrong with you?"

One partner pursues, the other withdraws. The classic dance: she wants connection, he wants space. She pursues harder, he withdraws further. Both feel abandoned — he by her criticism, she by his silence.

Both partners emotionally check out. The relationship becomes a business arrangement. There's no fighting because there's no reaching. This is the most dangerous pattern — attachment researchers call it the "death knell" of relationships.

"The enemy of love is not anger," Johnson writes. "It's emotional distance. It's the moment when one partner turns away and the other gives up."

Thinking the problem is the content of the argument. It's not about who left the dishes or who forgot the anniversary. It's about the attachment cry underneath: "Are you there for me? Can I count on you?" Most couples fight about surface issues while the real conversation is happening beneath the words.

The core of EFT — and the book's most practical contribution — is the "Hold Me Tight" conversation. This is a structured dialogue where partners learn to:

1. **Recognize** their demon dialogue and name it
2. **Reach** for each other with their attachment needs — not complaints or criticisms, but raw, vulnerable asks
3. **Respond** with presence and care

Johnson shares a story of a couple she worked with — a successful attorney and his wife. The attorney was brilliant in court but emotionally unavailable at home. His wife described feeling like "a ghost in my own marriage." During a session, Johnson helped the wife say: "I need to know I matter to you. When you pull away, I feel like I'm invisible. I'm scared that you don't need me."

The attorney's response — and Johnson guides this carefully — wasn't a defense or a solution. It was: "I hear you. I'm here. I'm not going anywhere." That's the entire point. Not fixing. Just showing up.

"Emotional engagement is the only real currency in relationships," Johnson writes. "Solutions and advice are just small change."

Thinking you need to "fix" your partner's feelings. When someone shares pain, the instinct is to solve it. But attachment science says the need is not for a solution — it's for connection. The most powerful thing you can say is not "Here's what you should do" but "I'm here. I'm with you."

Johnson has a chapter on sex that will annoy some readers and liberate others. Her claim: the best sex is not about technique, novelty, or adventure. It's about emotional presence.

"Great sex is not about great moves," she writes. "It's about being completely there with your partner — turned toward each other, not turned on by performance."

She cites research showing that couples in secure, emotionally connected relationships report more satisfying sex — and more of it — than couples who focus on "spicing things up." The mechanism is clear: when you feel safe with your partner, you can let go. You can be vulnerable. You can stop performing and start being present.

This flips the cultural script. The message from magazines, porn, and popular culture is that good sex requires novelty, skill, and adventure. Johnson says that's backward. Good sex requires safety, presence, and emotional accessibility.

Thinking sex is a separate domain from the rest of the relationship. Johnson says it's not. When couples are emotionally distant, sex suffers. When they rebuild emotional connection, sex improves naturally. "Sex is the canary in the coal mine of your relationship."

Johnson introduces the concept of **attachment narratives** — the stories we carry about ourselves in relationships. "I'm not lovable." "People always leave." "I have to be perfect to be accepted." These narratives are not neutral observations. They're scripts that shape how we reach for others and how we respond.

A partner who carries "I'm not good enough" will interpret their partner's bad mood as confirmation. A partner who carries "People are unreliable" will test and push and withdraw before they can be hurt. These narratives become self-fulfilling prophecies.

The EFT process helps couples rewrite these stories — not by positive affirmation but by creating new emotional experiences. When a partner who believes they're unlovable reaches out in vulnerability and is met with warmth, the narrative begins to crack. Not because they were told a new story, but because they lived one.

Thinking you can think your way out of a bad relationship narrative. You can't. Insight alone doesn't change attachment patterns. You have to *experience* something different. That's why EFT focuses on creating corrective emotional experiences in the therapy room, not just analyzing past wounds.

The book's most provocative claim may be its last: secure attachment is not just nice to have. It's a health intervention.

Johnson cites research showing that people in secure relationships live longer, have better cardiovascular health, stronger immune systems, and lower rates of depression and anxiety. The mechanism is physiological: secure attachment regulates the nervous system. When you know you're safe, your body stops preparing for threat.

"Loneliness kills," Johnson writes bluntly. "It's not a metaphor. It kills as surely as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day."

This reframes the entire project of relationship maintenance. You're not just "working on your marriage." You're protecting your health. You're building a buffer against stress. You're creating the conditions for your nervous system to rest.

Love is not a mystery or a transaction. It's an attachment bond, hardwired by evolution for survival. The problems in your relationship are not about poor communication or incompatible personalities. They're about broken attachment — moments when one partner reached for the other and found no one there. The solution is not more techniques. It's rebuilding the sense of safety and responsiveness that makes reaching possible. When both partners can turn toward each other and say "I'm here," the rest starts to heal on its own.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book was the "Protest Polka." I've seen it in my own relationships and every couple I've ever watched argue. One person moves closer, the other moves away. It looks like she's attacking and he's retreating, but underneath, both are asking the same question: "Are you there for me?" The insight is not just that this dance is destructive — it's that neither person is wrong. Both are trying to protect the attachment bond in the only way they know how. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

The book is heavily structured around EFT as a therapy modality, and Johnson's commercial interest in promoting her method is evident throughout. The tone can be evangelical — she treats attachment theory as the final word on love rather than a useful framework with limitations. The research is cherry-picked to support her case, and she doesn't engage seriously with critics who argue that EFT's results are inflated or that attachment styles are more fluid than she suggests. The writing is also repetitive: the same metaphors (love as survival code, partners as an emotional home) appear in nearly every chapter. And while Johnson criticizes pop psychology approaches to love, she occasionally slides into the same kind of overpromising — if only you use EFT, your relationship will be transformed.
