A few years ago, I was on a phone call with a friend who had just gotten a dream job at a dream company with a dream salary. She was miserable. "I did everything right," she said, genuinely confused. "Why don't I feel... enough?"

That phone call is the unnamed starting point of *The Good Enough Life*. The book collects voices from psychology, philosophy, sociology, and memoir to make a case that feels almost seditious: the relentless pursuit of "the best" is making us sick, and the alternative is not settling for mediocrity but embracing something the authors call "good enough" — a standard of living that's consciously chosen, intentionally built, and defensible against the culture that tells you it's not enough.

The collection is loosely organized around a central paradox: objectively, most of us in the developed world have better lives than any humans in history. We have more food, more safety, more entertainment, more choice, more opportunity. And yet rates of anxiety, depression, and meaninglessness have never been higher. The book's diagnosis is that we suffer not from scarcity but from *the ideology of more* — the belief that the best life is always the one with more achievement, more optimization, more growth.

The psychological research cited in the book is devastating. Studies by Barry Schwartz and others have shown that "maximizers" — people who always seek the best option — are significantly less happy, more depressed, and more regretful than "satisficers" — people who seek "good enough" and stop looking. Maximizers get better outcomes objectively. They just feel worse about them.

The book traces how this plays out across domains. In career: the person who keeps climbing the ladder even though they hate the view from each rung. In relationships: the person who ends a good relationship because they're afraid they're missing a great one. In consumption: the person whose Amazon cart is full of decisions they'll second-guess. In self-image: the person who can never feel accomplished because there's always someone more accomplished.

"Maximization is the thief of contentment," one contributor writes. "But it presents itself as ambition, so we praise it."

The Trap of maximization is that it's self-reinforcing. The more you optimize, the more you believe optimization is the point. You lose the capacity for simple enjoyment. You can't just watch a movie — you have to choose the *best* movie, stream it on the *best* platform, at the *optimal* time, with the *right* snacks, and even then you're half-wondering if there's a better movie you should be watching instead. The authors call this the "optimization spiral," and they argue it's a feature of modern capitalism, not a bug. An optimizing population is a consuming population.

The book takes care to distinguish "good enough" from mediocrity or complacency. "Good enough" is a *conscious threshold* — a decision that this is sufficient for the purpose at hand. It requires more self-awareness, not less.

**In work:** Good enough means doing excellent work that meets your standards, then stopping. Not because you couldn't polish further but because the additional polish wouldn't serve the purpose. The authors cite the "law of diminishing returns" applied to creative work: the last 10% of polish often takes 50% of the time and adds 2% of value. Good enough means knowing where that curve bends.

**In relationships:** Good enough means accepting that no partner, friend, or family member will meet all your needs. The myth of "the one" is a maximizer's trap. The authors cite Gottman's research on marriage: happy couples don't have fewer problems. They have the same problems but respond to them differently. "A good enough relationship is not one that's free of conflict," they write. "It's one where both people have decided the conflict is worth it."

**In self-image:** Good enough means knowing you're flawed and not needing to fix everything. The psychological concept of "unconditional self-acceptance" (popularized by Albert Ellis) appears here in a new frame: self-acceptance is not about feeling good about everything you do. It's about not tying your worth to your performance. "You are not your resume," the book says. "And you are not your worst mistake either."

Barry Schwartz's work on the paradox of choice is the scientific backbone of the collection, but the book pushes beyond it in useful directions. Schwartz famously showed that more choices don't make us happier — they make us more anxious, more regretful, and less likely to choose at all. But the authors argue that the problem isn't choice itself. It's the *optimization mindset* we bring to choice.

When you choose from abundance with a satisficing mindset — "what's good enough?" — choice is liberating. When you choose from abundance with a maximizing mindset — "what's the *best*?" — choice is paralyzing. The difference is not in the external options. It's in the internal standard.

The authors illustrate this with a study of job seekers. Those who searched for "the perfect job" took longer to find work, ended up in jobs no better than the satisficers, and were less satisfied with their eventual choice. The satisficers set a clear threshold, found something that met it, and stopped looking. They didn't settle for less — they settled for *enough*. And that "enough" turned out to be as good as "the best," minus the months of anxiety.

The book is at its best when it examines why "good enough" is so hard to accept. Three forces push against it:

**The Scarcity Mindset** — the belief that opportunity is limited and you must grab every chance. This is rooted in evolutionary psychology (we're wired for scarcity even in abundance) and reinforced by advertising and media. "Good enough" feels risky when you believe there may not be another chance.

**Social Comparison** — the endless measuring of your life against others'. The book engages with social media specifically, noting that platforms are designed to maximize comparison because comparison drives engagement. The authors argue that comparison is not just unpleasant — it's *cognitively addictive*. Your brain gets a small dopamine hit from feeling superior and a cortisol spike from feeling inferior. Both keep you scrolling.

**The Protestant Work Ethic** — the deep cultural belief that suffering and effort are morally superior to ease and contentment. The book traces this from Puritan theology to modern hustle culture. "Good enough" feels like laziness because our culture has moralized productivity. Rest is not rest; it's potential work not being done.

The book has a blind spot around privilege that's hard to ignore. "Good enough" is a much easier philosophy to embrace when you have enough. For someone living in poverty, good enough is not a choice — it's a constraint that they're trying to escape. The book gestures at this but doesn't wrestle with it seriously. "Choose good enough" is different when the choice is between a cramped apartment and homelessness versus between a comfortable apartment and a luxury one.

The writing quality varies. The psychological chapters are rigorous and clear. The philosophical chapters are sometimes impenetrable (the Heidegger section took me three reads). And a few chapters feel like filler — the author clearly wanted to hit a word count and padded with personal anecdotes that don't advance the argument.

There's also a strange absence of joy. The book argues for "good enough" as a defense against misery, but it rarely makes the case that "good enough" can be genuinely *thrilling*. The best conversations I've had, the best meals I've eaten, the best moments of my life — none of them were the "best" by any objective measure. They were just... good enough to be present for. I wanted the book to celebrate that, not just defend against its opposite.

The phrase "satisficer vs. maximizer" has become a permanent lens. I catch myself in decisions — what to eat, what to watch, how to spend an afternoon — and ask: "Am I maximizing or satisficing right now?" The answer alone is often enough to break the spiral.

The book also reframed how I think about regret. Maximizers experience more regret not because their choices are worse but because they can always imagine a better one. "The maximizer's curse," the book calls it — knowing you made a good choice but being haunted by the better choice you could have made. Recognizing this curse doesn't eliminate regret, but it undermines its authority.

Anyone who's ever accomplished something and felt nothing. Anyone who finds themselves optimizing their leisure time. Anyone who senses that "more" is not making them happier but can't articulate why. Especially valuable for young adults facing a world of infinite options. Skip it if you're genuinely content with your relationship to "enough," or if you find anti-capitalist critiques tiresome no matter how well-argued.
