You're scrolling through Instagram, and someone is in a foreign country, tasting something exotic, laughing with strangers, living. You're on your couch. The gap between their life and yours feels like a canyon. **A More Exciting Life** is a book-length argument against that feeling — not by denying that life can be dull, but by redefining what excitement actually is.

This anthology from The School of Life takes on a peculiar modern anxiety: the fear that you're boring and your life is unremarkable. It's a fear fed by social media, by the glorification of travel and adventure, by the unspoken assumption that a good life is a series of peak experiences. The book's counter-argument is radical in its simplicity: excitement is not found in exotic locations or extreme activities. It's found in a certain *quality of attention* that can be applied anywhere.

The book opens by diagnosing the problem: we have been sold a false image of excitement. The exciting life, as presented by media and culture, involves adventure, novelty, risk, and constant stimulation. It looks like an Everest climb, a startup IPO, a whirlwind romance, a year of travel.

The problem with this image is not that it's unattainable. It's that it's *wrong* — even for the people who appear to be living it. The book tells the story of a man who climbed Everest. He spent years training, huge amounts of money, and weeks in extreme danger. When he reached the summit, the feeling lasted about fifteen minutes. Then he had to go back down. The rest of his life had not changed. He was the same person with the same emotional landscape, now with a remarkable experience that had remarkably little impact on his daily happiness.

> "We confuse novelty with transformation. A new experience can be exciting without changing anything. The most exciting lives are often built on repetition, not novelty — on depth, not breadth."

**The Trap** of the bucket list mentality. Writing a list of things to do before you die sounds like a plan for an exciting life. In practice, it's a productivity system applied to leisure — another set of tasks to complete. The book argues that checking off experiences is not the same as having a rich inner life. The bucket list is a consumerist approach to living.

The book's central insight: excitement is not a property of events. It's a property of attention. Two people can attend the same concert. One is bored, thinking about work, scrolling between songs. The other is completely absorbed, noticing every detail, feeling the music in their body. The difference is not the concert. It's the quality of presence.

This is where the book draws on philosophical sources — the phenomenology of Husserl and Merleau-Ponty, the mindfulness traditions of Buddhism, the Romantic poets' attention to nature. The thread is consistent: the world is more interesting than we perceive it to be. What's lacking is not stimulation but *perceptual capacity*.

> "The world is full of remarkable things. The limitation is not in the world — it's in our ability to notice."

**The Trap** of believing you need to change your circumstances to feel alive. The book doesn't deny that some environments are genuinely more stimulating than others. But it warns against the idea that your life will become exciting once you move cities, change jobs, or find a new partner. The pattern of attention follows you. Wherever you go, you bring your capacity — or incapacity — for engagement.

The most practical section of the book is a guide to what it calls **deep noticing** — the skill of attending to ordinary experience with the same intensity we give to extraordinary moments.

It describes a walk through a familiar neighborhood done with fresh eyes: noticing the cracks in the pavement, the way light falls on a particular building, the conversation between two strangers on a bench, the smell of someone's cooking from an open window. The point is not to collect these observations like stamps. The point is to practice the mode of attention — to prove to yourself that the ordinary is inexhaustibly interesting when you actually look.

The book draws on the example of the French *flâneur* — the urban stroller who treats the city as a theater of endless fascination. The flâneur doesn't need to go anywhere special. Their excitement comes from the quality of their gaze.

> "The flâneur knows something we've forgotten: the street you've walked a thousand times is never the same twice. You just stopped noticing."

**The Trap** of turning noticing into another productivity practice. The goal is not to "optimize your attention" for better performance. The goal is to recover a sense of wonder that has nothing to do with output. The minute you start tracking how many "deep noticing" sessions you've done, you've missed the point.

Here's a counterintuitive claim: difficult experiences are often more exciting than easy ones. The book argues that we've confused comfort with happiness and excitement with pleasure. In reality, the most memorable experiences — the ones that make us feel most alive — often involve struggle, risk, and uncertainty.

A passage describes learning a new instrument as an adult. The first weeks are humiliating. Your fingers won't cooperate. You sound terrible. The book's question: why does this feel alive? Because difficulty forces presence. You can't play a scale while thinking about your email. The challenge demands full attention, and full attention is the feeling we call "being alive."

> "We think we want ease. What we actually want is a worthy struggle — something that demands everything we have."

**The Trap** of romanticizing difficulty for its own sake. Not every struggle is growth-promoting. Not every challenge is worth taking. The book acknowledges this but doesn't develop the distinction well. The line between "healthy challenge" and "harmful stress" is a real one, and the reader is left to draw it themselves.

The book's most provocative chapter is about boredom. Not as a problem to be solved, but as a source of information. The argument: boredom is not a lack of interesting things. It's a signal that you're avoiding something. When you're bored, you're not in a state of neutral — you're in a state of suppressed restlessness. Something is trying to surface.

The book suggests that the antidote to boredom is not more stimulation. It's stopping and listening to what the boredom is telling you. What are you avoiding? What feeling is too uncomfortable to feel, so you're labeling the world "boring" instead?

> "Boredom is not the absence of interest. It's the presence of avoidance."

This reframes the entire problem. You don't need a more exciting life. You need to stop running from your inner life long enough to discover that it's already fascinating.

The book's approach is genuinely useful, but it has real limitations. The advice to find excitement through attention rather than action works beautifully for people with stable, comfortable lives. For someone in genuine deprivation, the recommendation to "just notice more" can feel dismissive.

There's also an implicit assumption that everyone has the capacity for this kind of attention. Not everyone does. Depression, anxiety, trauma, and ADHD all affect the ability to be present. The book doesn't engage with these conditions meaningfully.

And while the critique of the "bucket list" mentality is sharp, the book offers no real alternative structure. It tells you what not to do without giving you a clear system for what to do instead. The result is a book that diagnoses the problem brilliantly but prescribes weakly.

The idea I keep returning to: the most exciting people I know are not the ones who have done the most things. They're the ones who pay the most attention. They find something interesting in a conversation that would bore anyone else. They notice the light, the texture, the small details. They don't need adventure — they bring aliveness with them. That's a more achievable goal than climbing Everest, and probably more valuable.

For anyone who's felt their life is boring. For the restless, the frustrated, the ones scrolling travel blogs at work. For people who suspect the problem might be inside them rather than outside. Skip if you're going through a genuinely difficult period — this book requires a baseline of stability to be useful.

Take a walk you've taken many times — to the store, around the block, through your office building. Do it without headphones or phone. Force yourself to notice five things you've never noticed before. Write them down. The point is not the list. It's the proof that the ordinary is richer than you think.

The next time you feel bored, don't reach for your phone. Set a timer for three minutes. Sit with the boredom. Ask it: what am I avoiding right now? What feeling is underneath this restlessness? You might not get an answer. But the question itself changes the relationship.

Identify one activity that involves genuine struggle and learning — not something you're already good at. It could be a language, an instrument, a sport, a craft. Commit to doing it for 20 minutes, once, without any expectation of progress. The goal is not to get good. The goal is to experience difficulty as aliveness.
