You're in the middle of a panic attack. Your heart is slamming against your ribs. You're sure you're about to die, go crazy, or lose all control. Every instinct says: run. Escape. Call an ambulance. Do something, anything, to make this feeling stop.

Now imagine someone telling you that the cure is to *make it worse*.

Welcome to Dr. David Burns's approach. A Stanford-trained psychiatrist who trained under Aaron Beck — the father of cognitive therapy — Burns wrote *Feeling Good* in 1980 and changed how the world treats depression. *When Panic Attacks* came later, in 2006, and it's the practical distillation of everything he learned treating severe anxiety over three decades. No theory. No gentle hand-holding. Just techniques that sound almost cruel until you try them and discover they work.

Burns starts from an uncomfortable premise: **your anxiety is not a disease that happens to you. It's a trick your own mind plays. And once you understand the trick, you can't be fooled by it anymore.**

Panic attacks feel physical — the shortness of breath, the racing heart, the dizziness — so we assume they *are* physical. We go to cardiologists, get EKGs, insist something is wrong with our bodies. But Burns points to the research: panic attacks are caused by nothing more than **the fear of fear itself**. You feel a slight flutter in your chest. You interpret that flutter as dangerous. That interpretation triggers adrenaline. More adrenaline = more physical symptoms. More symptoms = more fear. Within seconds, you're spiraling into full-blown panic.

The cause isn't your heart. It's your interpretation of what your heart is doing.

Burns calls this the **What-If Syndrome**. A tiny physical sensation triggers the thought: "What if I'm having a heart attack?" "What if I pass out?" "What if I can't breathe?" These thoughts act like gasoline on a spark. The panic attack is the fire that follows.

> "Your feelings are not created by the events that happen to you, but by the thoughts you think about those events."

The most famous technique in this book is the **Paradoxical Approach** — and it's the one that makes people furious when they first encounter it. Instead of trying to calm down during a panic attack, you try to make it worse. You say to yourself: "I'm going to make my heart pound even harder. I'm going to make myself as dizzy as possible. Let's see how bad this can really get."

Sounds insane. But here's why it works: panic requires two ingredients — the physical sensation *and* the resistance to it. The more you fight the sensation, the more you add fuel. When you stop fighting — when you actually try to *increase* the sensation — you remove the resistance. And without resistance, the panic feedback loop collapses. Your brain realizes: "Wait, if I'm trying to make it worse and it's not getting worse, then maybe this isn't actually dangerous." Game over.

> "Resistance makes panic grow. Acceptance and paradoxical effort shrinks it."

Burns tells the story of a patient named Marcia who was terrified of flying. On every flight she would grip the armrest, hyperventilate, and count down the minutes until landing. Burns told her: next flight, try to *make* the plane crash. Visualize it. Will it to happen. She laughed — but tried it. The moment she started trying to crash the plane, her fear evaporated. She'd switched from victim to observer. She was no longer at the mercy of the flight; she was the one trying to make the thing happen. And since she didn't actually have the power to crash a plane, she realized — painfully obviously — that her fear was a bluff.

Most people try to manage anxiety through avoidance and distraction. Deep breathing. Positive affirmations. "Calm down. Relax. It'll be okay." Burns would call this *resistance in disguise*. You're still fighting. You're still telling yourself the anxiety is dangerous and needs to go away. The paradoxical approach asks something harder: stop trying to escape and lean *into* the fear. That's terrifying — and it's the only thing that actually works.

Burns dedicates a large section of the book to the **hidden cause of most anxiety: the approval addiction.** He argues that the majority of social anxiety, panic attacks, and chronic worry stem from a core belief: "I need everyone to approve of me, or I'm worthless."

He calls this the **Approval Addiction**, and his treatment is characteristically blunt. He asks readers to do an **Approval Cost-Benefit Analysis** — literally write down the advantages and disadvantages of needing everyone's approval. Then he asks you to perform an **Anti-Approval Experiment**: go out and deliberately do something to provoke disapproval. Wear mismatched socks to a meeting. Ask a stupid question. Say something mildly controversial and watch the other person frown.

The point isn't to be unpleasant. It's to prove to yourself that disapproval doesn't kill you. The discomfort is temporary. You survive. And once you've proven that, the fear of disapproval loses its power.

Burns also introduces a taxonomy of cognitive distortions that cause anxiety — the same ones from his earlier work, now applied to panic specifically:

You predict disaster — "I'm going to fail this interview" — and believe your prediction as fact. You assume others are judging you negatively without evidence. You feel anxious, therefore you assume danger exists. You call yourself "an anxious person" or "a panicker" and live down to the label.

For each distortion, Burns offers a specific cognitive technique. For Fortune Telling: the **Cost-Benefit Analysis** — list the advantages and disadvantages of worrying. Does worrying actually help? Or does it just drain your energy while the feared event happens or doesn't happen regardless?

> "Worrying is like paying interest on a debt you may never owe."

People think they need to eliminate their anxiety completely to be happy. Burns argues the opposite — the goal isn't to never feel anxious. The goal is to stop being *afraid* of feeling anxious. Once you lose the fear of fear itself, anxiety becomes just another feeling. Uncomfortable, sure. But not catastrophic. Not something that needs to be fixed immediately.

One of the more unusual parts of the book is the **Shame-Attacking Exercises**, which Burns adapted from his colleague Albert Ellis. These are deliberately embarrassing behaviors you perform to desensitize yourself to shame. Ellis himself would go to a park, get on a crowded bus, and loudly say "Hello, everyone!" While people stared, he'd hold his ground. And survive.

The point: if you train yourself to tolerate shame — to let people stare, let them laugh — you rob social anxiety of its power. The worst thing that can happen is mild embarrassment. And you survive that too.

Here's the honest problem with this book: Burns is so confident in his methods that he can come across as dismissive. He tells stories of patients cured in a single session, which can make you feel like a failure if your panic doesn't resolve that fast. The "just try harder" undertone is real. Cognitive techniques require practice, and some readers — especially those with trauma-based anxiety — will find the workbook style too mechanical for their needs. Burns assumes a rational mind that can be reasoned with, but severe anxiety doesn't always cooperate with logic.

There's also a tension the book never resolves: Burns's techniques are brilliant for *acute* panic and specific phobias. But for the kind of anxiety that's diffuse, existential, or rooted in real-life circumstances (poverty, chronic illness, systemic oppression), cognitive restructuring can feel like painting over rust. You change your thoughts, but your life doesn't change. The book works best as a tactical toolkit, not a philosophical framework.

Take the paradoxical method alone. When your next panic attack starts, don't fight it. Don't reach for a paper bag or a Xanax. Say it out loud: "I'm going to make this panic attack as intense as I possibly can. Let's go. Bring it on." Rate your fear as you try. Watch what happens when you stop resisting. Try the paradoxical approach during one moment of social anxiety this week — lean into the awkwardness and see if your fear dissolves.

What Burns is really teaching is that your anxiety is a magician. It tells you it's in control. But once you've seen how the trick works, the magician can't fool you anymore. The panic attack still shows up — but now you know it's just sensations your own mind created. And you know the one thing that terrifies it more than anything: you not being afraid of it anymore.
