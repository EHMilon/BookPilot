Before she was untamed, Glennon Doyle was just trying to survive. *Carry On, Warrior* is the book that started everything — the collection of blog posts and essays that turned a suburban mom's anonymous online confessions into a movement. Reading it now, after *Love Warrior* and *Untamed*, you can see the raw material: the person she was before she knew who she was becoming. And that rawness is exactly why it matters.

Glennon started writing on a blog called *Momastery* while her kids were young, her marriage was struggling, and she was white-knuckling through early sobriety. She'd been a bulimic, an alcoholic, a mess. She got pregnant young, married young, and spent years trying to be a "good wife and mother" while falling apart inside. The blog was her confessional — she wrote about the fights with her husband, the guilt over her parenting, the terror of being honest with herself. People started reading. Then thousands. Then millions. Because she was saying what everyone was thinking but no one would say.

> "We don't need to be fixed. We need to be seen."

**The Trap here** is coming to *Carry On, Warrior* expecting the polished philosophy of *Untamed*. This book is not polished. It's messy. Glennon contradicts herself across essays — she says she's got it together in one chapter and admits she's falling apart in the next. Some essays feel like they were written in a parking lot while crying. That's not a flaw. That's the point. The book is a monument to the idea that you don't need to have your life sorted to help other people feel less alone.

Glennon's origin story matters here. Before she was a bestselling author, she was a bulimic and an alcoholic who got sober, got pregnant, got married, and started a blog called *Momastery*. She didn't write as an expert. She wrote as a fellow traveler — someone who was also drowning but had figured out how to tread water long enough to wave at others. The title comes from her credo: "We can do hard things." Not "I can do hard things." We. That's the whole ethos of the book. This book is not polished. It's messy. Glennon contradicts herself across essays — she says she's got it together in one chapter and admits she's falling apart in the next. Some essays feel like they were written in a parking lot while crying. That's not a flaw. That's the point. The book is a monument to the idea that you don't need to have your life sorted to help other people feel less alone.

The book is structured as a loose collection — there's no single narrative arc. Glennon writes about her wedding, her childhood, her cats, her parenting disasters, her recovery from addiction, her friendship with a woman dying of cancer. The connective tissue is not a story; it's a voice. She's the friend who will tell you the truth because she loves you.

**The Concept of "Brutiful"** — Glennon's invented word for the brutal + beautiful nature of real life — runs through every essay. Nothing is just good or bad. Everything is both. The wedding was beautiful and brutally honest. The birth of her child was ecstatic and terrifying. Even her sobriety is described not as triumph but as daily surrender.

> "Life is brutiful. Brutal and beautiful — and I have to say yes to both."

> "You don't have to be special to do special things. You just have to be here."

**The Trap here** is spiritual bypassing — using the idea of "brutiful" to avoid actually feeling the brutality. Glennon doesn't do that. She sits in the hard feelings. When she describes her daughter's anxiety or her son's defiance or her husband's frustration, she doesn't quickly pivot to "but it's all good." She stays in the discomfort long enough to let it teach her something. The trap would be using "brutiful" as a way to minimize pain rather than honor it.

The book's greatest gift is its specificity. Glennon doesn't write about "parenting struggles" in the abstract. She writes about the time her three-year-old threw a tantrum in the grocery store and she wanted to disappear. She writes about the fight with her husband over who left the milk out. She writes about the exact texture of shame — the hot, tight feeling of failing in public. These are not universal lessons. They're specific, lived moments. And that's what makes them universal.

One of the most powerful essays is about a woman named **Sister** — a mentor figure Glennon met in recovery. Sister told her that the opposite of addiction isn't sobriety. It's connection. This becomes the book's hidden thesis: what saves us is not willpower or knowledge or even love — it's allowing ourselves to be known. When Glennon writes about her bulimia, she's not confessing because she wants sympathy. She's confessing because she knows that the only way out of shame is exposure.

> "The opposite of addiction is not sobriety. It is human connection."

The parenting essays are some of the most vulnerable. Glennon describes a moment when her son was being difficult in a store and she felt the hot shame of being judged. She could have yelled, dragged him out, punished him. Instead, she got down on his level and whispered, "I see you're having a hard time. I'm here." And he calmed down. She wasn't being a good parent because she knew the right technique. She was being a good parent because she remembered what it felt like to be a child drowning and needing someone to notice.

But she also writes about the moments when she failed at this — when she screamed, when she retreated, when she chose her own comfort over her child's need. *Carry On, Warrior* doesn't present a perfect mother. It presents a real one, which is more useful.

The essays on marriage are where the cracks show. Glennon writes about her husband Craig, about their fights, about the gulf between them. She's trying so hard to be grateful, to focus on the good, to "choose love." Reading it now, knowing what came next — the divorce, the coming out, the relationship with Abby Wambach — you can feel the strain. She's papering over a wound that would eventually break open. At the time, she believed the paper was the solution. *Carry On, Warrior* captures her before she knew she was a cheetah.

One of the collection's most-read essays is **"Don't Carpe Diem."** It's a response to the pressure new parents feel to savor every moment. "People tell you to enjoy every minute of parenthood," Glennon writes. "That's a lie that makes exhausted, overwhelmed people feel like failures." Instead, she gives permission to just survive. Some moments are not magical. They're exhausting. Pretending otherwise doesn't make you a better parent — it makes you a more anxious one. This essay alone has been shared millions of times because it says what no one else will: you don't have to love every minute for your life to be meaningful.

Let me be honest about the criticism. This is a first book, and it shows. The essays are uneven — some are sharp and devastating, others feel like filler. Glennon's voice is still developing; she borrows the cadence of her favorite speakers and writers without fully owning it. The "rah-rah, you're a warrior" tone can feel hollow when the subject is genuinely heavy. And for readers who don't share her Christian faith, the frequent references to God and prayer can be alienating — though she's careful to frame it as her experience rather than prescription.

There's also a tension between her message of radical honesty and the careful curation of online confession. Glennon was building a brand even as she was breaking down. The vulnerability is real, but it's also performative — she knows which stories will resonate, which details to include and omit. That doesn't make the book dishonest, but it does make it strategic. And some readers will feel that.

Anyone in the messy middle of life — new parents, people early in recovery, anyone who feels like they're failing at being an adult. Also, fans of *Untamed* who want to see where the journey began.

Readers who prefer tight narrative arcs or fully developed philosophy. This is a collection of dispatches from the trenches, not a coherent worldview.

The word "brutiful" — you'll find yourself using it. And the image of a mother crouching down to whisper to her struggling child, because that's what we all need.

1. **Send one brutiful text.** Message a friend and share something both hard and beautiful from your week. Don't polish it. Just say it. That's how connection starts.

2. **Name one shame.** Write down one thing you're ashamed of — something you've never said aloud. You don't have to share it. Just write it. Exposure in private is the first step toward freedom.

3. **Carry on.** Pick one area of your life where you're struggling. Instead of fixing it, just stay present to it for 24 hours. Don't solve, don't escape. Carry it for one day. You're stronger than you think.
