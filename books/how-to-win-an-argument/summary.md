Most people think winning an argument means having the better facts. Cicero, the greatest orator in Roman history, would find this hilarious. For him, argument was not about truth — it was about moving people. He could take any side of any case and make it compelling, not because he was dishonest but because he understood that people are moved by emotion, storytelling, and character long before they're moved by logic. **How to Win an Argument** collects Cicero's rhetorical writings — mostly from *De Oratore* and other treatises — into a practical guide that is startlingly relevant two thousand years later. Every time you've seen a politician deflect a question, a lawyer spin a narrative, or a marketer sell you something you didn't need, you've seen Cicero's techniques in action.

Cicero lived through the death throes of the Roman Republic and made his name as a lawyer and senator by being the most persuasive man in Rome. He defended and prosecuted everyone from corrupt governors to assassins. He gave speeches that changed history and, eventually, one that got him killed. His writings on rhetoric represent the most complete system of persuasion the ancient world produced. This book, edited by James May, distills that system into categories and examples that read like a masterclass in manipulation — if you choose to use it that way.

Cicero's foundational framework is the three modes of persuasion — a structure that predates him but that he perfected:

**Ethos** — your character as a speaker. Before anyone hears your argument, they judge whether you're credible, trustworthy, and worth listening to.

**Pathos** — the emotions of the audience. Logic works on the mind, but emotion moves the body. People make decisions emotionally and justify them rationally.

**Logos** — the logic of the argument itself. The facts, the reasoning, the structure.

Cicero's insight: you need all three. Facts alone don't win arguments. A logical structure without emotional weight is dry and forgettable. Emotional appeal without credibility is manipulation. The master persuader builds all three simultaneously.

"The orator must be a good man, skilled in speaking," Cicero writes — and the order matters. Character comes first. Not because it's more important than skill, but because without character, skill is dangerous.

Thinking logic alone will persuade. It won't. If your audience doesn't trust you (ethos) or doesn't care about the outcome (pathos), the facts don't matter. Cicero's model forces you to ask: "Why should they listen to me?" and "Why should they care?" before you get to "What's the evidence?"

Cicero's second major contribution is the concept of **stasis** — finding the essential point of contention in any argument. Before you can argue, you need to know what you're *actually* arguing about.

He identifies four types of stasis:

Did it happen? (Fact)
What kind of thing is it? (Definition)
How serious is it? (Quality)
Is this the right forum to argue it? (Procedure)

Most bad arguments fail at this level. Two people arguing about politics might discover they don't even disagree about the facts — they disagree about what the facts *mean* (definitional stasis). Two partners arguing about a late arrival might be arguing about facts ("I was only five minutes late") when the real issue is quality ("Five minutes shows you don't respect my time").

"The wise orator discovers the true point of dispute," Cicero writes, "for in any controversy, the first task is not to answer the opponent but to find the question."

Arguing at the wrong level. You argue facts when the real disagreement is about values. You argue definitions when the real issue is emotions. Finding the stance means asking: "What are we actually fighting about?" before you start fighting.

Cicero's six-part speech structure is the backbone of Western persuasion. Every courtroom opening you've heard, every TED talk, every political stump speech follows some version of it:

Hook the audience. Establish goodwill. Make them want to listen.

Tell the story — but your version, naturally. Not "what happened" but "what happened from the perspective that makes my case."

State your points clearly. Tell them what you're going to argue. Cicero believed in clarity of structure: the audience should never be lost.

Your evidence, arguments, and reasoning. This is where logos shines — but it's built on the emotional foundation of the first three parts.

Anticipate and dismantle your opponent's strongest arguments. Cicero insists on doing this before the opponent makes them — kill the counterargument before it's born.

Summarize and appeal to emotion. Cicero's perorations were famous for their emotional power — he would move audiences to tears, anger, or pity with a few sentences.

"There is no natural order for a speech," Cicero admits. "But there is an effective order. The audience's mind has a structure. Your speech should match it."

Diving straight into your argument without establishing context or connection. Cicero would have hated the modern style of immediate bluntness. He believed that the first ten percent of any speech is about earning the right to be heard.

Cicero was unapologetic about using emotion. He didn't see it as manipulation — he saw it as aligning with reality. Humans are emotional creatures. A persuasive argument speaks to that reality.

"The delivery of speech should be adjusted to stir the feelings," he writes. "It is not enough to prove your case. You must make the judge *feel* the justice of it."

He outlines specific emotional strategies: make the audience feel pity for your client and anger at the opponent. Create fear about what happens if you lose. Build hope about what happens if you win. Connect your case to values they already hold.

His most famous example: in defending the poet Archias, Cicero didn't argue about facts or law. He spent most of the speech praising literature and culture — appealing to the jury's identity as educated Romans. He knew that if they felt proud of their learning, they'd rule in favor of a poet. It worked.

Thinking emotion is dirty or dishonest. Cicero would say that pretending emotion doesn't matter is the real dishonesty. All persuasion is emotional. The question is whether you're aware of the emotions you're invoking.

Ethos, for Cicero, was the most important of the three modes. He built elaborate systems for projecting credibility:

Show that you know what you're talking about. Cite specifics. Demonstrate deep knowledge of your subject.

Show that you care about the audience's interests, not just your own. A persuader who seems self-interested is instantly less credible.

Show that you're a person of character. This doesn't mean you're perfect — it means you have principles and you live by them.

Cicero's own life was a case study in ethos-building. He started as a "new man" — no senatorial ancestors — and rose to the highest office in Rome entirely through his reputation as an orator. His credibility was his currency.

"Let the orator himself be a man of high moral character," he writes. "The audience will forgive much in a person they trust, and forgive little in a person they suspect."

Trying to fake ethos. Audiences are remarkably good at detecting insincerity. Cicero's advice: don't pretend to have character. Actually develop it. The most persuasive people are the ones who are genuinely credible, not the ones who act like it.

One of Cicero's most surprising arguments is that humor is a serious rhetorical weapon. He devotes significant space to jokes, irony, and wit — because humor disarms. A well-timed joke makes the audience relax, makes the opponent look stiff, and makes the speaker look confident.

"Laughter is the most effective means of refuting an opponent," Cicero writes. "It clouds his judgment and wins the audience's favor."

He distinguishes between wit (the good kind — clever, relevant, surprising) and buffoonery (the bad kind — forced, crude, irrelevant). A master persuader uses humor like a surgeon's scalpel: precisely, rarely, and with devastating effect.

Overusing humor. Cicero warns that too many jokes make you seem unserious. A joke is powerful precisely because it's unexpected. If you're always joking, the audience stops taking you seriously even when you're not joking.

Persuasion is not about having the best facts. It's about understanding how human minds actually work. People are moved by character, emotion, and story before they're moved by logic. The master persuader builds credibility, stirs feelings, structures their case clearly, and anticipates objections — all before delivering a single fact. Cicero's system is two thousand years old, and it's still the most complete model of persuasion we have. Not because we haven't improved on the details, but because human nature hasn't changed.

The stasis framework — finding the actual point of disagreement — is the most useful tool in the book. I've started applying it in every argument I have. Before responding, I ask: is this a factual disagreement, a definitional one, or a values one? The answer changes everything. Someone who says "This movie is bad" isn't making a factual claim. They're making a qualitative one. Arguing about facts is pointless. The real conversation is about what makes a movie good.

The book is a collection of excerpts from Cicero's larger works, assembled for modern readers. It lacks the coherence of a single sustained argument. The translation choices are often clunky — the Latin rhetorical terms don't always land in English. And the advice is amoral: Cicero's system works equally well for truth and manipulation. He doesn't address the ethical dimension of persuasion, which is a significant omission for a modern reader. The editor's framing is also thin — you get Cicero raw, without much context about the political or personal stakes he was navigating.
