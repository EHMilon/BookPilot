Here's the sentence that stopped me: "We teach girls to shrink themselves." Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie wrote that in 2014, and it's been lodged in my skull ever since. *We Should All Be Feminists* is barely 50 pages — you can read it in an hour — but it's one of those rare pieces of writing that rewires something fundamental in how you see the world. It's not a comprehensive treatise on feminism. It's a personal essay that does something harder: it makes feminism feel obvious.

Adichie, the Nigerian novelist behind *Half of a Yellow Sun* and *Americanah*, originally delivered this as a TEDx talk in 2012. The video went viral, Beyoncé sampled it in "Flawless," and the essay was published as a standalone book with proceeds going to women's education in Nigeria. But its power doesn't come from its reach. It comes from the specific, lived texture of Adichie's examples — moments that are so familiar they're almost invisible, until she points at them and says, "This is not normal."

> "We teach girls to shrink themselves, to make themselves smaller. We say to girls, 'You can have ambition, but not too much. You should aim to be successful, but not too successful. Otherwise you would threaten the man.'"

**The Trap here** is thinking feminism is about women being angry at men. That's the caricature, and Adichie dismantles it with surgical precision. Feminism, in her telling, is not about hate. It's about noticing the way we raise boys and girls differently and asking: what if we just raised humans? The trap is getting stuck in the culture-war framing that turns feminism into a fight between genders, when the real fight is between a system that limits everyone and the people who want to be free from it.

Adichie structures the essay around a series of personal stories. She recalls being called a "feminist" as an insult by a male classmate in university — not because of anything she'd said, but because she'd argued with him about something. The label wasn't a description of her beliefs. It was a punishment for not knowing her place. She remembers a waiter in a Nigerian restaurant who ignored her and her female friend, directing all his attention to the man at the table — assuming the man was the one paying, the one in charge. She describes a male professor who genuinely believed women weren't suited for certain kinds of intellectual work, and who wasn't even aware his bias was a bias.

> "My own definition of a feminist is a person who says, 'Yes, there's a problem with gender as it is today, and we must fix it. We must do better.'"

**The Trap here** is thinking these are small things, isolated incidents, not worth making a fuss about. A waiter ignoring you. A classmate calling you a name. A professor who doesn't respect your mind. Each one is small. But Adichie's point is that the small things stack. They form a world where women, from childhood, learn that the default human is male. Every interaction, every assumption, every throwaway comment reinforces the message: you are the second sex. You're not angry about any single incident. You're exhausted by the cumulative weight.

One of the essay's most powerful passages is about how we raise boys. Adichie argues that feminism benefits men too — maybe as much as it benefits women. Because the same system that says "women should be nurturing" also says "men should be invulnerable." We teach boys to suppress their emotions, to never show weakness, to define their worth by their ability to provide and protect. This is a cage too. Adichie describes a male friend who couldn't cry in front of his own father. Another who was told that expressing vulnerability made him less of a man.

> "The problem with gender is that it prescribes how we should be rather than recognizing how we are."

**The Trap here** is zero-sum thinking — the belief that if women gain something, men must lose something. Adichie rejects this entirely. A world where women are free to be ambitious, emotional, and full humans is also a world where men are free to be vulnerable, nurturing, and full humans. Feminism isn't about taking power from men and giving it to women. It's about dismantling the system of rigid roles that makes everyone smaller.

Adichie also takes on the question of language and culture. She notes that in many Nigerian languages, the word for a feminist has a derogatory connotation — it implies a woman who can't get a husband, who is man-hating, who has failed at womanhood. She reclaims the word by being specific about what she means: "A feminist is a person who believes in the social, political, and economic equality of the sexes." That's it. That's the whole definition. Everything else is noise.

> "I am angry. We should all be angry. Anger has a long history of bringing about change."

The essay also addresses the uncomfortable issue of women's own complicity in patriarchy. Adichie describes watching female relatives enforce gender roles on younger women — teaching them to cook, to defer, to accept less. She's clear: women can be sexist too. The system perpetuates itself through everyone, including its victims. Recognizing that is not blaming women. It's acknowledging the depth of the problem. The essay closes with a vision — a future where we don't talk about gender as much, because it no longer determines what a person can become. "All of us would be happier — freer to be our full, complex selves." It's a hopeful ending, but not a naive one. Adichie knows how far we are from that world. Let me be honest about the criticism. *We Should All Be Feminists* is an essay, not a book, and it reads like one. It's thin — you could argue it doesn't go deep enough into structural oppression, systemic inequality, or intersectionality. Adichie is writing for a mainstream audience, and some critics have pointed out that her feminism is liberal and individualistic — focused on changing minds rather than changing systems. She doesn't address class, race, or global economics in any depth. The essay is an introduction, not an analysis.

There's also the question of who the essay is speaking to. Adichie writes as if her audience needs to be persuaded that sexism exists and is damaging. For readers already living that reality — Black women, poor women, trans women — the essay may feel like it's stating the obvious. Its power is as a gateway text, not a destination.

There's also the question of who "we" refers to. The title assumes universal agreement — "we should all be feminists" — but the essay is clearly addressed to people who aren't already convinced. For people who've been doing this work for decades, there's nothing new here. The essay's power is in its accessibility, not its originality.

Everyone. I mean that. Teenagers, adults, people who think they already agree and people who think they don't. It's the best 50-page introduction to feminism I've ever read.
