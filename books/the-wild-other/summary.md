Clover Stroud opens *The Wild Other* with her mother's brain — leaking, dying, a bubble of blood expanding inside her skull. She was sixteen. Her mother had been thrown from a horse on a country road in Oxfordshire, and the girl who arrived at the hospital didn't get to say goodbye to the woman who raised her. Her mother was gone, replaced by a body that lived for sixteen more years without ever becoming her again. That rupture — the sudden, violent transformation of a mother into an absence — is the wound that everything else in this book circles.

But *The Wild Other* is not a grief memoir. Not entirely. Because what Clover Stroud did with that wound was not process it in a therapist's office. She went looking for wildness. She found it in horses, of course — she grew up riding, and the connection between a woman and a half-ton animal that could kill her with a kick is the first wildness she knows. She found it in men — volatile, dangerous men. She found it in travel — a year in the American West, working on a ranch, falling in love with a cowboy whose violence was never quite beneath the surface. She found it in sex, in danger, in the ache of being alive after someone you love has stopped being alive.

> "I didn't know that grief could feel like a wild animal, one that lives inside you, sleeping and waking without your permission."

The book has the structure of a wound that keeps reopening. The chapters alternate between her teenage trauma and her adult life, but the timeline jumps and loops in a way that mirrors traumatic memory — you don't experience grief chronologically; you experience it associatively. A smell, a sound, a certain quality of light, and you're back in the hospital corridor. Stroud doesn't narrate her grief; she inhabits it. The prose is sensual, physical, sometimes almost unbearably direct.

> "I wanted to be broken open. I wanted the wild to tear through me and leave me raw."

It's a dangerous desire, and Stroud knows it. But she also knows that something in her needs to be broken before it can heal.

The book opens with a **prologue** that's almost unbearable. Stroud's mother, riding a horse named Dusty on a quiet country road. The horse spooked. A lorry coming the other way. The impact. Stroud arriving at the hospital to find her mother's skull open, her brain swelling, her face unrecognizable. The mother survived — survived for sixteen years in a state that was not quite life and not quite death, aware of nothing and no one, a body without a person inside it. Stroud doesn't linger on the medical details. She doesn't need to. The wound is in every sentence she writes, every choice she makes, every man she loves and every horse she rides. The book is not about the accident. The book is about what lives in the space that the accident created.

The **American West section** is the most vivid. Stroud goes to Wyoming to work on a ranch, and the landscape nearly swallows her. The vastness, the silence, the physical brutality of ranch work — it's the opposite of the English countryside she grew up in, and that's exactly what she needs. She falls in love with a man who takes her hunting, who teaches her to shoot, who brings her as close to the edge of violence as she can get without crossing it. She describes riding through snow, the horse's breath clouding, the silence so absolute that she can hear her own heartbeat. It's the first time since her mother's accident that she feels present in her own body.

> "I wanted to be touched by the wild. I wanted it to leave marks on me."

But the book's most powerful section is her return to England and her gradual, reluctant decision to have children. Stroud is terrifyingly honest about motherhood: she doesn't romanticize it. The body that carries a child, the body that tears to deliver it, the body that feeds it — she's acutely aware of the physicality of it all, and the way it mirrors the physicality of her mother's dying. She describes the moment of giving birth as "the wildest thing I've ever done" — not a moment of transcendence but of raw, primal power. The women in her family, she realizes, have always been defined by their bodies. Her mother was defined by the body that betrayed her. Stroud insists on being defined by the body that can create, and endure.

**The Trap,** which Stroud identifies clearly, is the romance of wildness — the idea that danger and intensity are the same as aliveness. She nearly falls into it more than once. The cowboy who bruises her. The horse that throws her. The pregnancy that nearly kills her. At a certain point, you have to ask: is this a life-affirming relationship with risk, or are you just trying to feel *something* because grief has numbed you to everything else? Stroud doesn't answer the question definitively. She just holds it up and lets it glint.

Stroud writes powerfully about **her own body as a site of wildness**. After her mother's accident, she became acutely aware of the fragility of the body — how quickly it could be broken, how irrevocably. But instead of retreating from that awareness into safety, she leaned into physical intensity. She describes riding bareback at a gallop through the Oxfordshire countryside, the horse's power thrumming up through her spine. She describes the aftermath of a fall: the broken collarbone, the concussion, the strange relief of a body that can still feel pain. There's a scene where she deliberately cuts herself — not suicidally, but as a way of confirming that she's still alive, that her body still registers. It's uncomfortable to read. It's meant to be.

The **horses** are the book's real through-line. Stroud writes about horses the way some people write about gods — with reverence, fear, and a kind of helpless love. There's a scene where she's riding bareback on a horse that doesn't fully trust her, and the horse spooks at something in the undergrowth. The world narrows to the animal's muscles tensing under her thighs, the heat of its flank, the calculation of whether to hold on or fall. Those moments, she says, are the only times her mind goes completely silent. The grief stops talking. The fear stops narrating. There's just the horse and her and the present instant, which is both terrifying and sacred.

> "Horses taught me to feel pain without running from it. They taught me that the only way through fear is into it."

**Criticism:** The book has a novelist's instincts but a memoirist's structural looseness. Some sections feel like they could have been cut without loss — a relationship in Italy, a briefly mentioned job, a friend who appears and disappears. Stroud's honesty is disarming, but it also means the book sometimes lacks the editing that would give it more narrative momentum. The prose is beautiful but occasionally overwritten — she reaches for the word "wild" so often that it starts to lose its charge. And the relationship with the American cowboy, which takes up a third of the book, ends without the emotional weight that the pages invested in it. It feels like Stroud is still in the middle of that story, not finished with it.

**What stuck with me** is the idea that grief can be a form of wildness. We think of grief as sadness — a heavy, still thing. But Stroud experiences it as a kind of feral energy, a hunger for intensity that makes ordinary life feel unbearable. She doesn't try to tame it. She rides it. And the book's power comes from her willingness to be vulnerable in that riding — to show us not the healed version of herself but the woman in the middle of the wound, still bleeding.
