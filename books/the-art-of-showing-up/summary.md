You know that friend who texts back three weeks late, apologizes profusely, says "let's definitely grab coffee soon," and then disappears for another month? That friend is not a bad person. That friend is probably overwhelmed, stretched thin, and carrying a quiet shame about every unanswered message in their inbox. **The Art of Showing Up** is a book for that friend, which is to say it's a book for almost everyone alive right now. Rachel Wilkerson Miller, who made her name writing about self-care at BuzzFeed and later at SELF, noticed something strange about the wellness conversation: everyone was talking about taking care of yourself, but almost no one was talking about how to take care of each other. Her book is a gentle, practical, deeply humane guide to being present for the people you love — not in theory, but in practice. Not perfectly, but consistently.

Miller's central claim is that "showing up" is a skill, not a personality trait. We treat reliable people as if they were born that way — steady, dependable, always there when it counts — when in fact the ability to show up is something you practice and get better at, like cooking or playing piano. The book breaks showing up into four parts: showing up for yourself, showing up for others, asking others to show up for you, and what to do when showing up is hard.

*The Trap:* The trap is to read this as a self-help book about being nicer. It's not. It's a skill book about being more competent at relationships. Miller is not interested in making you feel guilty about the texts you haven't answered. She's interested in giving you a system for answering them.

Before you can show up for anyone else, Miller argues, you need to show up for yourself — and this is where the book is most surprising. She defines showing up for yourself not as bubble baths and candles (though she's not against those) but as the boring, unglamorous work of paying attention to what you need and giving it to yourself before you're desperate. She talks about the "dread check" — a practice of checking in with yourself before social commitments to see if you actually want to go (enthusiastic yes), can go (reluctant but willing yes), or are going out of obligation (no). She gives permission to cancel. She gives frameworks for figuring out the difference between a real obligation and a made-up one.

The book's most useful tool here is the **"spoons" framework** — borrowed from the chronic illness community — where you imagine your energy as a finite number of spoons each day. Every task costs a spoon. Showing up for someone costs spoons. If you've already spent all your spoons, you cannot will yourself into having more. Miller adds an important update to this framework: the difference between visible and invisible spoon costs. Getting dressed and going to a party might cost one visible spoon, but if you're anxious or depressed or just exhausted, navigating small talk might cost two invisible spoons you didn't account for.

*The Trap:* A lot of self-care advice turns into permission to never do anything hard. Miller is careful to distinguish between honoring your limits and using them as an excuse. The goal is not to avoid discomfort but to know the difference between discomfort that will grow you and discomfort that will break you.

She reimagines Gary Chapman's framework for actual adult friendship — quality time, acts of service, words of affirmation, gifts, physical touch — and maps them onto concrete actions. If your friend's love language is acts of service, bringing them dinner after a breakup is showing up. If it's quality time, sending a DoorDash gift card misses the point.

Miller draws a pyramid with three levels. At the base is "maintenance" — regular, low-stakes contact. A text. A like. A "thinking of you." In the middle is "active support" — showing up during a specific hard time. At the top is "crisis" — the ambulance, the hospital, the all-hands-on-deck emergency. Her crucial insight: most of us reserve our showing up for the crisis level, and then wonder why our friendships feel thin. The work is in the base. The regular, boring, unglamorous maintenance. The reply that comes within a week. The "how did your presentation go?" that follows up on something you remembered.

When someone tells you something terrible, Miller offers a script that is so useful it should be printed on a card: "I'm so sorry that happened. What do you need right now? Do you want me to help you problem-solve, or do you want me to just listen? Do you want to be alone, or do you want company?" She calls this the "what do you need" protocol, and it solves one of the most common friendship problems: trying to fix when someone wants comfort, or offering comfort when someone wants solutions.

*The Trap:* Miller's frameworks can feel like they're overcomplicating something that should be natural — and that's because, for some people, it *is* natural. If you're someone who innately knows how to show up, the book will feel like a manual for a skill you already have. But for the rest of us — the ones who genuinely want to be there but don't know how — the structure is a gift.

This section is the emotional core of the book, and it's the part that Miller clearly had to learn the hard way. She writes about her own tendency to downplay her needs, to say "I'm fine" when she's not, to wait for people to read her mind and then feel resentful when they don't. The solution she offers is brutally simple: ask for what you need. But asking is hard, so she breaks it down into smaller steps: start with low-stakes requests to build the muscle, use "I" statements ("I'm having a hard week and I'd love to see you"), and be specific about what you want ("Can you bring dinner on Tuesday?" instead of "I could use some support").

The most powerful chapter is about what happens when you ask and the person doesn't show up. Miller is honest: it hurts. It might mean the friendship is not as strong as you thought. It might mean the person is going through something you don't know about. It might mean you need to widen your circle. She doesn't offer platitudes about "everything happens for a reason." She offers permission to grieve the disappointment and practical advice for evaluating whether the relationship is worth continued investment.

*The Trap:* Asking for what you need sounds simple in theory. In practice, it requires vulnerability that most of us have spent our whole lives avoiding. The book acknowledges that this is the hardest part and doesn't pretend there's a shortcut.

Miller devotes the final section to the situations where showing up is genuinely difficult: when you're the one going through it and can't show up for anyone; when the person you need to show up for has hurt you; when you're grieving; when you're depressed; when you're so burned out that your capacity for other people is zero. She offers not solutions — because there are no solutions for some of these — but strategies for minimizing damage and staying connected without depleting yourself.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is that friendship is not magic — it's maintenance. We treat good friends like they're rare gems that you're lucky to find, when in fact most of us lose good friendships not because they weren't strong enough but because we didn't water them. Miller's book reframed friendship for me from something you *have* to something you *do*. A friend is not a noun. It's a verb. You friend someone by showing up. The book made me look at my unanswered texts differently — not as a moral failing but as a skill deficit. And skills can be learned.

The book is relentlessly practical, which is both its strength and its weakness. Miller rarely ventures into the deeper psychological reasons we struggle to show up — attachment theory, family-of-origin patterns, the role of anxiety and depression. The book assumes you *want* to show up and just need the how-to, but for many readers, the problem is deeper than a lack of frameworks. The tone can also feel a bit too BuzzFeed-friendly at times — lots of listicles and bullet-point callouts and social-media-friendly language that can make the book feel lighter than its subject matter deserves. And the spoon theory, while useful, is borrowed heavily from other writers without sufficient credit or expansion.

If you've ever been called a bad friend and wanted to change, read this book. If you're the friend who always shows up and is tired of carrying the weight, read this book — the section on asking for help might be exactly what you need. If you're currently going through a hard time and don't know how to let people in, read this book. Skip it if you're looking for a deep psychological analysis of relationships; this is a practical handbook, not a therapy session. Skip it if bullet points and scripts feel condescending to you; Miller's tone assumes you need the basics, and that assumption might not fit.

1. **The maintenance text.** Pick one friend you haven't talked to in a while. No apology for the delay. Just: "Hey, I was thinking of you. How are things?" Send it right now.
2. **The dread check.** Before your next social commitment, check in: Do you want to go, can you go, or are you going out of obligation? Honor the answer. Cancel if you need to. But cancel early, not last-minute.
3. **Ask for one thing.** This is the hard one. Identify one need you've been waiting for someone to notice. Then tell them. Text a friend: "Can we talk for twenty minutes tonight? I'm having a rough one." Notice how it feels to ask.
4. **Map your support pyramid.** Draw three circles. Who's in your base (regular contact)? Who's in your active support (crisis crew)? Are there too few names? Too many? Fill in the gaps.
