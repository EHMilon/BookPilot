You are mostly not you. For every human cell in your body, there are roughly ten microbial cells — bacteria, fungi, viruses — living on your skin, in your mouth, and densely packed along every inch of your digestive tract. By cell count, you are about 10% human. The other 90% is a microscopic ecosystem that evolution has been fine-tuning for hundreds of millions of years. Alanna Collen, a biologist with a PhD in evolutionary biology from Imperial College London, wrote *10% Human* to make one argument that should terrify and liberate you in equal measure: the modern world has been systematically destroying your microbiome — and that destruction is the root cause of epidemics ranging from obesity to anxiety to autism.

Collen isn't a nutrition guru or a wellness blogger peddling a detox protocol. She's a working scientist who spent years in the lab. Her book is a deeply researched, often frightening look at how antibiotics, C-sections, processed food, and even the obsession with hygiene have created a public health crisis hiding in plain sight. She calls this "the extinction of the inner ecosystem."

Most people have heard of the "hygiene hypothesis" — the idea that our obsession with cleanliness has made our immune systems overreactive, leading to allergies and asthma. Collen takes this much further. She argues that the real problem isn't just cleanliness; it's the wholesale destruction of microbial diversity. The bacteria we evolved with for millions of years are disappearing from our bodies — and the consequences go far beyond seasonal allergies.

"The immune system is not a fortress to be protected from the outside world," she writes. "It is a garden that must be cultivated by the microbes that live within us." This reframing is the book's deepest contribution. We've been treating our bodies as though they're under siege from germs. Collen says we're the ones doing the besieging. The germs are mostly our allies.

She tells the story of the Yanomami people of the Amazon, who have the most diverse gut microbiomes ever recorded — about 50% more microbial species than the average Westerner. When they encounter modern humans, they don't get sick from our diseases. We get sick from the lack of *their* microbes. "Having a diverse microbiome," Collen notes, "is like having a well-stocked toolbox. When a problem arises, you have the right tool for the job." A depleted microbiome, by contrast, is like trying to build a house with only a hammer.

This is Collen's most provocative claim, and she backs it with a stack of studies. She describes experiments where researchers took gut bacteria from obese mice and transplanted them into lean mice — and the lean mice got fat without eating more food. The same has been shown in humans. Your gut bacteria don't just digest food; they extract energy from it, regulate appetite hormones, and influence which nutrients your body absorbs. Two people can eat the same meal and get different outcomes because they have different microbial residents.

"It turns out that the bacteria in your gut have a say in what you eat," Collen writes. "They produce compounds that can make you feel hungry or full, that can make you crave sugar or fat. You think you're making a free choice when you reach for the chocolate bar. But that choice is partly bacterial."

The Trap here is to turn this into a new form of determinism. "It's my gut bacteria, not my fault." Collen is careful: your microbiome is shaped by what you eat, how you're born, and the antibiotics you've taken. It changes. You can influence it. The problem is that the modern diet — high in processed foods, low in fiber — systematically feeds the wrong bacteria. "The bacteria that thrive on sugar and refined carbohydrates are the ones that send hunger signals to your brain," she says. "Feed them, and they multiply. And the more they multiply, the more they influence your cravings." It's a feedback loop that has nothing to do with willpower.

The most surprising section of the book is the link between gut bacteria and the brain. Collen walks through the evidence connecting gut dysbiosis (microbial imbalance) to depression, anxiety, and even autism. The gut-brain axis, she explains, is a two-way highway. Your brain sends signals to your gut (that's why you get butterflies before a presentation), but your gut sends signals back to your brain — and those signals are partly microbial.

She describes a study where mice given a probiotic strain of *Lactobacillus* showed fewer stress behaviors than controls. When their vagus nerve (the main communication line between gut and brain) was severed, the effect disappeared. The bacteria weren't making the mice calmer through some vague "wellness" mechanism. They were sending specific chemical signals along a specific nerve pathway. "We like to think of ourselves as rational beings," Collen writes dryly, "but our moods are partly determined by the bacteria in our colon deciding how we should feel."

The Trap here is over-interpretation. The gut-brain connection is real, but it's one variable among many. Depression isn't just "bad gut bacteria," and Collen doesn't claim otherwise. What she does is show that the microbiome is a meaningful lever — something we've been ignoring while reaching for pharmaceuticals. "We have treated the brain as though it exists in a sterile bubble," she writes. "It doesn't. It exists in a body that is host to trillions of microbes, and those microbes matter."

Collen devotes a sobering chapter to the damage done by antibiotics. She's careful to say she's not anti-antibiotic — they save lives. But the evidence that a single course of antibiotics can permanently reduce microbial diversity is strong. "The effect of antibiotics on your microbiome is like a forest fire," she writes. "Some species survive. Some don't. And the forest that regrows is never quite the same as the one that burned." Multiple courses in childhood appear to correlate with higher rates of obesity, allergies, and inflammatory bowel disease. Correlation isn't causation, but the pattern is consistent across dozens of studies.

C-sections are another frontier. Babies born vaginally are colonized by their mother's vaginal and gut bacteria — a microbial inheritance that primes the immune system. Babies born by C-section are colonized by whatever bacteria happen to be on the mother's skin, the hospital bed, and the doctor's hands. The microbial profiles are measurably different for months afterward, and C-section babies have higher rates of asthma, allergies, and obesity. Collen doesn't argue that C-sections are evil — many are medically necessary — but she argues we should be aware of the trade-off and explore solutions like "vaginal seeding" (swabbing the baby with the mother's vaginal fluids).

The processed food section is the most damning. Modern diets are dramatically lower in fiber than the diets our ancestors ate. Fiber is not just roughage — it's the food your beneficial bacteria need to thrive. Without it, they starve. "Our bodies haven't changed much in the last ten thousand years," Collen writes. "But our food has changed completely. And the microbes that depend on that food are suffering."

The book is excellent on diagnosis but weaker on prescription. Collen's recommendations — eat more fiber, avoid unnecessary antibiotics, consider fermented foods, prefer vaginal birth when possible — are sensible but not revolutionary. Readers hoping for a detailed protocol may be disappointed. The science sections can get dense; Collen is a researcher and it shows in the prose. There are stretches where the reader is swimming in study citations without enough narrative breathing room.

The broadest critique is that Collen sometimes overstates the certainty of the science. The microbiome field is young, many studies are correlational, and the causal mechanisms are still being worked out. Collen generally hedges well — she uses language like "suggests" and "may" — but the momentum of the book can sweep readers past the uncertainty. A layperson might finish it believing that gut bacteria explain everything from depression to cancer, which is not where the science is yet.
