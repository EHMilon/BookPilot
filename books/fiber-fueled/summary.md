Dr. Will Bulsiewicz — "Dr. B" to his patients and followers — was a classic gastroenterologist before he became a convert. He treated colitis, Crohn's, and IBS with the standard toolkit: steroids, immunosuppressants, biologic drugs. They worked about as well as putting a bandage on a severed artery. Then he discovered the work of Dr. Denis Burkitt, a missionary surgeon who noticed that rural Africans eating a high-fiber, plant-based diet had almost none of the chronic diseases killing Westerners. Bulsiewicz changed his diet. Then he changed his practice. Then he wrote *Fiber Fueled* — a book that makes the case that fiber is not just "good for you" but the single most potent therapeutic agent available, and we've been systematically removing it from our food for the last century.

The central argument is elegantly simple: fiber is the fuel for your gut microbiome. Without it, the trillions of bacteria in your colon starve. When they starve, they turn on you — they start eating the mucus layer that protects your intestinal wall, creating inflammation, permeability, and disease. Bulsiewicz calls this the **Gut Health Cycle**: fiber feeds good bacteria, good bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, SCFAs heal the gut lining and reduce inflammation. Remove fiber, and the cycle reverses. The trap is that most people think of fiber as roughage — the stuff that makes you regular. They don't think of it as the primary food source for an entire organ system (the microbiome weighs 2-4 pounds). Bulsiewicz wants you to understand that you're not just eating for yourself. You're eating for a hundred trillion tenants.

"I eat a high-fiber diet not because I'm virtuous, but because I know what happens when I don't," Bulsiewicz writes. "My gut bacteria start eating my colon." The book dives into the science of what SCFAs actually do. Butyrate, the most researched, is the primary energy source for colon cells. It's anti-inflammatory. It strengthens the gut barrier. It even has epigenetic effects, switching on genes that protect against cancer. Propionate travels to the liver and regulates cholesterol synthesis. Acetate enters the bloodstream and signals the brain about satiety. Bulsiewicz argues that the fiber-is-just-roughage view is like calling gasoline "just the stuff that makes the engine go" — technically true, missing the point entirely.

The book introduces **The 28-Day Plant-Based Gut Repair Program**, which is the practical core. It's not a low-carb diet. It's not paleo. It's not keto. Bulsiewicz is openly critical of these approaches because they restrict the very thing the microbiome needs most: fermentable fiber. He calls the high-fat, low-fiber diet "the single most effective way to destroy your gut microbiome." The program has two phases. Phase 1 (the first 14 days) is elimination: no meat, no dairy, no eggs, no alcohol, no added oil, no processed food. This resets the gut environment. Phase 2 is reintroduction — systematically adding foods back to see how your gut responds. The trap is that people go vegan or plant-based but eat processed junk — vegan cookies, plant-based burgers, refined carbs — which do nothing for the microbiome. Bulsiewicz emphasizes diversity of whole plants: 30 different plants per week is his target. The science supports this — the more diverse your plant intake, the more diverse your microbiome.

One of the book's strongest sections addresses the fear of **FODMAPs** — fermentable carbohydrates that cause gas and bloating in sensitive individuals. The low-FODMAP diet has become hugely popular for IBS, but Bulsiewicz warns it's a diagnostic tool, not a lifestyle. The low-FODMAP diet starves beneficial bacteria. Long-term use reduces microbiome diversity. His approach is the opposite: instead of restricting FODMAPs indefinitely, you heal the gut first (with fiber, probiotics, and anti-inflammatory foods), then reintroduce FODMAPs systematically. This is called **microbiome restoration** — fixing the ecosystem so it can handle fermentable foods again. The Low-FODMAP diet has become a standard GI recommendation for IBS, and Bulsiewicz respects its diagnostic utility. His issue is with people who stay on it for years. He's seen patients who've been Low-FODMAP for 6-12 months, restricting healthy foods like garlic, onions, apples, and legumes, and their microbiome diversity has collapsed. They have less bloating, sure, but they've also lost the bacteria that protect against colon cancer and regulate the immune system. The goal should always be to heal enough to expand the diet, not restrict forever. The trap is that people with gut issues often conclude "fiber hurts me" when what's actually happening is that their microbiome is so damaged that it can't handle fiber yet. The solution isn't less fiber. It's the right fiber at the right time, with the right support.

"Inflammation is your body's response to the gap between what was expected and what actually happened," Bulsiewicz writes. He applies this to the immune system's interaction with gut bacteria. When the microbiome is healthy, the immune system tolerates food antigens and commensal bacteria. When the microbiome is degraded, the immune system starts attacking — food sensitivities, autoimmune flares, allergies. This is the **hygiene hypothesis** updated for the gut: we're not too clean, we're too fiber-deprived. Bulsiewicz points to studies showing that children who grow up on farms, eating diverse plant foods and exposed to animal microbes, have dramatically lower rates of allergies and autoimmune disease. The mechanism, he argues, is a more diverse and resilient microbiome.

The book tackles **Fiber and Weight Loss** from a fresh angle. Bulsiewicz doesn't count calories. He counts fiber. He cites research showing that people who eat 30g of fiber per day lose as much weight as those on complex calorie-restricted diets — without counting a single calorie. The mechanism is multi-layered: fiber increases satiety (both physically and through SCFA signaling to the brain), reduces caloric absorption (by binding to fats and carbs in the gut), and shifts the microbiome toward bacteria that extract fewer calories from food. Yes, some bacteria are "extractors" — they harvest more energy from the same meal. A fiber-rich diet shifts the balance toward bacteria that leave more on the table. The trap is that fiber supplements (psyllium, inulin) are not the same as whole-plant fiber. They provide one type of fiber, not the hundreds of different compounds found in whole plants. Bulsiewicz is clear: supplement if you must, but don't pretend it replaces eating plants.

The honest weakness of *Fiber Fueled* is also its strength: the optimism. Bulsiewicz is so convinced that plants can heal everything that he sometimes glides over the reality of severe gut disease. For someone with advanced Crohn's or ulcerative colitis, a high-fiber diet can be genuinely dangerous during a flare. Bulsiewicz acknowledges this but doesn't spend enough time on how to navigate the severe cases. There's also an under-addressed tension: many people with IBS and SIBO genuinely cannot tolerate high-FODMAP fibers until they've addressed the underlying overgrowth, and Bulsiewicz's "repair first" approach can backfire if implemented too aggressively. And while he criticizes the low-carb movement with scientific rigor, he sometimes dismisses it rather than acknowledging that some people genuinely thrive on lower-carb approaches — genetics, metabolic health, and activity level create real variation.

Anyone with chronic digestive issues who's been on the medication treadmill. People who want to lose weight without counting calories. Vegetarians and vegans looking for a science-backed framework. Anyone in an acute Crohn's or UC flare (consult your GI first). People who can't tolerate significant dietary change due to eating disorder history. Anyone looking for a low-effort solution — this program requires real cooking.

Count how many different plants (vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, herbs, spices) you ate in the last 24 hours. If it's under 10, that's the problem. If it's under 5, you're in the danger zone. Tomorrow, eat at least 5 different plants at lunch. Don't overthink — a lentil soup with onions, carrots, celery, and turmeric counts.

Look up the fiber content of everything you ate yesterday. Add it up. If it's under 25g (women) or 30g (men), you're starving your microbiome. The single highest-leverage swap: replace white rice with lentils or chickpeas. A cup of lentils has 15g of fiber. A cup of white rice has 0.6g.

Cook one meal that contains at least 10 different plants. Bulsiewicz's suggestion: a giant salad with mixed greens, shredded cabbage, carrots, bell peppers, cherry tomatoes, cucumber, red onion, toasted pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, and a lemon-tahini dressing with garlic and parsley. Eat it slowly, without distractions. Notice the difference in how you feel versus a typical meal.

For the next 72 hours, write down every meal, your stool consistency (Bristol Stool Scale), your energy levels, and any bloating or discomfort. This is not for guilt — it's data. Bulsiewicz's program starts with knowing your baseline. Most people have no idea what their normal is because they've never paid attention.
