You know that feeling when you solve a problem, feel the relief wash over you, and then — three weeks later — the exact same problem comes back, wearing a slightly different face? That's not bad luck. That's the fast fix cycle. And Carl Honoré wrote an entire book about how to break out of it.

*The Slow Fix* came a decade after *In Praise of Slowness*, and it's a narrower, sharper book. If the first one was about pace of life broadly, this one is about a single, devastating observation: we are epidemic-level bad at solving problems permanently. We patch, we bandage, we put a fresh coat of paint on a rotting wall. And then we're surprised when the wall collapses.

Honoré's background in investigative journalism serves him well here. He spent five years researching case studies across fields — from medicine to education to business to criminal justice — trying to understand what made some solutions stick while others evaporated. The pattern he found is almost embarrassingly consistent: the quickest fix is almost never the deepest one. And deep solutions, while they take more time upfront, save exponentially more time on the back end.

> "A slow fix is not slow in the sense of dragging its feet. It is slow in the sense of taking the time to get things right."

Take the case of Dr. Donald Berwick, whom Honoré profiles at length. In 2004, Berwick launched a campaign called the 100,000 Lives Campaign, aimed at reducing preventable deaths in American hospitals. The conventional approach would have been to issue directives, set targets, and demand compliance. Berwick did something different. He spent months building relationships with hospital staff, understanding their workflows, listening to their frustrations, and co-creating solutions rather than imposing them. The campaign exceeded its goal — saving an estimated 122,000 lives — not because Berwick had better answers, but because he took the time to help hospitals find their own.

**The Trap of the fast fix** is that it's not just ineffective — it's addictive. Fixing something quickly gives you a dopamine hit. You feel competent. You close the ticket. You move on. But shallow fixes create what Honoré calls "fix fatigue" — the exhausted feeling of repeatedly solving the same surface-level problem while the root cause remains untouched. It's mopping the floor while the sink is overflowing.

Honoré identifies a set of principles that characterize deep, lasting solutions. He calls them the "Slow Fix Principles," and they're worth walking through because they form the book's skeleton.

**1. Face the full problem.** The American medical system treats symptoms, not root causes. A patient comes in with chest pain, gets a stent, goes home — and no one asks about the stress of his job or the fact that he eats fast food in his car four times a week because his employer doesn't give him a real lunch break. Facing the full problem means sitting in the discomfort of complexity rather than immediately looking for the lever that will make the discomfort go away.

**2. Think long.** The slow fix asks a single question that fast fixes never ask: *What will this solution look like in five years?* In education, this means moving beyond test-score accountability — which produces short-term gains and long-term damage — toward genuine learning outcomes. In business, it means investing in training and culture rather than quarterly results.

**3. Go deep, not wide.** Honoré tells the story of the Memorial Hospital in South Bend, Indiana, which was hemorrhaging money and patients in the 1990s. A fast fix would have been to cut costs, fire staff, and run a marketing campaign. Instead, a new CEO, Philip Newbold, spent two years changing the *culture* — not the balance sheet. He focused on empathy, communication, and staff morale. The hospital turned around financially — but that was a byproduct, not the goal. He went deep on the root cause (a broken culture) and the financial results followed.

**4. Engage everyone.** The most durable fixes are not handed down from experts. They emerge from the people who will live with the solution. Honoré profiles a Canadian forestry company that was locked in a bitter conflict with environmentalists. The fast fix would have been to sue them or hire PR. Instead, the company invited the activists to the table, gave them access to internal data, and spent two years working through disagreements. The resulting plan was slower to develop but had zero resistance to implementation — because everyone had a hand in building it.

**The Trap of collaboration** is that it can become "process paralysis" — endless meetings where everyone's voice is heard and nothing gets decided. Honoré is careful to distinguish slow fix engagement from bureaucratic slow-walking. The difference is intentionality. Are you meeting because you need diverse input to make a genuinely better decision? Or are you meeting to avoid making a decision? One is slow fix; the other is just slow.

**5. Accept the mess.** This is the psychological hardest principle. Fast fixes are attractive because they create clean narratives: "we identified the problem, implemented the solution, case closed." Slow fixes are inherently messy. They involve trial and error, partial progress, setbacks, and mid-course corrections. Honoré quotes the urban planner Jane Jacobs: "Cities have the capability of providing something for everybody, only because, and only when, they are created by everybody." The mess isn't a bug — it's the feature. Real problems don't yield to neat solutions.

> "A slow fix is not a quick answer but a lasting one. It is not a search for the simplest solution but the most effective one."

The book's most powerful example is about punishment. Honoré visits the Halden Prison in Norway, often called the world's most humane prison. The fast fix approach to crime is to lock people up for longer, hoping the threat of punishment will deter future crime. Norway tried something different: treat prisoners as people who will eventually re-enter society, and design the prison experience to make that re-entry successful. Cells have flat-screen TVs and en-suite bathrooms. Guards eat meals with prisoners. There are kitchens, workshops, and music studios.

Norway has one of the lowest recidivism rates in the world — around 20%, compared to 50-60% in the UK and US. The Norwegian approach is more expensive upfront.

> "The slow fix is not about doing things slowly. It's about doing them right the first time."

It takes longer to show results. It's politically unpopular to be "soft on crime." But it produces a lasting fix to a problem that the US and UK have been fast-fixing for decades with tougher sentencing — and failing.

I'll admit: this is where the book reaches its emotional peak for me. The Halden case is devastating because it exposes the gap between what we *know* works and what we're *willing to do*. The fast fix isn't always ignorance. Sometimes it's cowardice. We know the deeper solution. We just don't have the courage to implement it.

Honoré extends this logic to parenting, education, relationships, and personal health. The pattern is the same everywhere: we default to speed because speed feels like action, and action feels like progress. But treating the symptoms doesn't cure the disease — it just trains the disease to be more resilient.

The book's second half loses some steam as it tries to apply the framework to too many domains, and some of the business case studies feel cherry-picked. Not every slow fix succeeds, and Honoré doesn't spend enough time on the ones that failed despite "doing everything right." The principles are sound, but he oversells their universal applicability.

> "The fast fix is like building a house on sand. It looks good at first, but the cracks will come."

Still, the book belongs next to *Thinking, Fast and Slow* on the shelf of anyone who's tired of solving the same problems twice.

Honoré's diagnosis is too sharp to ignore: we're not bad at solving problems — we're bad at being patient with the problem long enough to solve it properly.

The speed of a solution and its durability are inversely related. The faster the fix, the shorter its shelf life. The slow fix requires sitting in discomfort, engaging the messy complexity of real systems, and accepting that lasting solutions take more time upfront. But they save exponentially more time on the back end — because you stop re-solving the same problem. The choice isn't between fast and slow. It's between solving it once and solving it forever.

The Halden Prison story. I can't stop thinking about it because it challenges our most basic assumptions about justice. We believe punishment should be unpleasant — that's the point. But Norway asked a different question: what if the point of prison is to produce someone who won't commit another crime? When you ask that question, the fast fix (longer sentences, harsher conditions) doesn't just fail — it's actively counterproductive. The slow fix (real rehabilitation) makes you uncomfortable. It offends your sense of fairness. But it works. That gap — between what feels satisfying and what works — is where Honoré's entire argument lives.

> "When you face a complex problem, the most expensive decision you can make is to choose the cheap, quick solution. Because you will pay for it again, and again, and again."

The book reads at times like a TED Talk stretched to book length.

The principles are solid, but the examples can feel repetitive — you get the pattern after three case studies, and there are about twelve. Honoré also glosses over the structural barriers to slow fixes. In a political system driven by election cycles, a five-year solution is a non-starter. In a business world driven by quarterly earnings, investing in culture without immediate returns gets you fired. His advice is aimed largely at decision-makers with the power to act, which means the book is most useful for people who are already in positions of influence — which, by definition, is not the majority of readers. And the "slow fix" framing can feel like a rebranding of established management principles (systems thinking, root cause analysis, iterative improvement) rather than an original contribution.
