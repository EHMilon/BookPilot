**Think Like a Monk** by Jay Shetty

The fitness influencer turned Vedic monk turned motivational speaker — it sounds like a parody of the modern self-help industry. Jay Shetty's origin story is unusual enough to be genuine: he grew up in a British-Indian family, went to business school, got a corporate job at Accenture, then left it all to train as a monk in Mumbai. He spent three years living on a temple floor, meditating at 4 AM, and studying ancient Vedic texts, sleeping in a dormitory with forty other monks. Then he left the monastery to bring the teachings to the secular world. *Think Like a Monk* is the result: a practical guide to applying monastic principles to ordinary life. It's aimed at people who feel overwhelmed by ambition, social media, comparison, and the pressure to achieve. Shetty's core argument is that you don't need to join a monastery to benefit from monastic training. You can learn to think like a monk while living in the world — holding a job, maintaining relationships, scrolling through Instagram.

The first lesson of monastic training is the most counterintuitive for an achievement-obsessed culture: your identity is more important than your achievements. Monks spend years cultivating a sense of self that is not dependent on external validation. A monk who is scrubbing floors and a monk who is leading a meditation retreat are equally valuable — because value is intrinsic, not functional. The monastery is organized around this principle: every task, no matter how humble, is equally an offering.

"Your identity is the only thing that stays with you forever," Shetty writes. "Achievements come and go. Promotions, awards, recognition — they're like weather. Your identity is the climate."

Shetty uses the monk's concept of *dharma* — not just your career or destiny, but your unique contribution to the world, the thing you're meant to do that only you can do. He distinguishes dharma from *karma* (action) and *artha* (wealth). Dharma is not discovered by thinking about what you want; it emerges through service, through paying attention to what brings you into a state of flow, through noticing what people thank you for.

The trap of achievement addiction. You get the promotion, the degree, the follower count, and you feel empty — because you used the achievement to define yourself, and achievements are never enough. The monk's alternative: define yourself by your values and let your achievements be expressions of those values, not their source. When your identity is secure, you can achieve without needing the achievement to hold you up.

The most practical section of the book deals with the mind's default negativity. Monks are not naturally serene; they train their minds away from negativity through daily practice. Shetty breaks this down into a three-stage process: label, reframe, release.

**Labeling** is the first step. When a negative thought arises, you don't fight it; you name it. "I am having a thought of jealousy." "I am having a thought of inadequacy." The act of labeling creates distance between you and the thought. You are not the thought; you are the observer of the thought. In the monastery, monks practice this so consistently that they can watch their emotions rise and fall like waves without being swept away.

"Monks don't have fewer negative thoughts," Shetty writes. "They have a different relationship with them. They notice them earlier, and they don't hold on as long."

**Reframing** is the second step. Monks ask: what can I learn from this? Every obstacle is also a teaching. Jealousy reveals what you value. Failure reveals what you need to learn. The art is in the reframing — turning a liability into an opportunity. Shetty's own story: when a potential investor told him his ideas about combining ancient wisdom with modern media were "too confusing," he didn't get discouraged. He reframed the rejection as a sign that he needed to simplify his message.

**Releasing** is the difficult third step. The monk's goal is not to eliminate negative thoughts — that's impossible, and trying is a source of additional suffering. It's to let them pass without attachment. Thoughts are like clouds. They come, they go. The problem is when you build a house on the cloud and pretend it's solid ground.

Trying to think positively all the time. Shetty is careful to distinguish the monk's approach from toxic positivity. The goal is not to replace negative thoughts with positive ones — that's suppression, not transformation. The goal is to see thoughts clearly and choose which ones to engage with.

The monk's concept of detachment is deeply misunderstood in the West. It's not about not caring. It's about not *needing*. Shetty distinguishes between preferences and attachments. It's fine to prefer success, comfort, and companionship. The problem is when preference becomes attachment — when you cannot be happy without it.

The monk trains by practicing small detachments: going without food for a day, sleeping on a hard floor, staying silent for weeks. The goal is not asceticism for its own sake but the realization that you can survive without things you thought you needed. Each small detachment is a proof: I was wrong about what I needed to be okay.

Shetty's most striking example is about his relationship to food. As a monk, he ate the same simple meal every day for three years. When he left the monastery, he found himself overwhelmed by the choices in a supermarket. He had to retrain himself to see food as nourishment, not comfort. The monk's detachment from food preferences wasn't a deprivation; it was a liberation from the constant mental energy spent on choosing, craving, and regretting food.

"The less you need," Shetty writes, "the freer you are. Freedom is not having more. It's needing less."

Mistaking detachment for apathy. The monk who is detached from outcomes is not indifferent to the world; they are more present, more engaged, because they're not distracted by the anxiety of results. The trap is thinking that detachment means not caring. It means caring without clinging.

The monk's path is not about self-improvement in the Western sense. It's about service. Shetty argues that the single most reliable source of meaning is helping others. This is not a sentimental claim; it's a practical one. The monks Shetty lived with were deeply happy, not because they had no problems but because their lives were organized around contribution.

Shetty's method for finding your purpose is refreshingly concrete. He suggests a three-step process: (1) list the things you're good at, (2) list the things you love doing, and (3) list the problems you see in the world. Your purpose is at the intersection. It's not a mystery to be revealed; it's a design problem to be solved.

The final section of the book is about gratitude, and it's where Shetty's monks training shows most clearly. He doesn't mean gratitude in the popular sense of "count your blessings." He means a daily, systematic practice of noticing what is already present. In the monastery, monks end every day with a gratitude reflection — not for the big things but for the small ones. A warm meal. A kind word. The fact that the roof didn't leak.

The psychology is classic but effective: the mind has a negativity bias that evolved for survival. Left unchecked, it scans for threats, losses, and deficiencies. Gratitude is the counter-practice. It trains the mind to scan for gifts instead of gaps.

"Gratitude," Shetty writes, "is not a feeling. It's a discipline. You don't wait to feel grateful. You practice gratitude until it becomes a habit, and the feeling follows."

Monastic wisdom is not about withdrawing from the world. It's about learning to live in the world without being consumed by its demands. The monk's tools — identity over achievement, labeling over suppression, detachment over clinging, service over ambition — are not airy ideals. They are practical disciplines that can be practiced by anyone, anywhere, regardless of their circumstances. The question is not whether you can think like a monk. The question is whether you're willing to do the work of noticing your mind, choosing your values, and training yourself to live by them.

The concept of *dharma* as the intersection of your gifts and the world's needs — and the monk's insistence that it's found through service, not endless introspection. The three-stage model for working with negative thoughts (label, reframe, release) is genuinely useful in moments of emotional overwhelm. And the image of the monk who doesn't have fewer problems but relates to problems differently — that's the core shift the book is asking for.

*Think Like a Monk* is too long. Shetty repeats his points many times, and the book would be stronger at half the length. The tone occasionally tips into the platitudinous — there are sentences that sound like they were written for Instagram captions rather than a book. The personal stories are effective but can feel calculated; you're always aware that Shetty is crafting a brand as much as sharing wisdom. The Vedic sources are quoted selectively to support the argument rather than explored in their own complexity. And the book's engagement with the genuine challenges of modern life — systemic inequality, structural oppression, mental illness, the genuine difficulty of "detaching" when you're struggling to survive — is superficial. It's a book for people who have enough and need to feel better about how they relate to it, not for people who are in crisis. The monk who lives inside a temple and the executive who meditates in their penthouse are not facing the same difficulties, and Shetty doesn't adequately acknowledge that gap.

Anyone who feels caught in the hamster wheel of achievement and social comparison. Anyone looking for a practical, structured approach to ancient wisdom with modern examples. Anyone who finds YouTube monks palatable rather than cringe. Skip it if you're looking for deep philosophical engagement with Eastern spirituality, if you find Shetty's polished influencer style grating, or if you're in a genuinely difficult life situation where "detachment" feels like a privilege you can't afford.

1. Identify one achievement you're using to define your worth. Write down: what would I be without this achievement? If the answer is "nothing," that's exactly the problem the monk's training is designed to solve.

2. The next time a negative thought arises, stop and label it: "I am having a thought of _." Say it out loud. Notice the distance the label creates between you and the thought. You are not the thought; you are the one who noticed it.

3. Identify one person you're jealous of. Instead of suppressing the jealousy or acting on it, ask: what does this jealousy reveal about what I actually value? Write down the honest answer. That's a signal about what you might want to pursue.

4. Practice the monk's morning: before you check your phone, take three conscious breaths. Set one intention for the day. Not a goal — an intention. How do you want to *be* today, not what do you want to *do*? End the day by asking: did I live that intention, even imperfectly?
