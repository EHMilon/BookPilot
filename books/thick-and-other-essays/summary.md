The first time someone called Tressie McMillan Cottom "thick," it was not a compliment. She was a fat Black girl in the American South, and the word was deployed the way words are always deployed against bodies that take up too much space — as a correction, a judgment, a way of saying *you are too much and not enough at the same time*. She took the word anyway. She turned it over in her hands, felt its weight, understood its history, and then she wrote a book that makes you feel the word in your bones — not as an insult, but as a description of what it means to be a Black woman in America. **Thick: And Other Essays** is a collection of nine essays that move between sociology, memoir, cultural criticism, and the kind of sharp-eyed analysis that Cottom has spent her career developing as a professor and public intellectual. It was a finalist for the National Book Award, and reading it, you understand why: every page feels like the author has been thinking about this sentence for years.

Cottom writes in the tradition of James Baldwin and Zora Neale Hurston, where the personal is never just personal — it's evidence. Each essay in this collection starts with a story about her own life and then opens outward into a larger argument about race, class, gender, and the structures that shape all of them. The essay "In the Name of Beauty" begins with her experience getting her hair done at a Black salon and becomes a stunning analysis of how Black women's bodies are commodified, policed, and judged. The essay "Know Your Blacks" starts with a white journalist asking Cottom to explain Black people to white readers and becomes a meditation on the extractive nature of diversity work in media.

*The Trap:* The temptation is to read these as "personal essays" in the modern sense — vulnerable, relatable, emotionally resonant. Cottom is doing something different. She is using her life as data, not as content. The difference matters. A personal essayist asks you to feel with them; Cottom asks you to think with her.

The title essay is the book's masterpiece, and it deserves the space Cottom gives it. She writes about being a fat Black woman in academia — a world that is obsessed with thinness, whiteness, and the performance of professionalism. She traces the history of "thick" as a term, from its use to describe enslaved people deemed strong enough for hard labor, to its reclamation in Black vernacular as a compliment for curvy women, to its contemporary ambiguity — at once a celebration and a limitation.

The central insight of the essay — and perhaps the book — is that Black women's bodies are never just bodies. They are public property, read and judged and commodified in ways that white women's bodies are not. Cottom writes about being called "angry" when she is passionate, "aggressive" when she is assertive, "too much" when she is present. She writes about the exhausting calculus of respectability politics — the constant negotiation between being seen and being safe.

The essay's most devastating passage is about the time she was nearly raped, and the man stopped because, she believes, he looked at her and saw a person instead of a body. She writes: "I have spent a lot of time wondering what, exactly, he saw in that moment. I want to believe it was my humanity. But I know that for Black women, humanity is not always visible." She does not resolve this question. She lets it sit, uncomfortable, the way it should.

*The Trap:* There's a tendency in writing about body politics to either celebrate reclamation uncritically ("love your body!") or to wallow in victimhood. Cottom does neither. She insists that the body is a site of both oppression and joy, and she refuses to let you settle on one feeling.

One of the most talked-about essays in the collection is "Dying to Be Competent," in which Cottom examines the death of Black women in America — specifically the high maternal mortality rates, the violence, the ways that Black women die younger and sicker than everyone else. She connects the dots between individual tragedy and structural failure: the Black woman who dies in childbirth because her pain wasn't taken seriously, the Black woman who is shot by police and described as a "threat," the Black woman who works herself to death because she cannot afford to stop.

Cottom's argument is that competence kills Black women. The expectation that Black women must be *better* than everyone else to be seen as *equal* — the "twice as good to get half as much" framework — creates a death spiral of overwork, neglected health, and chronic stress. She writes about her own health scares, the way she ignored symptoms because she was too busy being competent to be sick.

*The Trap:* White readers, especially well-meaning ones, can read this essay and feel guilty without changing anything. Cottom seems to anticipate this and pushes past guilt into a harder question: what would it mean to *see* Black women as fully human, not as super-competent workers or sources of wisdom or objects of pity, but as people with the same right to frailty and failure as everyone else?

Throughout the collection, Cottom returns to a theme that she has explored in her academic work: the emergence of a Black female professional class and the particular pressures it faces. In "Black Girlhood, Interrupted," she writes about the ways that Black girls are denied the category of childhood — seen as older, more sexual, less innocent than white girls of the same age. In "The Price of Fabulousness," she examines the economic realities behind Black women's fashion and beauty labor, arguing that the pressure to look good is both a form of resistance and a tax on survival.

Her analysis of respectability politics is particularly sharp. She has no patience for the argument that if Black people just behaved better, they'd be treated better. She calls this "the bootstraps of the left" — a way for progressive people to distance themselves from racism without actually challenging it. Her alternative is not a rejection of respectability but a clear-eyed account of its costs: "Respectability is a defense, not a strategy. It may keep you safe in a particular moment, but it will not free you."

*The Trap:* Cottom's analysis can feel relentless, even bleak. She does not offer easy solutions, and readers who need hope packaged with their criticism will find the book exhausting. That's the point. Some problems don't have solutions yet, and pretending they do is dishonest.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is Cottom's framing of competence as a survival strategy that becomes a death sentence. I've seen this pattern in my own life — the way that being reliable becomes a trap, the way that being good at your job means you're asked to do more, the way that being strong means no one asks if you need help. Cottom made me wonder what it would look like to be less competent on purpose, to let things fall, to see what happens when you're not the one holding everything up. She doesn't advocate for this exactly — she's too honest about the risks — but she names the cost of competence in a way that makes you feel it.

The collection is uneven. The essays that lean more into academic sociology can feel dense and jargon-heavy, especially for readers who don't have a background in critical theory. The essay "The Crisis of Black Womanhood, or What Makes Someone a 'Refugee'?" is as rigorous as anything Cottom has published in academic journals, which means it's also less accessible than the more personal pieces. The essay about her father's illness, while moving, feels slightly out of place in the collection's broader argument. And the book's refusal to offer solutions — while intellectually honest — can leave readers feeling stuck. Cottom diagnoses with surgical precision but prescribes sparingly.
