Western psychology has a bias you probably never noticed. It prizes independence. The healthy adult is autonomous, self-reliant, capable of standing alone. Dependency is framed as a developmental stage to be outgrown, a pathology in adults, a sign of weakness. Takeo Doi, a Japanese psychiatrist trained in both Japanese and Western traditions, wrote **The Anatomy of Dependence** to challenge this assumption. His argument: the capacity for healthy dependence is not a weakness — it is a fundamental human need that Western psychology pathologized because it couldn't fit into its cultural framework of individualism.

Doi's central concept is *amae* — a Japanese word that has no precise English equivalent. It derives from the verb *amaeru*, meaning "to depend on another's love" or "to behave in a way that invites another's indulgence." The prototypical example is the infant who reaches for its mother, expecting to be held without having to ask. Doi argues that this need never disappears. It transforms, but it persists throughout life. In Japanese culture, amae is recognized and accepted. The expectation that close others will anticipate your needs, the comfort of being able to let your guard down, the pleasure of being cared for — these are not signs of immaturity. They are signs of trust. Doi describes Japanese relationships as being organized around the mutual satisfaction of the amae need. A husband says to his wife: "It's cold" — and she brings him a jacket without being asked. That is amae in action. The Trap is that you've been trained to read this as a failure of communication. "Why didn't he just ask?" Because asking would destroy the entire structure of the interaction. The pleasure is in being understood without having to explain. In Japanese psychology, this is not laziness. It's intimacy.

Doi traces how Western psychology pathologized dependence through the lens of Freudian and post-Freudian theory. The healthy adult, in this framework, is independent, individuated, separate. The dependent adult is regressed, stuck, unhealthy. Doi examines this assumption critically and finds it — not wrong, exactly, but culturally specific. It reflects Western individualism, not universal human development. He points to the mother-child relationship as the universal foundation, and argues that the infant's amae is not something to be overcome but something to be transformed into adult forms of connection. Doi draws on his clinical experience with Japanese patients to show how their psychological distress often presents differently — not as fear of dependence but as inability to find a safe person to depend on. "The psychological development of the individual is not from dependence to independence," Doi writes, "but from one form of dependence to another." The Trap is that Western psychotherapy may inadvertently reinforce the very independence that leaves people isolated. Doi isn't arguing for codependence. He's arguing that the complete absence of dependence is not freedom. It's a particularly lonely form of isolation.

Doi shows how amae structures Japanese relationships at every level. The hierarchical relationships in Japanese companies — the *senpai* (senior) and *kohai* (junior) system — are organized around amae. The senior takes care of the junior, and the junior depends on the senior. This is not exploitation. It's a culturally recognized form of connection that provides both security and guidance. The Japanese language itself reflects amae: there are specific grammatical forms for speaking to superiors, to equals, to inferiors. These are not just politeness markers. They are acknowledgments of where each person stands in relation to the other, and of the mutual obligations that come with that position. Doi also examines the dark side of amae — how it can lead to irresponsibility, to the expectation that others will clean up your messes, to a childlike avoidance of adult accountability. He describes how some Japanese adults become trapped in a pattern of excessive amae — expecting constant care while giving nothing back. The concept is not romanticized. Doi treats amae as a neutral force — healthy in the right context, destructive when unchecked. The Trap is either celebrating amae uncritically ("Japan is so wonderfully connected!") or condemning it entirely ("Japan is so pathologically dependent!"). Doi's contribution is more subtle: amae exists everywhere in human relationships. Japan simply gives it a name and a recognized place in the social architecture.

Doi makes a crucial distinction that gets lost in most discussions of his work. Healthy amae is mutual — both parties give and receive. Neurotic amae is one-sided — the person who always depends without ever giving back, or the person who always gives without ever allowing themselves to depend. Doi draws on his clinical experience to show how Japanese patients present with amae-related issues differently from Western patients. A Japanese patient might complain of feeling "cut off" from others, of not being able to *amaeru* — to find someone safe enough to depend on. The treatment, in Doi's framework, is not to teach independence (as a Western therapist might) but to help the patient find healthy, mutual relationships of dependence. "The goal of therapy is not to eliminate amae," he writes, "but to transform it from a source of suffering into a source of connection." This reframing has profound implications for how we think about mental health across cultures.

In the book's most unexpected chapter, Doi explores amae in religious experience. He argues that the concept of God as a loving parent, the practice of prayer (reaching out to a higher power for comfort), the experience of surrender in meditation — these are all expressions of the amae need at a spiritual level. He draws on St. Paul, on Zen Buddhism, on the Psalms, to show that the desire to be held, to be cared for, to depend on something larger than yourself, is not a stage to outgrow. It's a permanent feature of human consciousness that expresses itself through whatever framework is available. The Trap is seeing this as reductionist — as if Doi is saying "religion is just amae." His point is more generous: the experience of dependence is so fundamental to being human that it generates entire systems of meaning. The capacity for trust, faith, and surrender that you bring to relationships is the same capacity you bring to spiritual life.

Doi also explores the relationship between amae and the Japanese concept of *enryo* (reserve or restraint). In Japanese social interaction, the ability to read when amae is appropriate and when enryo is required — knowing when to depend and when to hold back — is a sophisticated social skill, not a contradiction. The interplay between these two forces creates the texture of Japanese relationships. Doi's insight is that Western psychology has no equivalent framework for understanding this balance, so it tends to see either complete independence (enryo taken too far) or unhealthy dependence (amae taken too far) without recognizing the dynamic relationship between them.

The book is a classic for good reason, but it has limitations. Doi wrote it in the 1970s, and some of his claims about universal psychology feel dated. The cultural essentialism — suggesting that all Japanese people experience amae in the same way — has been challenged by subsequent research showing significant variation within Japan. The book also makes little room for gender analysis: amae in Japanese society is heavily gendered, and Doi treats it as though it applies equally to men and women. It doesn't. The male salaryman's amae toward his wife (expecting care without asking) is a very different experience from the wife's amae (who does she depend on when she needs care?). The psychoanalytic framework Doi uses — heavy on Freud, light on data — will frustrate readers who prefer evidence-based psychology. And the book's central insight — that the need for dependence is universal — is persuasive but not operational. Doi diagnoses the problem but offers little practical guidance for what to do with the diagnosis. The translation from Japanese clinical experience to universal human psychology is also sometimes stretched too thin.

If you are interested in Japanese psychology, cross-cultural psychiatry, or the question of why independence is treated as a universal virtue in Western culture, this book will open a door you didn't know existed. If you want practical relationship advice or a comprehensive guide to Japanese culture, it will feel too focused on a single concept and too theoretical for everyday use.

List the people in your life you feel safe depending on — the ones who would anticipate your needs without being asked. If the list is very short, that's data about your relational environment. Don't judge it. Just notice it.

Ask someone for something you could easily do yourself — a small thing: "Can you pass me that?" "Can you hold this for a moment?" Notice the discomfort. That discomfort is your training in radical independence. The Japanese view is that accepting help is a gift to the other person — it allows them to feel useful.

Reverse the experiment. Anticipate a need of someone close to you — something they need but haven't asked for. Meet it without being asked. Notice the quality of connection this creates. That wordless understanding is what amae feels like from both sides.
