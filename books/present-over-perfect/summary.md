Shauna Niequist was a professional striver. She built a successful writing career, a happy marriage, a beautiful family, and a relationship with God that was the center of her life — and she was falling apart. **Present Over Perfect** is the story of what happens when you get everything you thought you wanted and discover that it's not enough, that the striving itself has hollowed you out. Niequist is a Christian writer in the evangelical tradition, but this book — her most successful — transcends the genre. It's not about theology. It's about the exhaustion of living for approval and the slow, frightening work of learning to live for something else.

The book's central metaphor is the split between "old Shauna" and "new Shauna" — the woman who ran on ambition and the woman who is learning to rest. Old Shauna was a performer, a pleaser, a woman who rooted her identity in what she could produce. She wrote books, planned parties, said yes to every opportunity, and measured her worth by the volume of her output. New Shauna is slower, quieter, more present — a woman who is learning that her value is not the same as her productivity.

The split is not presented as a clean break. Niequist is clear that she still struggles, still falls back into old patterns, still has to choose presence over productivity one day at a time. This honesty is the book's greatest strength. She is not writing from a place of arrival; she is writing from the middle of the struggle.

*The Trap:* The "new self / old self" framework is Christian in origin (Paul's letters), but Niequist uses it in a way that flattens complexity. Real change is rarely as neat as a before-and-after story, and the book's structure sometimes suggests a transformation that Niequist's own life contradicts — she is still ambitious, still productive, still writing books and speaking at conferences. The "new Shauna" looks a lot like the old one, just with more margin and better boundaries.

Niequist traces her drivenness back to a single wound: the need for approval. She writes about growing up in a high-achieving family, the daughter of a well-known pastor and author, always performing, always earning love through accomplishment. She carries this pattern into adulthood: the obsessive checklists, the inability to say no, the panic when someone is disappointed in her, the frantic belief that if she just does enough, she will finally feel safe.

The chapter where she describes a breakdown on her bathroom floor is the emotional peak of the book. She is on the floor, unable to move, crying, her body finally refusing to cooperate with the schedule she has imposed on it. She writes: "I had been running for so long that I forgot what it felt like to stand still. My body made the decision for me. It just stopped."

She describes the slow realization that the approval she was chasing could never fill the hole she was trying to fill. The approval of readers, editors, friends, family — none of it would ever be enough because the problem was not a lack of approval. The problem was that she had handed the keys to her self-worth to other people.

*The Trap:* The approval narrative is powerful and true for many readers. But it can also become its own kind of performance — the confession of inadequacy that is really a bid for more approval. "Look at how honest I am about my brokenness" can be just another way of saying "look at me." Niequist is not obviously guilty of this, but the line is thin.

The book's most useful section is the middle, where Niequist describes the specific practices that helped her shift from productivity to presence:

**Sabbath.** She writes about reclaiming Sunday as a day of rest — not a day of church obligations and family events, but a day of genuine stopping. No work. No email. No production. Just being. She describes the discomfort of it at first, the feeling that she was wasting time, the slow learning that wasting time is sometimes the most productive thing you can do.

**Saying no.** Niequist argues that every yes to something is a no to something else, and most of us say yes to the wrong things because we're afraid of disappointing people. She offers a simple filter: does this fill me or drain me? If it drains you, say no. Even if it's a good opportunity. Even if it would help your career. Even if the person asking is important.

**Eating together.** Niequist is a food writer at heart — her earlier books are full of recipes and dinner party scenes — and she argues that shared meals are one of the most underrated spiritual practices. Not fancy dinners, not elaborate cooking projects, but simple meals shared with people you love. The table is where presence happens.

**The slow life.** She writes about trading the fast life for the slow life — not as a rejection of ambition but as a reorientation of priorities. The slow life is about choosing depth over breadth, about investing in a few relationships deeply rather than spreading yourself across many. It's about reading one book carefully instead of skimming ten. It's about writing one good paragraph instead of a rushed chapter.

*The Trap:* The "slow life" is a luxury. Niequist acknowledges this obliquely — she has a husband with a good job, a flexible career, the resources to choose margin — but the book would be stronger with a fuller reckoning with its privilege. Telling a single mother working two jobs to "slow down" is not just unhelpful; it's cruel.

The book is Christian, but lightly so. Niequist references God, scripture, and her church community, but she does not preach. Her faith is the architecture of her life, not the argument of her book. She writes about prayer as a practice of presence, about scripture as a source of wisdom rather than a rulebook, about her community at a progressive evangelical church in Michigan as the place where she learned to be known.

For readers who share her faith, these sections will be deeply resonant. For readers who don't, they may feel like a foreign language. Niequist does not translate her spirituality into secular terms, which is honest but also limiting. The book works best for people who already believe that slowing down has spiritual significance; for skeptics, the "God" chapters may be a barrier.

The idea I couldn't shake after finishing this book is about the difference between a life that looks good and a life that feels good. Niequist had the former — the career, the family, the public success — and was miserable. The shift she describes is not about doing less but about measuring differently. She stopped asking "what will this produce?" and started asking "what will this cost me?" and "what will this give me?" Those are better questions, I think. They force you to reckon with the trade-offs that hustle culture hides.

The book is repetitive — the same ideas about rest, margin, and slowness appear in different forms across the chapters. The writing, while warm and personal, can be precious in the way that Christian women's writing often is — too many sentences about candlelight and warm tea and God's whispers. The book also has a fundamental tension it never resolves: Niequist is clearly still ambitious, still writing books, still building a career. The "present over perfect" ethos is real but partial. She has not stopped striving; she has learned to strive differently. The book would be stronger if it acknowledged that this is not the same as true rest.
