Prakash Iyer has one of those jobs that sounds made up: he was the managing director of Kimberly-Clark in India, then a leadership coach, then an executive who helps athletes transition into business. But his book, *The Habit of Winning*, is not a corporate success manual. It's a collection of stories — from sports, business, history, and everyday life — arranged around a quiet but persistent argument: winning is not about talent, luck, or circumstances. It's a habit. A set of mental and behavioral patterns that can be learned, practiced, and internalized.

The book opens with a story about the Indian cricket team's 1983 World Cup victory — the underdog story that changed Indian sports forever. Iyer uses it to establish his central theme: the teams and individuals who win are not the ones with the most resources. They are the ones who have cultivated the *habit* of expecting to win and acting accordingly. "Winning," Iyer writes, "is not a matter of luck. It's a matter of habit." Kapil Dev's team didn't know they were supposed to lose. Nobody told them the script. And that ignorance of the expected outcome turned out to be their greatest asset.

> "Winners don't do different things. They do things differently."

The book is structured as a series of short chapters — each built around a story and a lesson. There's the story of the marathon runner who collapsed 200 meters from the finish line and refused medical help, insisting on crawling across the line — a lesson about finishing what you start. There's the story of the hotel waiter who became a CEO by treating every task, no matter how small, as an opportunity to excel. There's the story of Arjuna and Krishna from the Mahabharata — Iyer is Indian, and his cultural references draw heavily from Indian mythology, which gives the book a distinctive flavor compared to Western self-help.

The chapter on "the power of persistence" tells the story of a man who applied for 30 jobs and was rejected 30 times. On the 31st attempt, he got hired. That man went on to become one of the most successful business leaders in India. Iyer's point is not that persistence always pays off — it's that the people who succeed are simply the ones who were still standing when the opportunity finally arrived. Everyone else had quit.

> "The difference between success and failure is often just one more attempt."

One of the book's most memorable stories is about a young boy who wanted to play cricket but was told he wasn't good enough. He practiced alone for hours every day, using a wall as his bowling partner. Years later, he became a national player. The story is framed not as a motivational tale but as a lesson in what Iyer calls "self-belief without evidence" — the ability to continue investing in yourself when the external world gives you no reason to.

Another story that stays with you: the executive who started every morning by writing down three things he was grateful for. Not goals. Not tasks. Just gratitude. Iyer presents this not as a spiritual practice but as a strategic one — gratitude shifts your brain from scarcity mode (what's missing) to abundance mode (what's present). And abundance mode is where creativity, confidence, and good decisions live. The executive credited this simple practice with getting him through the most difficult period of his career.

The book explores the concept of "mental toughness" through the lens of sports psychology. Iyer draws on interviews with athletes, coaches, and psychologists to build a case that the biggest battles are internal. The opponent who beats most people is not on the other side of the net. It's in their own head — the voice that says "you're not good enough," "this is too hard," "maybe next time."

The most memorable chapter is about what Iyer calls "the power of the invisible." He tells the story of a young cricketer who was told he'd never make it because he was too short. He didn't fight the criticism. He didn't argue. He just worked — on his technique, his timing, his mental game — until his height became irrelevant. The invisible factors — discipline, patience, the refusal to quit — mattered more than the visible limitation. This is a thread running through the entire book: the things that determine your success are usually the things no one can see.

Iyer introduces the idea of "the 10,000-hour rule" (popularized by Malcolm Gladwell) but adds his own twist: those 10,000 hours must be *deliberate* practice, not just repetition. Practicing the same thing the same way for 10,000 hours will make you good at doing that one thing. But deliberate practice — pushing against your limits, analyzing your failures, making micro-adjustments — is what produces mastery.

> "Practice does not make perfect. Only perfect practice makes perfect."

The Trap is the belief that winning is a destination. Iyer's most counterintuitive argument is that winning is a *process* — and that the people who are good at winning are also good at losing. They don't enjoy losing, but they extract value from it. They treat every loss as a data point, not a verdict. The person who can't lose gracefully will never be a consistent winner, because loss is inevitable. What matters is what you bring back from it.

This is where Iyer draws on one of his most powerful stories — the tale of a business leader whose company went bankrupt. He lost everything. Friends abandoned him. The press wrote his obituary. A few years later, he built a second company — larger than the first. When asked how he did it, he said: "The bankruptcy taught me everything I needed to know about business. The first company was just my tuition." That's the habit of winning: not avoiding loss, but extracting everything valuable from it and moving on.

The book also explores what Iyer calls "the leader's paradox": the people who most deserve to lead are often the ones who least want to. The ones who most want to lead are often the ones who shouldn't. He tells the story of a reluctant CEO who took the job only because no one else would — and turned out to be the best leader the company ever had. His secret: he didn't want the power. He wanted to serve. And that orientation — service over ambition — is what made him effective. This is Iyer at his best: taking a familiar observation and giving it a story that makes it felt rather than just understood.

The book's critical weakness: it's explicitly designed for a mass-market Indian audience, and some of its cultural references won't land for non-Indian readers. The stories from cricket and Indian business may feel distant if you don't follow those worlds. Iyer's writing style is simple and aphoristic — almost like a series of motivational posters stitched together. There's very little research or evidence; the book relies entirely on anecdote and inspiration. If you need data, rigorous argument, or original frameworks, this is not the book. Its strength is in its warmth and accessibility, not its intellectual depth.

The book also leans heavily on the "positive thinking" tradition that has been criticized for ignoring structural barriers. Not everyone who practices the "habit of winning" will win — circumstances, privilege, and systemic factors play a massive role. Iyer acknowledges this in passing but doesn't engage with it seriously. The implicit message — "if you just try hard enough, you'll succeed" — is comforting but incomplete.

The idea I couldn't shake: that winning is not about being the best. It's about being your best *consistently*. The people who win over the long term are not the ones who have occasional moments of brilliance. They are the ones who show up every day, do the work, and refuse to let the bad days derail them. That's the habit. Not the victory — the *motion* toward it.

There's a chapter near the end called "The Habit of Giving" that captures Iyer's philosophy best. He tells the story of a successful businessman who, every year, quietly funded the education of ten underprivileged children. No publicity. No ceremony. When asked why, he said: "Someone did it for me when I was young. I'm just returning the favor." Iyer's point: winning is hollow if it doesn't expand your capacity to give. The habit of winning, in its fullest expression, is not about accumulation — it's about circulation. Success that flows through you to others is success that lasts.

Who should read this: anyone who needs a motivational reset, young professionals starting their careers, sports fans who want a leadership angle, readers looking for an accessible Indian perspective on success. Who should not: readers who want evidence-based or academically rigorous material; anyone who is skeptical of the "positive thinking" genre.
