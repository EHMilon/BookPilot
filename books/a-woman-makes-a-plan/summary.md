There's a kind of woman who has been underestimated her entire life. Not with cruelty necessarily—but with a soft, condescending certainty that she doesn't quite belong in the rooms she walks into. She's told she's too tall, too ambitious, too outspoken. She's told to wait her turn, to be patient, to let others lead. And she spends decades proving everyone wrong, not because she's driven by revenge, but because she simply refuses to accept the story other people wrote for her.

Maye Musk is that woman. And **A Woman Makes a Plan** is the manual she wrote for women who need to learn how to be her.

> "The most important tool you have is your attitude. How you respond to what happens makes all the difference."

At 74, Maye Musk is a supermodel, a registered dietitian, and the mother of Elon Musk—though the book is careful to establish that she was successful long before her son's fame. Her life story reads like an epic of quiet rebellion: she escaped an abusive marriage with nothing but her three children and her determination, put herself through multiple graduate degrees, built a career in two different countries, and became a global icon of aging gracefully. This book isn't her full memoir—it's a collection of lessons organized around the theme of making and remaking a plan for your life.

**The structure** is thematic rather than chronological. Each chapter addresses a specific arena: survival, relationships, career, health, aging, money. The organizing principle is that life doesn't follow a straight line. You make a plan. Life disrupts it. You make another plan. The title is a verb, not a noun. Planning is continuous.

**Survival** is the first lesson, and it's the book's most harrowing section. Maye describes her marriage to Errol Musk—a man she's careful not to villainize but depicts clearly as psychologically and physically abusive. She stayed for nine years because divorce was stigmatized in 1970s South Africa, because she had no money of her own, because she was afraid. The escape came when the law changed and she could finally leave. The lesson she draws is not "leave immediately" but "plan your escape." She spent years preparing—building her qualifications, saving what she could, waiting for the moment. The plan took nine years to execute. But it worked.

> "The more you know, the more you can do."

**The core philosophy** is built around what Maye calls **the plan**. Not a single plan for your whole life—that's a fantasy. A plan for the next season. A plan for getting through a crisis. A plan for a career pivot. A plan for health. The content of the plan matters less than the practice of planning itself. Planning gives you agency. It shifts you from reaction to action. When you have a plan, you have a direction. Even a wrong plan is better than no plan.

**Career** advice fills most of the book, and it's refreshingly concrete. Maye didn't build a career in modeling by being the prettiest—she built it by being reliable. She showed up early. She was easy to work with. She said yes to jobs that paid less than she wanted because they built relationships. She networked continuously, not as a strategy but as a habit of being pleasant and useful. Her dietitian practice grew the same way—word of mouth, professionalism, showing up consistently for fifteen years until she became the person everyone referred to.

**The Trap** in Maye's approach is that it can seem deceptively simple. "Just make a plan" and "just be reliable" don't sound like profound insights. But the book's power is in the accumulation—she made a plan, then another, then another, over six decades. Each plan was imperfect. Each one got her through.

> "You don't have to be perfect. You just have to keep going."

**The Story** that captures her philosophy best is about her move to Canada. In her forties, she decided to leave South Africa to give her children better opportunities. She had no money, no job lined up, no home. She packed what she could carry, landed in Toronto with her three kids, and started over. She rented a cheap apartment, found work where she could, and rebuilt her professional life from scratch. The plan for that move took years of preparation. The execution required her to sleep on a friend's couch for months. But she did it. Because the plan mattered more than the comfort of staying.

**Why this is hard:** Because most of us want guarantees. We want to know the plan will work before we commit to it. Maye's lesson is that you rarely get guarantees. You get probabilities, effort, and the willingness to adjust. The book makes a powerful case for starting even when you're not sure, for taking the imperfect step rather than waiting for the perfect opportunity. Her career was built on saying yes to things she wasn't fully qualified for, then becoming qualified through the doing.

**The emotional arc** is unusual for a self-help book. There's very little wallowing. Maye doesn't dwell on the trauma of her marriage or the difficulty of single motherhood. She mentions the facts and moves on. This isn't emotional suppression—it's a pragmatic choice. The book's energy is forward-moving. The past happened. The question is: what are you going to do now?

**Honest criticism:** The book is uneven in depth. Some chapters are rich with specific advice—her sections on money management and career building are genuinely useful and hard-won. Others feel thinner—the chapters on relationships and aging offer broad observations rather than deep insight, as if she held back from the vulnerability those topics require. Maye's personality is charming but can also be frustrating; her relentless positivity sometimes reads as denial or avoidance of pain she hasn't fully processed. The "just make a plan" advice works wonderfully for someone with her specific resilience and privilege (being white, educated, and naturally beautiful opened doors that wouldn't open for everyone). And the book's relationship with Elon Musk is noticeably handled—she's clearly proud of him, but the book avoids any serious discussion of his controversies. That's her right as a mother, but it can feel evasive.

**What sticks:** The image of a woman in her seventies still making plans. Not planning to retire, not planning to slow down. Planning to *grow*. That alone is a radical counterpoint to the narrative that life peaks in your twenties and declines from there. Maye's example suggests something different: that each decade can be better than the last, if you keep making plans.
