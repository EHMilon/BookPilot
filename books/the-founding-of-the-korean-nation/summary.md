There is a story the Korean people tell themselves about their origins. In 2333 BCE, a legendary figure named Dangun — the son of a celestial god and a bear-woman — founded the first Korean kingdom, Gojoseon, on the banks of the Taedong River. The story is myth, not history, but it captures something essential about the Korean relationship with their nation: Korea is not a modern invention. It is ancient. It has been invaded, occupied, divided, and nearly erased from maps more times than almost any other nation. And yet it persists. **The Founding of the Korean Nation** is not about Dangun. It is about the actual historical processes — political, military, cultural, and philosophical — that created the Korean nation as it exists today.

The book traces Korea's emergence from the Three Kingdoms period (57 BCE – 668 CE) through the Unified Silla and Goryeo dynasties to the Joseon Dynasty (1392–1910), which gave Korea its Confucian character, its written language (Hangeul), and its cultural identity. It treats the "founding" not as a single event but as a recurring process — a nation that has had to reinvent itself multiple times.

The book opens with the Three Kingdoms period, when Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla competed for control of the Korean Peninsula. The authors argue that this period established the DNA of Korean statecraft: intense competition, rapid military innovation, and a willingness to borrow foreign technology (especially Chinese writing and Buddhism) while maintaining cultural distinctiveness.

The story of the Hwarang — the elite warrior-scholars of Silla — is given special attention. These young aristocrats were trained in both martial arts and classical Chinese poetry, embodying a fusion of warrior discipline and aesthetic refinement that would shape the Korean ideal of the cultivated leader. The Hwarang code of loyalty, honor, and sacrifice became a touchstone for later Korean military and political culture. *"The Hwarang were not just soldiers,"* the authors write. *"They were the architects of a Korean identity that could stand alongside China without being consumed by it."*

> "A nation that does not know its founding struggles is a nation without a spine. Korea's spine was forged in the wars of the Three Kingdoms."

Silla's unification of the peninsula in 668 CE (with Tang Chinese help, which it then expelled) created the first unified Korean state. The book devotes significant attention to this period because it established patterns that would persist for centuries: centralized bureaucracy, state-sponsored Buddhism as a unifying ideology, and a tributary relationship with China that balanced autonomy with pragmatic submission.

Buddhism during Unified Silla was not just a religion. It was a state-building technology. The great temples — Bulguksa, Seokguram — were built as political statements as much as religious ones. They projected power, sophistication, and cosmic legitimacy. The authors note that Korea's adoption of Buddhism was not passive copying. Korean monks traveled to China and India, translated texts, and developed distinct Korean Buddhist schools. *"Korea did not receive Buddhism,"* the book argues. *"It took Buddhism and made it Korean."*

The Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392) gave Korea the name it still uses. Goryeo was the period of the aristocratic literati, the Tripitaka Koreana (the most complete collection of Buddhist scriptures carved into 80,000 woodblocks), and the development of Korea's distinctive celadon pottery. It was also a period of constant threat — from the Khitans, the Jurchens, and the Mongols, who eventually conquered Goryeo after decades of resistance.

The book tells the story of the Mongol invasions as a defining trauma. Goryeo held out for nearly thirty years — longer than any other Mongol campaign — and was only subdued through a combination of military pressure and diplomatic negotiation. The Mongol occupation (which lasted roughly a century) left deep scars but also introduced new technologies, administrative practices, and cultural influences that Korea adapted and transformed. The Goryeo experience taught the Korean ruling class a lesson that would persist: survival required both military strength and diplomatic flexibility.

> "Korea has always been a small fish in a big pond. The survival strategy of the small fish is not to become bigger but to become more resilient."

The Joseon Dynasty (1392–1910) is the longest and most influential of Korea's dynasties, and the book gives it the most space. Joseon was founded by General Yi Seong-gye, who overthrew the Goryeo dynasty and established a new order based on Neo-Confucianism — a radical shift from the Buddhist orientation of Goryeo. The authors describe this as a revolution, not a transition.

Neo-Confucianism restructured every aspect of Korean life: family structure (patrilineal, with elaborate ancestor veneration), education (the civil service examination system), governance (a bureaucracy of scholar-officials), and social hierarchy (the *yangban* aristocracy). The Joseon period saw the creation of Hangeul by King Sejong the Great — a writing system designed to make literacy accessible to commoners. The authors present Sejong's reign as a golden age, but they also document the system's rigidities: the exclusion of women from public life, the brutal suppression of heterodoxy, the corruption of the yangban class in the later dynasty.

One of the most dramatic sections covers the Imjin War (1592–1598), when Japan's Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded Korea with the largest invasion force East Asia had seen. The Joseon military initially collapsed, and the court fled north. But the war was saved by a combination of Admiral Yi Sun-sin's naval victories — his "turtle ships" and brilliant strategy — and the mobilization of civilian militias (*uibyeong*). China's Ming dynasty eventually intervened, and Japan was driven back.

The book argues that the Imjin War shaped Korean national consciousness more than any other event before the 20th century. The memory of Japanese brutality, the heroism of Yi Sun-sin, and the humiliation of the court's flight created a narrative of resilience and betrayal that still resonates. *"The Imjin War taught Korea that survival depends on the people, not the government. This lesson would be relearned many times."*

The final chapters cover Joseon's decline in the 19th century — the internal corruption, the factional struggles, the failure to modernize as the West and Japan encroached. The *Kaehwagi* (Enlightenment Period) saw attempts at reform — the Gabo Reforms, the Donghak Peasant Revolution, the rise of the Independent Club — but they were too little, too late. Japan annexed Korea in 1910, ending the Joseon Dynasty and beginning 35 years of colonial rule.

The book ends not with the fall but with the survival. The Joseon system collapsed, but the Korean people did not. The colonial period, the division, the war, and the authoritarian era were still to come. The founding of the Korean nation, the authors suggest, is not a historical period. It is an ongoing process — a people who have repeatedly faced extinction and repeatedly chosen to continue.

This book is a survey, not a deep academic work. Specialists will find it overly general and lacking in historiographical nuance. The coverage is uneven — Joseon receives the most attention while the earlier periods are somewhat compressed, and the 20th century is almost entirely absent, meaning the book ends where most readers will want more analysis. The authors rely heavily on the "resilience narrative" — Korea's story is presented as one of continuous survival against external threats — which downplays internal conflicts, class struggles, and the genuine appeal of the Confucian system to many Koreans. There is also little engagement with revisionist scholarship that complicates the heroic narrative of figures like Admiral Yi Sun-sin or King Sejong.

Ideal for readers who want a concise, readable overview of pre-20th century Korean history. Useful for travelers, K-culture enthusiasts, and anyone who wants to understand the historical roots of modern Korea. Less suitable for academic study or for readers who already have deep knowledge of Korean history.

Learn the story of one Korean historical figure — Yi Sun-sin, King Sejong, or a leader from the Three Kingdoms. Read the full story, not just the Wikipedia summary. Understanding a nation's heroes is understanding its values.

If you have access, visit a Korean historical site or museum. If not, take a virtual tour of Gyeongbokgung Palace or Bulguksa Temple. Pay attention to the design — architecture is frozen philosophy.

Korea's founding story is one of repeated survival against overwhelming odds. Consider your own "founding story" — what challenges shaped you? Write a short account of one period when you had to reinvent yourself.
