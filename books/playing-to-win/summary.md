You can't win if you won't choose. That's the entire argument of Playing to Win, and it's more ruthless than it sounds.

A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin became strategy legends at Procter & Gamble, where Lafley served as CEO and Martin as an outside advisor. When Lafley took over in 2000, P&G was in trouble — stock price stagnant, iconic brands like Tide and Pampers losing share, culture dominated by risk-averse consensus-seeking. Conventional wisdom said a 165-year-old consumer goods giant couldn't innovate. Lafley and Martin proved conventional wisdom wrong, doubling P&G's revenue and quintupling its profits.

Their tool: a strategy framework called the **Strategy Logic Flow** — five questions so simple they seem almost trivial. The power isn't in any single question. It's in the commitment to answer them clearly, honestly, and together.

> "Strategy is a series of choices. If you aren't willing to make explicit choices about where to play and how to win, you don't have a strategy."

The five questions:

Each question forces a choice. Not answering is also a choice — the choice to drift.

This is bigger than "increase shareholder value." It's about *purpose*. Why does your company exist, and what does "winning" mean for you?

Lafley describes P&G's aspiration: "To improve the lives of the world's consumers, now and for generations to come." That sounds like corporate fluff, but for P&G, it was a genuine filter. Every product decision — from reformulating Tide to creating Swiffer — was evaluated against that aspiration. It guided where they invested and where they walked away.

Most companies skip this step entirely or default to financial targets. "Our aspiration is to grow revenue by 8%." That's a target, not an aspiration. An aspiration gives you direction when the targets are ambiguous. When two projects both meet the financial criteria, what makes you choose? That's what aspiration does.

This is the defining strategic choice — and the one most organizations refuse to make. You can't be everywhere. You have to pick your ground.

The "where to play" question covers five dimensions: which customers, which channels, which product categories, which geographies, and which vertical stages of production. Lafley and Martin tell the story of P&G's decision to enter the **feminine care market in developing countries**. The choice wasn't obvious. Women in rural India didn't use branded pads. The infrastructure wasn't there. The margins would be thin. But P&G decided to play there because it aligned with their aspiration — improving lives — and because they believed they could win through innovation (like lower-cost products designed for local needs).

The key: they knew exactly *where* they were playing. They didn't say "we'll sell feminine care everywhere." They chose specific markets, specific channels, specific price points.

> "Strategy is about what you're not going to do. The hardest part of strategy is deciding what to say no to."

Organizations try to serve everyone. "We don't want to exclude any customer segment." This is the path to mediocrity. When you try to be everything to everyone, you end up being nothing to no one. Real strategy requires excluding people. It's uncomfortable, and that's why most companies won't do it.

Once you know where you're playing, you need an answer to: "How do we win in this specific space?" This requires a **competitive advantage** — something you do better than anyone else that matters to your chosen customers.

Lafley uses the example of **Tide**. In the laundry detergent market, P&G's "where" was mass-market consumers across all income levels. Their "how" was superior cleaning performance — a relentless pursuit of stain removal that no competitor could match. Every innovation, from Tide with Bleach to Tide Pods, was driven by the question: "Does this make clothes cleaner?"

By contrast, **Mr. Clean** competed differently — not on stain removal but on simplicity and nostalgia. Different where (consumers who value a straightforward cleaning brand), different how (brand-driven simplicity rather than technical superiority). Both won. But the "how" had to match the "where."

Companies try to win by being "better" in general. Better doesn't mean anything without a defined "where." Are you better on price? On quality? On service? On speed? You have to choose a dimension of competition and stake your claim. Trying to win on all dimensions is a recipe for winning on none.

Strategy is only as good as the capabilities that support it. Lafley and Martin argue that most strategies fail not because they're wrong but because the organization doesn't have — or won't build — the capabilities to execute them.

For P&G, this meant investing heavily in **consumer research**. They created a program called "Living It" where P&G employees would go into consumers' homes and watch them use products. This wasn't a marketing gimmick — it was a capability that fed every other strategic choice. When you know exactly how people use your products, you know where to innovate.

Organizations set ambitious strategies but refuse to invest in the capabilities needed to deliver them. "We want to be a digital-first company" — great. Do you have the data scientists? The agile development teams? The digital marketing expertise? If not, your "strategy" is a fantasy. Capabilities are what make strategy real.

This is the most overlooked question. You can have the right aspiration, the right where, the right how, the right capabilities — and still fail because your management systems (rewards, metrics, culture, structures) are misaligned with your strategy.

Lafley tells the story of how P&G had to overhaul its innovation process. Previously, P&G had a "Not Invented Here" culture — everything had to be developed internally. Lafley shifted to **"Connect and Develop"** — a management system that actively sought external innovations and partnered with outside inventors. This required changing how R&D was evaluated, how partnerships were structured, how success was measured.

> "If the organization is not designed to deliver the strategy, the strategy will not be delivered."

Organizations bolt a new strategy onto an old operating system. The strategy says "innovate" but the bonus system rewards cost-cutting. The strategy says "customer-centric" but the org structure is product silos. The systems have to change — and that's the hardest, slowest part of strategy implementation.

> "The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do."

Lafley and Martin provide a detailed case study of the **Pantene brand turnaround**. In the early 2000s, Pantene was losing share to premium competitors. P&G's old approach: compete on price and promotions. The new strategy: choose to play in the health-and-beauty space (not the commodity hair care space) and win through a "beauty through science" positioning backed by superior product performance. This required different capabilities (hair science research, premium packaging, salon-quality marketing) and different management systems (training stylists, partnering with salons, changing the retail shelf strategy). Pantene went from declining to becoming a $3 billion brand.

**The idea I couldn't shake** was the concept of **strategy as a cascade of choices**. Most of my prior experience with strategy was a single decision — "this is our plan for the next year" — followed by execution. But Lafley and Martin show that strategy is actually a chain of choices, each one constraining and enabling the next. If you get the aspiration wrong, nothing downstream works. If you pick the wrong "where," your "how" doesn't matter. If you don't invest in capabilities, your beautiful strategy is a fiction. The cascade makes strategy fragile — one broken link and the whole thing fails. It also makes it powerful: when all five questions have consistent answers, you have something hard for competitors to replicate.

The book is essentially a P&G case study stretched to book length. Lafley and Martin have deep experience at one company, and while P&G is impressive, the book doesn't have enough examples from other industries. Can a five-person startup use this framework? What about a nonprofit? The heavy P&G focus makes the framework feel less universal than the authors claim.

The five-question framework is elegant but can become formulaic. Some strategic situations are ambiguous enough that they resist clean categorization into the five questions. The framework is strongest for established companies making deliberate strategic pivots and weakest for organizations facing existential uncertainty where the questions themselves might be wrong.

There's also an *assumption of agency* that doesn't fit every leader. The book assumes you have the power to make these choices. Many leaders are operating within constraints they can't control — parent company mandates, regulatory requirements, resource limitations imposed from above. The framework doesn't address how to strategize when you're not in full control.
