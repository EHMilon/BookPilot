You're lying in bed at 3 AM, staring at the ceiling, replaying a moment you can't undo. The words you said. The job you didn't take. The person you should have called. The version of your life that exists only in your head — the one where you made the other choice. **If Only** is a book-length excavation of that feeling. Not to wallow in it, but to understand what it's actually made of, and whether it can be put to use.

The book is an anthology from The School of Life, the institution founded by Alain de Botton to provide "an emotional education" through accessible philosophy and psychology. It collects essays, aphorisms, and reflections on regret — that most under-discussed of human emotions. We have a thousand books on happiness and gratitude. Almost none on the haunting sense that we've taken the wrong path. If Only fills that gap by arguing that regret isn't just normal — it's necessary.

Here's the book's central inversion: regret is not a failure of emotional management. It's a sign that your conscience is working. The people who feel no regret are not enlightened. They're either deeply defended or pathologically detached.

The book draws on the work of psychologist Daniel Kahneman and the "hedonic treadmill" — our frustrating habit of adapting to any circumstance, good or bad. But it takes the research in a different direction. If we adapt to everything, regret is the exception: a refusal to adapt. A stubborn insistence that something mattered, that a choice was significant, that life could have been otherwise.

> "Regret is the price of caring. If you didn't care, you wouldn't regret. The question is not how to eliminate regret, but what your regrets tell you about what you actually value."

**The Trap** of the self-help industry's obsession with "no regrets." The book calls this toxic positivity dressed as wisdom. "No regrets" is not a philosophy of strength — it's a strategy of avoidance. The person who claims to have no regrets is either lying to you or has numbed themselves so thoroughly that they can no longer tell the difference between acceptance and resignation. Real emotional maturity doesn't eliminate regret. It learns to read it.

The book's most powerful section deals with what it calls **shadow lives** — the versions of your life that exist only in the counterfactual. The city you didn't move to. The career you didn't pursue. The relationship you walked away from.

It tells the story of a woman in her forties who chose stability over passion — a steady job, a predictable marriage, a sensible life. She didn't make the "wrong" choice. She made a defensible choice. And yet, twenty years later, she finds herself haunted by the road not taken, unable to shake the feeling that she chose security at the cost of aliveness.

The book's insight: this kind of regret is not a mistake to be corrected. It's an education. The shadow lives we grieve reveal what we actually wanted but were too afraid to let ourselves want. The grieving is the first step to knowing.

> "The life you didn't live is not a failure. It's a map of your unacknowlonged desires."

**The Trap** of believing you can "fix" regret by going back and making the other choice. The book warns against romanticizing the alternative. The path not taken is not a paradise — it's a fantasy, and fantasies are flawless because they've never been tested against reality. The goal is not to switch tracks. It's to understand what the fantasy tells you about what's missing *here*.

If Only categorizes regret into types, each with its own texture and lesson.

**Regret of action** — the things you did that you wish you hadn't. The cruel word, the betrayal, the impulsive decision. This kind of regret is sharp and specific. It has a clear source. The book argues that action-regret, while painful, is the easiest to learn from because the lesson is concrete: don't do that again. What's harder is the shame that keeps you from examining what happened.

**Regret of inaction** — the things you didn't do. The risk you didn't take. The apology you never made. The love you never expressed. The book argues this is the more dangerous kind because it has no closure. An action regret has an ending point — you did the thing, it went badly, you process it. An inaction regret is open-ended. You can spend a lifetime wondering "what if."

> "We regret our failures of omission more than our failures of commission. The things we didn't do haunt us longer than the things we did."

**The Trap** of treating action and inaction regrets the same way. They require different responses. Action regret needs processing and repair. Inaction regret needs a different kind of reckoning — not self-flagellation for what didn't happen, but a clearer view of what you want *now*.

The book draws on a philosophical tradition called **counterfactual thinking** — the human tendency to imagine alternatives to reality. It cites research showing that people who engage in counterfactual thinking are more likely to learn from their mistakes — but only when the counterfactuals are used constructively.

The distinction matters. There's a difference between replaying a mistake with shame ("I should have known better") and replaying it with curiosity ("What would I need to believe or do differently next time to get a different result?"). The first keeps you stuck. The second moves you forward. The book calls this the difference between **dwelling** and **working through**.

A philosopher quoted in the collection makes the case that regret is a form of intelligence — a way of knowing, through emotion, what logic alone can't tell you. You can reason through a decision endlessly. Regret bypasses the reasoning and tells you directly: this mattered. The feeling is the data.

> "Regret is not a failure of rationality. It is a deeper rationality — one that uses feeling as evidence."

The book has a quiet assumption that its reader has the luxury of choice — that their regrets are about relationships, careers, and self-expression rather than survival. This is a real limitation. The framework works beautifully for the middle-class indecisive; it's less useful for someone whose regrets stem from constraints they couldn't control.

There's also a tension the book doesn't fully resolve: how do you know when you're productively processing regret versus spiraling into rumination? The line between "learning from regret" and "being consumed by it" is drawn conceptually but not practically. A reader prone to overthinking could use this book to justify more overthinking.

And the anthology format means uneven quality. Some essays are revelatory; others feel like filler. The book could be 30% shorter and lose nothing.

The idea I couldn't shake: regret is not the opposite of a good life. It's a tax you pay for caring. The people who feel deep regret are the people who tried, who invested, who had skin in the game. The alternative is not a life without regret. The alternative is a life without investment — and that's not freedom, it's withdrawal.

Also: the distinction between action and inaction regrets is genuinely useful. I've started noticing which kind of regret I'm feeling when I feel it — and the response is different. Action regrets I can process and release. Inaction regrets I have to sit with longer, because they're not about the past. They're about what I'm still not doing.

For the overthinkers, the people who replay conversations in the shower, anyone who's ever said "if only" and meant it. Skip if you're in a fragile state — this book doesn't comfort, it excavates — or if you find the "no regrets" philosophy genuinely works for you.

Write down three regrets. For each one, label it: action or inaction? Then ask yourself: what does this regret tell me about what I actually value? Not "what should I have done" — "what matters enough to me that this regret exists at all?"

Pick one significant "what if" from your past. Spend 10 minutes writing the fantasy version — the life where you made the other choice. Then, in a different color or column, write what would realistically have been difficult about that path. The goal is not to dismiss the fantasy but to see it clearly — fantasy and friction together.

Of your regrets, which one points to something you still have time to address? Not to undo the past, but to act on what it revealed. Make one concrete plan — call someone, start something, stop something — based on what your regret has been trying to teach you.
