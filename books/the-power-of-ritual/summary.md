Three friends meet every Tuesday night over Zoom. They don't chat about their day. Instead, they light a candle, read a poem, sit in silence for three minutes, then each shares what they're grateful for and what they're struggling with. No one in the group would call themselves religious. But they're doing something unmistakably sacred. Casper ter Kuile, a Harvard Divinity School graduate and former Ministry of Innovation at the Church of England, spent years studying why people are leaving traditional religion — and what they're building instead. **The Power of Ritual** is his answer: we haven't stopped needing ritual. We've stopped having it provided for us. The book is a field guide to building your own.

Ter Kuile's background is unusual — a theologian who studied everything from evangelical megachurches to paganism to CrossFit — and it gives him a rare perspective. He's not arguing for a return to religion. He's arguing that the *forms* religion created (ritual, community, sacred time, intentional reflection) are human necessities, not optional extras. When people leave church, mosque, or synagogue, they don't stop needing these things. They just lose the infrastructure for them. The book is about how to rebuild that infrastructure in secular life.

The core claim: ritual is not a religious thing. It's a human thing. What we call "religion" is really just a bundle of practices that happened to be organized by institutions. The practices themselves — marking transitions, gathering in intentional ways, creating sacred space, telling origin stories — are older than any organized religion and survive outside them.

Ter Kuile draws on anthropological research showing that every human culture, without exception, has ritual. It's not a superstition we outgrow. It's a psychological and social technology for making meaning. When we lose it, we don't become more rational. We become more disconnected.

> "Rituals are the scaffolding of meaning. Without them, our lives are just a series of unmarked days, one after another, with nothing to distinguish the ordinary from the significant."

**The Trap** of thinking you need to believe in God to have meaningful rituals. The secular world has created a false binary: either you participate in organized religion, or you have nothing. Ter Kuile argues there's a vast middle ground — what he calls **"spiritual but not religious"** taken seriously, not as a shrug but as a deliberate practice.

The book organizes rituals around four fundamental human needs:

**Connection** — to others, to nature, to something larger than yourself. Ter Kuile tells the story of a "death café" — a group that meets monthly to talk about death over tea and cake. It sounds morbid. Participants describe it as liberating. The ritual structure (a time limit, a talking piece, a closing circle) allows people to discuss something they desperately need to discuss but have no social container for.

**Rest** — not just sleep, but intentional stopping. The Sabbath, in any tradition, is a technology for enforced rest. Without it, we work endlessly. The book describes a woman who instituted a "Friday night reset" — no screens, no work, just a bath, a book, and a glass of wine at the same time every week. She calls it her anchor. The ritual is the structure that protects her from her own tendency to never stop.

**Reflection** — making space to ask big questions. The book's most beautiful example: a man who, every New Year's Eve, writes himself a letter reviewing the past year — what he learned, what he regrets, what he hopes for. He seals it and reads it the following New Year, layering years of his own voice into a conversation with his past and future selves.

**Growth** — marking transitions. Ter Kuile argues that modern life has almost no rites of passage. Graduation, marriage, and funerals are the only remaining ones, and they're increasingly thin — a party and a speech, then back to normal. We have no ritual for becoming a parent, for starting a new job, for recovering from illness, for midlife. The book describes a "threshold ceremony" created by a group of friends — when someone in their circle made a major life change, they'd gather, write letters, share a meal, and physically walk the person across a chalk line drawn on the floor. Simple. Effective. Unforgettable.

> "We have rituals for birth, marriage, and death — but nothing for the thousand other thresholds we cross in a lifetime."

**The Trap** of believing rituals must be elaborate or traditional to work. The most powerful rituals are often the simplest — a consistent weekly walk, a shared meal with a specific intention, a journaling practice on Sunday nights. Elaboration is not depth. Repetition with intention is depth.

The book's most practical section is a step-by-step guide to ritual creation, distilled from studying how rituals work across cultures.

**Intention** — Why are you doing this? Not "because it sounds nice" but "because I need to mark this transition / create this connection / make space for this feeling."

**Structure** — Every ritual needs a beginning, a middle, and an end. Ter Kuile calls this the **ritual arc**: an opening (lighting a candle, ringing a bell, taking three breaths), a central practice (sharing, reading, sitting, writing), and a closing (a final word, a gesture, a literal closing of the circle).

**Repetition** — A one-off is an experience. A repeated experience is a ritual. The power comes from doing it consistently, so the structure becomes familiar and the meaning deepens over time.

**Community** — Not always necessary (some of the best rituals are solitary), but ritual is inherently social. Even a solo ritual connects you to something — nature, ancestors, your past and future selves. The book encourages finding at least one ritual you do with others.

**Adaptation** — A ritual that becomes rigid dies. The best rituals have room to evolve. Ter Kuile describes a "full moon circle" that began with strict rules (crystals, specific chants, exact timing) and became brittle as participants felt pressured to perform it "correctly." The group rediscovered its vitality when they loosened the form and let the practice breathe.

> "The ritual is not the form. The ritual is what the form carries. When the form becomes the focus, the meaning leaks out."

The book's broader argument is sociological: the decline of organized religion is not the end of the sacred. It's the beginning of a more dispersed, personalized relationship with it. Ter Kuile is optimistic about this — he sees the creativity and sincerity of people building their own practices as evidence that the human need for meaning is undiminished.

But he's also honest about the risks. Personalized rituals can become self-indulgent. Without institutional memory, practices can lose depth. A community of three friends is fragile — one person moves away, and the ritual dies. There's something the old religions had that DIY spirituality lacks: resilience, accountability, and a tradition of wisdom that outlasts any individual.

The book doesn't solve this tension. It names it and moves on, which feels like a gap. If DIY rituals are fragile, what's the alternative? Ter Kuile hints at "intentional communities" and "micro-churches" but doesn't develop the thought.

The idea I keep coming back to: I don't need to believe in anything supernatural to benefit from the forms religion developed. The candle, the silence, the shared intention, the marking of time — these are technologies, not dogmas. They work whether or not you believe in the theology that originally surrounded them. I started a Sunday evening reset ritual after reading this. Three months in, it's become the most reliable hour of my week.

The book's strength — its generous, non-judgmental tone — is also its weakness. Ter Kuile is so reluctant to criticize anything that the book can feel like it lacks discernment. Is a weekly CrossFit class really a ritual? Is brunch with friends? The category becomes so broad it loses meaning. The book needs more rigor about what distinguishes a ritual from a routine.

There's also a class problem the book mostly ignores. Ritual creation takes time, energy, and psychological bandwidth — luxuries that aren't equally distributed. The single mother working two jobs doesn't have room for a weekly "threshold ceremony." The book assumes a level of privilege that goes unacknowledged.

And the DIY focus, while empowering, can feel lonely. The book doesn't adequately address the fact that building your own ritual culture is hard work that falls on you alone. Sometimes you want someone else to have done the work — to hand you a tradition that's already meaning-full.

For anyone who misses something about religion without wanting to go back to it. For the people who've felt that modern life is thin but couldn't name why. For the organizers, the host-types, the people who instinctively gather others. Skip it if you're content with your current spiritual or secular arrangements.

Identify a transition coming up in the next month — a birthday, a project ending, a new beginning. Design one simple ritual to mark it: a meaningful location, a short reading or reflection, a closing gesture. Write down exactly what you'll do.

Choose one recurring slot in your week — Sunday evening, Friday morning, Wednesday lunch — and assign it a single ritual intention. Not "relax" (too vague) but "review my week and write down what I learned" or "make one intentional call to someone I care about." Commit to doing it for the next four weeks.

Create the smallest possible ritual — a minute of silence before your first coffee, a three-breath pause before entering your home, a gratitude sentence before bed. The goal is not the content. The goal is proving to yourself that you can insert intentionality into your day. The first one is the hardest. After that, you know how.
