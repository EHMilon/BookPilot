You have walked on paths your whole life and never once asked what you were walking on. That's the uncomfortable gift of *The Old Ways*: it makes you realize that every step you've ever taken was on ground freighted with meaning you never noticed. Robert Macfarlane's third book about landscape and the human relationship to it is, on its surface, an account of walking various ancient routes across Britain, Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. But that's like saying *Moby-Dick* is a book about whaling. The real subject is what happens to a person when they submit to the logic of a path — when they let a route older than memory pull them forward and shape their attention.

Macfarlane is a Cambridge academic with the soul of a poet and the stamina of a long-distance walker. His previous books (*Mountains of the Mind*, *The Wild Places*) established him as the leading voice in a new wave of British nature writing — lyrical, deeply researched, unafraid of the mystical. But *The Old Ways* is his masterpiece. It's looser, more digressive, more willing to wander than anything he'd written before. Which is exactly the point: the book is trying to embody what it describes.

The central insight is deceptively simple: paths are not just routes across terrain. They are records of human intention, sedimented over centuries. Every footpath, drove road, pilgrims' way, and ancient trackway is a story compressed into soil. Macfarlane walks the Icknield Way, one of Britain's oldest roads, and describes it as "a chalk ridge of such palaeolithic authority that it seems to have been shouldered from the earth rather than built." He walks the sea roads of the Western Isles, where the distinction between land and water dissolves. He follows the tracks of the Mesolithic dead, the holloways sunk so deep into the English landscape that walking them feels like descending into a geological wound. He traces the Ridgeway, the Broomway, the Pilgrims' Way — each one teaching him something different about attention, memory, and the body.

> "A path is a text with a thousand authors, constantly revised."

The book is structured as a series of journeys, each one orbiting a different kind of path and a different companion. Macfarlane walks with artists, poets, naturalists, and friends — but the most important companion is the ghost of Edward Thomas, the poet and essayist who walked the ancient ways of southern England in the years before World War I and died at Arras in 1917. Thomas haunts the book. His poems surface in moments of exhaustion or revelation. His voice — wry, melancholy, fiercely attentive — becomes a kind of tuning fork for Macfarlane's own sensibility.

One of the most powerful sections describes Macfarlane walking the Broomway, a tidal path off the coast of Essex that is only passable at low tide. The path is not on land. It's on the seabed. If you time it wrong, the tide comes in and you drown. Macfarlane walks it with a local guide who knows the route by feel — by the texture of the sand underfoot, the color of the water, the position of distant landmarks. The metaphor writes itself: there are paths that can only be known through the body, not through maps. And there are ways of knowing that can't be transmitted, only passed on by walking together.

**The Trap** that Macfarlane is writing against is the modern assumption that the only real knowledge is knowledge you can Google. He's arguing — gently, without polemic — that the landscape holds knowledge that can't be extracted into information. You have to *go* there. You have to *walk* it. You have to let your body learn what your mind can't grasp. This isn't nostalgia. It's phenomenology. Macfarlane cites the philosopher John Wylie, who writes about "the way in which self and landscape are folded together in the act of walking" — the idea that walking a path doesn't just change where you are, but who you are.

> "Walking is how the body measures itself against the earth."

The book also journeys further afield: to Palestine, where Macfarlane walks with a Palestinian guide along ancient routes that are being erased by the separation wall; to the Himalayas, where he walks a pilgrimage route to a lake at 16,000 feet; to Spain, where he traces the path of the poet Antonio Machado through the Soria countryside. Each journey deepens the same argument: that we are made by the routes we take, that the ground remembers us, and that walking is a way of thinking that the modern world has largely forgotten.

The section on the Icknield Way — one of Britain's oldest roads — is the book's philosophical core. Macfarlane describes walking a section where the path has been used continuously for over 4,000 years. "Imagine," he writes, "that every generation of people on this island has walked this line." The weight of that continuity is almost physical. You feel it in your legs. The path is not just a route. It's a palimpsest of human presence. And walking it is a form of communion.

The figure of Eric Ravilious, the English painter, appears throughout the book like a watermark — his watercolors of the South Downs, the chalk paths, the coastal routes, become a visual companion to Macfarlane's prose. Ravilious died in 1942 when his plane disappeared off Iceland, but Macfarlane keeps him alive through the book, walking the same landscapes, seeing them through a painter's eyes. It's a beautiful structural choice — not analysis, not biography, but a kind of ghostly companionship that deepens the book's central claim: that we walk with the dead as much as the living.

The weakness of *The Old Ways* is the same as its strength: it's meditative to the point of diffusion. Macfarlane's prose is so consistently beautiful that it can become exhausting — there's no relief from the lyricism, no flat patches to rest. Sentences like "The paths were the lines that held the world together in a time before maps, when the land was still a manuscript waiting to be read" are gorgeous, but after 400 pages of them, you might find yourself craving something ugly, or at least plain. The book also assumes a level of cultural and geographical literacy that not every reader will have. If you don't know the British landscape, some of the geographical descriptions will blur into abstraction.

And there's a political quietness that can frustrate. Macfarlane touches on land rights, enclosure, displacement, and the privatization of the commons, but he never quite dwells. He seems more comfortable describing a flint than analyzing a land dispute. For some readers, that's a feature — the book is an escape from politics into deep time. For others, it's a cop-out.

One of the most haunting sections is about the Palestinian guide who walks Macfarlane through a landscape being systematically erased. The guide points to a hill where a village used to be, now buried under a forest planted by the Israeli government specifically to hide the ruins. Macfarlane describes the moment with characteristic restraint — a sentence, a pause, a shift to something else — but the image stays. Paths are not neutral. They're political. They're erased by power as surely as they're created by use. The book touches this realization and then, frustratingly, moves on.

But none of this diminishes what the book does best: it makes you want to walk. Not exercise-walk, not commute-walk — *walk* as a mode of attention, a way of reading the world. After reading *The Old Ways*, I started noticing footpaths I'd walked past for years. I started asking where they led. I started understanding that the ground under my feet was not just dirt but memory. And once you start seeing that, you can't unsee it.
