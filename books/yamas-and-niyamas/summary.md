I read *The Yamas & Niyamas* during a period when my yoga practice felt hollow. I could do the poses. I could breathe. But something was missing — a sense that what I was doing on the mat had anything to do with how I lived the rest of my day. Deborah Adele's book was the antidote to that feeling, and it arrived like a text from a friend who had been through exactly the same thing.

The premise is simple: before Patanjali gets to the poses, he lists ten ethical guidelines — five restraints (yamas) and five observances (niyamas). These are the "don'ts" and "dos" of classical yoga, and most modern practitioners skip them because they sound like Sunday school rules. Don't hurt. Don't steal. Don't lie. Be clean. Be content. They sound like a moral checklist, and moral checklists are boring.

Adele's argument — and it's a good one — is that these aren't *rules*. They're not commandments handed down from a judgmental sky-daddy. They're *experiments* — invitations to try living a certain way and see what happens. Each yama and niyama gets its own chapter, and the structure is the same: what it is, what it looks like in daily life, what gets in the way, and what shifts when you actually live it.

Let's start with *Ahimsa* — non-violence. Adele makes the case that violence isn't just hitting people. It's the harsh inner critic, the impatient thought, the judgment you pass on yourself for eating the wrong thing, the way you clench your jaw in traffic. Violence, in her framework, is anything that separates, diminishes, or hardens. Non-violence is the practice of keeping yourself soft in a world that rewards hardness.

**The Trap with Ahimsa:** Adele is careful to say this isn't about being a doormat. Non-violence toward yourself means saying no when you need to. It means setting boundaries. It means letting people be disappointed in you. "Ahimsa," she writes, "is not about being nice. It is about being deeply connected to the truth of who you are, and acting from that place." That distinction — nice versus true — runs through the whole book.

*Satya* — truthfulness — comes next. Adele says *satya* isn't just about not lying. It's about living in alignment with reality. Most of us lie constantly in small ways: we say we're fine when we're not, we agree to things we don't want to do, we present a version of ourselves that's polished for consumption. These aren't big lies, but they accumulate into a life that isn't quite yours. The practice of *satya* is the slow, uncomfortable process of closing the gap between what you show and what's real.

She shares a beautiful framework: *satya* without *ahimsa* becomes cruelty; *ahimsa* without *satya* becomes avoidance. Truth without compassion is a weapon. Compassion without truth is a sedation. They need each other. This interplay between the yamas — the way they modify and balance each other — is one of the book's real strengths.

*Asteya* — non-stealing — is not just about physical theft. Adele extends it to time, attention, energy. When you're late, you're stealing someone's time. When you're distracted in a conversation, you're stealing attention. When you take credit for work you didn't do, you're stealing recognition. The practice becomes: am I taking more than I'm giving? Where am I subtly extracting rather than contributing?

*Brahmacharya* — often translated as celibacy — gets a major rework in Adele's hands. She interprets it as "right use of energy" — not abstention from sex, but a conscious relationship with your own vitality. Where does your energy go? Is it scattered across a dozen half-hearted pursuits, or is it focused and intentional? *Brahmacharya* is the practice of not leaking.

**The Trap with Brahmacharya:** Modern interpretations can swing too far the other way, treating any sexual energy as inherently creative or sacred, which is its own form of pressure. Adele avoids this by keeping the focus on *intention* rather than content. The question isn't "what are you doing with your sexual energy?" It's "what are you doing with *all* your energy, and is it serving you?"

*Aparigraha* — non-possessiveness — closes the yamas. Adele connects this to the hoarding of not just things but experiences, relationships, identities. The practice is letting go. Holding lightly. Not needing to own, control, or keep. She writes: "We are born with open hands, and we die with open hands. It is only in the middle that we clench."

What I love about her treatment of *aparigraha* is that she doesn't moralize about materialism. She's not telling you to sell everything and live in a hut. She's asking: *what are you holding that's holding you?* The unused project you won't abandon. The grudge you keep warm. The identity you've outgrown but can't drop. The practice isn't about renouncing the world; it's about noticing the weight in your hands and choosing, consciously, whether to keep carrying it.

There's a story she tells about a woman who couldn't let go of her dead husband's clothes, years after he passed. The closet was a shrine. Letting go felt like betraying his memory. The practice of *aparigraha* for this woman wasn't about throwing everything away in one dramatic purge; it was about starting with one shirt, noticing the feelings, breathing through them, and slowly, over months, creating space. That's the real work — not renunciation but the slow, tender practice of release.

One of the book's best insights is that these five yamas are not sequential. You don't master *ahimsa* and then move on to *satya*. They're simultaneous and interlocking. You practice all of them at once, badly, for a lifetime. The perfection isn't the point — the practice is.

The niyamas shift from restraint to active practice. *Saucha* (cleanliness) is both literal (clean your space, clean your body) and inner (clear communication, cleared mental clutter). *Santosha* (contentment) isn't passive acceptance; it's the active choice to find enoughness in the present moment while still working toward your goals. "Contentment," Adele writes, "is not the resignation to what is, but the ability to be happy with what is, even as you work for change."

*Tapas* (discipline) is the fire of practice — the willingness to do what's uncomfortable for the sake of growth. Adele is good here: she distinguishes *tapas* from self-punishment. The point isn't to suffer; the point is to generate heat that burns away what's not you. "The intensity of *tapas*," she says, "cooks the impurities out of us."

*Svadhyaya* (self-study) is the practice of turning the microscope inward. Adele recommends journaling, therapy, honest conversations with mirrors — anything that helps you see your own patterns clearly. *Svadhyaya* without *tapas* can become navel-gazing; *tapas* without *svadhyaya* can become burnout. They balance each other.

*Ishvara Pranidhana* (surrender) closes the book. This is the hardest one for modern, secular readers. Adele translates it as "letting go into something larger than yourself" — not necessarily God, but the intelligence of life, the flow of things, the mystery. It's the practice of relaxing your grip on outcomes, trusting that you're held by something even when you can't feel it.

There's a deeper point Adele makes about the niyamas that I want to underline. *Svadhyaya* — self-study — sounds like a soft practice. Journaling, reflection, introspection. But Adele insists it's one of the most demanding niyamas because it requires you to face what you'd rather not see. The patterns you've been running for decades. The stories you tell yourself about why you can't change. The ways you've been lying to yourself about who you are and what you want. "Self-study," she writes, "is not for the faint of heart. It is the practice of taking off the masks, one by one, until you find nothing but the truth underneath."

And *Ishvara Pranidhana* — surrender — is where she ends for a reason. After all the doing, the striving, the examining, the letting go, there's finally a point where you have to stop and let something larger carry you. She doesn't name what that something is, and I respect that. It could be God, the universe, your highest self, the collective wisdom of your ancestors, gravity. The practice is the same: relax your grip on the steering wheel and trust that the road knows where it's going.

**Honest Criticism:** The book is clearly written for a Western, mostly white, middle-class audience that has the luxury of examining their inner lives in this way. Adele doesn't always acknowledge her own positionality. The writing can be repetitive — each chapter follows the same four-step structure, and by the time you hit *Ishvara Pranidhana*, you might feel like you've read the same book ten times. Some interpretations feel stretched or sanitized for modern consumption — *brahmacharya* as "right use of energy" is useful but loses the radical edge of the original concept.

Also: the book tends toward the inspirational rather than the practical. Adele describes what these principles *feel* like but doesn't always give concrete strategies for living them when they're hard. "Let go of outcomes" is good advice; it's also nearly impossible to implement without a scaffold.
