In 1945, Jean-Paul Sartre walked into a crowded Paris auditorium and delivered a lecture that would become the most famous defense of existentialism ever written. The title was supposed to be defensive: "Existentialism Is a Humanism." The movement had been attacked from all sides — communists called it decadent individualism, Christians called it nihilistic atheism, the public called it a joke about café philosophers. Sartre needed to explain, clearly and forcefully, what existentialism actually was and why it was not a philosophy of despair but a philosophy of hope. The lecture that resulted is messy, brilliant, uneven, and historically essential. It contains the movement's most famous phrases, its clearest arguments, and — if you push hard — its deepest contradictions.

This is the slogan that defines existentialism and distinguishes it from everything that came before. For traditional philosophy — and for most religious thinking — a thing's *essence* (what it is) comes before its *existence* (that it is). A paper cutter, Sartre's example: before it exists, there is a concept of what a paper cutter should be, designed for a purpose. Its essence precedes its existence.

For human beings, Sartre says, it's the other way around. You are born. You exist. And then — through your choices, actions, commitments — you create your essence. You are not born with a fixed human nature, a predetermined purpose, a soul with a destiny. You are a blank, and you fill yourself in as you go.

> *"Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself."*

People hear "existence precedes essence" and think it means "you can be anything you want, and nothing is determined." Sartre would say: you can *choose* anything, but your choices carry consequences, and you are *responsible* for them. You're not free from the consequences of your freedom. You're responsible for *everything* you do, and there's no one else to blame.

If there's no God, no human nature, no cosmic plan, then we are radically free. But this freedom, Sartre insists, is not a gift. It's a burden. He uses the word **condemnation**: "Man is condemned to be free."

Why condemned? Because you didn't choose to be born. You didn't choose to be thrown into a world with specific circumstances, a body, a history, a language. But once you're here, you *must* choose. Not choosing is itself a choice — the choice to avoid responsibility, which is still a choice. There is no escape. You are "without excuse."

> *"We are left alone, without excuse. That is what I express when I say that man is condemned to be free."*

This is why existentialism is often experienced as anxiety. Sartre calls it **anguish** — the vertigo of total responsibility. If you truly accept that you are the sole author of your life, that no excuse (bad upbringing, poverty, genetics, fate) can explain away your failures, then each decision carries a weight that feels unbearable. Most people avoid this anguish by pretending they have no choice — by adopting a **bad faith** attitude.

**Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) is Sartre's term for the lie we tell ourselves to avoid freedom. It takes two forms.

The first: pretending you are like an object — that your past determines you, that your job defines you, that your "personality" leaves you no choice. The waiter Sartre describes in *Being and Nothingness* who moves a little too precisely, speaks a little too fast, plays the role of "waiter" as if it were his essence. He is in bad faith because he has decided that he *is* a waiter rather than a free being who *chooses* to wait tables.

The second: pretending you are pure freedom with no constraints — that you can ignore your situation, your body, your history. This is the opposite error. You are free, but your freedom is always exercised within a **situation**. You cannot choose to fly. You cannot choose to be born rich. But you can choose your *attitude toward* your situation.

The most common objection is that existentialism ignores material conditions. A starving person is not "free" to choose their meal. A slave is not "free" to choose their master. Sartre's response is that the slave is free to choose their attitude toward slavery — to resist, to accept, to rebel, to die. Critics (and later Sartre himself) found this response deeply unsatisfying. It's philosophically consistent but politically naive. Freedom *in* chains is still chains.

Sartre gives his most famous example. A young man comes to him during the war. His brother has been killed by the Germans. He lives alone with his mother, who has only him. Should he join the Free French Forces and fight for his country, or stay and care for his mother? No moral system can tell him what to do. Christian ethics says "love thy neighbor" — but which neighbor? Kantian ethics says "treat people as ends" — but both options treat someone as a means. There is no pre-existing answer. The young man must choose, and in choosing, he creates the value of his choice. The choice itself — the act of choosing — is what matters. He cannot look to any external authority for the answer.

Here's Sartre's boldest claim. When you choose, you are not just choosing for yourself. You are choosing for *everyone*. Every act implies a universal image of humanity. If you marry, you are saying: "Marriage is a valuable institution, and all humans should consider it." If you fight in a war, you are saying: "It is right for humans to fight when their freedom is threatened."

This is the basis of Sartre's claim that existentialism is a humanism: you cannot escape responsibility for the image of humanity you create through your actions. Every choice is a declaration of value. Every life is a proposal for how to live.

> *"In choosing myself, I choose humanity."*

The lecture is a mixed bag — brilliant in moments, sloppy in others. Sartre simplifies his own earlier work (especially *Being and Nothingness*, which is far more complex and cautious) to make a popular case. He mischaracterizes his critics, especially the Christians, and his dismissal of Freudian determinism is too quick — modern psychology has shown that unconscious drives limit freedom in ways Sartre didn't account for. The "choosing for all humanity" argument is rhetorically powerful but logically shaky: it seems to imply a kind of universalism that Sartre's radical freedom shouldn't permit. If I choose to be a thief, am I really saying all humans should be thieves? Sartre would say yes — and that's why you can't choose to be a thief in good faith. But the logic here requires premises that the lecture doesn't fully supply.

The most serious problem is the tension between radical freedom and situation. Sartre insists that we are always free to choose our response to any situation — but this becomes harder to maintain when you consider severe trauma, mental illness, addiction, or economic desperation. Later in his life, Sartre himself moved toward a more socially and economically determinist position. The early, heroic existentialism of this lecture is powerful but dangerously individualistic.

Anyone who has ever felt overwhelmed by choice and wished for someone else to decide. Anyone who blames their circumstances for their failures and wants to understand what it would mean to take full responsibility. And anyone who has heard "existentialism" used as a slur and wants to know what it actually says. Skip it if you want rigorous, systematic philosophy — this is a popular lecture, not a treatise, and it shows in the looseness of some arguments.

You are not born with a fixed nature. You create yourself through your choices. You are utterly responsible for what you become, with no excuses, no external authority, and no escape from the burden of that responsibility. Every choice you make creates a model for how humanity should live. Freedom is not a blessing — it is a condition you cannot escape. And the only authentic response is to embrace it fully, with all its anxiety, and act.

The concept of bad faith — the lie we tell ourselves to avoid freedom — has been the most practically useful idea for me. I catch myself doing it constantly. "I can't because of my job." "I have to because of my family." "That's just the way I am." Each of these is a potential act of bad faith: a refusal to acknowledge that I *choose* to stay in my job, *choose* to prioritize family, *choose* to identify with my habits. The question "am I in bad faith right now?" has become a kind of mental checkpoint. And the answer is sometimes "no, this is a genuine constraint." But it's often "yes, and you know it."

1. **Catch yourself in one act of bad faith today.** Listen for "I have no choice" or "I can't because" or "that's just who I am." When you hear it, ask: is this a genuine constraint, or am I pretending it is to avoid responsibility? Be honest.

2. **Make one choice today knowing it creates an image of humanity.** Pick something small. Not your career or your marriage — just a Sunday afternoon. If you read a book, you're saying: "this is what humans should do with their free time." If you scroll your phone, same thing. Choose consciously.

3. **Tonight, write down one thing you are blaming on external circumstances and ask: what would it mean to take full responsibility for it?** Not to accept blame — to accept *agency*. To say: "I am where I am because of choices I made, and I can make different choices tomorrow." That's the existentialist move.
