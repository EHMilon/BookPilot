The idea of rewilding has moved fast. Fifteen years ago it was a fringe concept debated in conservation biology journals. Today it's a fixture of environmental policy in Europe, a mainstream topic in nature writing, and the subject of countless documentaries, campaigns, and land-management debates. But what exactly *is* rewilding? Is it bringing back wolves? Letting forests grow back? Abandoning farmland? Reintroducing extinct species? The answer, as this collection of essays by leading ecologists and conservation biologists makes clear, is: all of the above, and none of it, and it depends.

*Rewilding*, edited by Nathalie Pettorelli, Sarah M. Durant, and Johan T. du Toit, is not the book you read for inspiration (that's Monbiot, or Isabella Tree's *Wilding*). It's the book you read when you want to understand the *science* of rewilding — the debates, the definitions, the controversies, and the evidence. It's an academic volume written by scientists for scientists, but it's also one of the most clarifying books on the subject for anyone willing to work through the language. Because the biggest problem with rewilding, as this book makes clear, is that no one can agree on what it means.

> "Rewilding is not a single method. It is a spectrum of approaches united by a common goal: restoring ecological processes that have been disrupted by human activity."

The book opens with a taxonomy of rewilding. **Pleistocene rewilding** — the most controversial form — proposes reintroducing species that went extinct during the last ice age, or their closest living ecological equivalents. This is the "bring back the mammoth" school, associated with the ecologist Sergey Zimov and his Pleistocene Park in Siberia. The argument: many of the ecosystems we think of as "natural" (like the Arctic tundra) are actually artifacts of megaherbivore extinction, and restoring large grazers could slow permafrost melt.

**Trophic rewilding** — the Yellowstone model — focuses on reintroducing top predators and keystone species to trigger cascading ecological effects. This is what most people mean when they say "rewilding." The evidence is strong but context-dependent: wolves work in Yellowstone, but the same cascade doesn't always happen in other ecosystems. The book's contributors are honest about the failures: some wolf reintroductions have had minimal effect; some beaver reintroductions have caused unexpected conflict with landowners.

**Passive rewilding** — the Knepp model — simply stops human management and lets nature recolonize. No reintroductions. No interventions. Just "stop and watch." This is the purest form of the philosophy, and it's also the most politically practical because it doesn't require the contentious step of releasing large animals. The science on passive rewilding is encouraging: abandoned farmland in Europe and North America shows rapid biodiversity recovery within a decade, especially when connected to existing natural areas.

**The Trap** for the rewilding movement is that it has become a word that means everything and nothing. The book's contributors are sharply aware that "rewilding" has been adopted by everyone from radical ecologists to corporate land managers who use it to greenwash development projects. "If everything is rewilding," one contributor notes, "nothing is." The book is an attempt to restore specificity — to say that rewilding is not just "letting things grow" but a specific ecological intervention with measurable outcomes.

> "The danger of a popular word is that it becomes a container for every hope and every grievance. Rewilding needs definition, not devotion."

One of the most valuable chapters is on **rewilding and climate change**. The relationship is not straightforward. Rewilding can sequester carbon — restoring forests and wetlands pulls CO2 from the atmosphere. But some rewilding scenarios (like reintroducing large grazers) can increase methane emissions. The net effect depends on the ecosystem, the species involved, and the time scale. The book argues for a nuanced approach: rewilding should be evaluated on its ecological merits, not assumed to be automatically beneficial for climate.

The **social dimensions** are covered in several chapters. Rewilding is not just an ecological project — it's a social one. It requires land, and land in most countries is owned, farmed, or inhabited. The book explores conflicts between rewilding and traditional farming, between conservation and rural communities. The most striking case study is the Scottish Highlands: rewilding advocates want to restore Caledonian forest, but local communities depend on sheep farming and deer stalking. The conflict is not resolvable by science alone — it's a fight about values, identity, and who has the right to determine what a landscape should be.

The book's **structure** reflects the breadth of the field. The first section covers the ecological science — what rewilding means in terms of ecosystem function, biodiversity, and landscape connectivity. The second section examines the social and political dimensions — how rewilding interacts with farming, land ownership, indigenous rights, and rural economies. The third section looks at practical case studies from around the world: Europe, North America, Africa, South America. The fourth section grapples with the ethical and philosophical questions that rewilding raises. This structure makes it a reference work as much as a narrative — you can read the whole thing, or you can dip into the chapters relevant to your interest.

> "Rewilding is not about recreating the past. It is about restoring the capacity for the future to be wild."

This distinction — between restoration and creation — is the book's most valuable conceptual contribution.

The book opens with a necessary **history of the term** "rewilding." The word was coined in the 1990s by the conservation biologist Michael Soulé, who used it to describe a specific approach: large, connected protected areas with top predators reintroduced. Soulé's "three C's" — cores, corridors, and carnivores — became the founding framework. But the term has since been adopted, adapted, and sometimes diluted. The book traces this evolution with academic rigor, showing how the concept has fragmented into competing schools of thought. For anyone who has used the word "rewilding" casually in conversation, this history is a humbling reminder that the term carries more baggage than most people realize.

One of the most useful contributions is a chapter on **measuring rewilding success**. What metric do you use? Species richness? Ecosystem function? The presence of top predators? Carbon sequestration? Public engagement? Each metric tells a different story, and the choice of metric reveals the underlying values of the person doing the measuring. The authors propose a framework based on "ecological integrity" — not just the presence of certain species, but the *processes* that sustain them: disturbance regimes, nutrient cycling, predation, decomposition. A rewilded landscape is not one that looks a certain way. It's one that functions a certain way.

The chapter on **rewilding ethics** is the one that stayed with me. The authors ask a genuinely difficult question: is rewilding about restoring the past, or creating the future? If we reintroduce wolves to Scotland, are we trying to restore a historical state (how Scotland was 5,000 years ago)? Or are we creating a new state that has never existed before (an ecosystem with human-subsidized wolf populations in a heavily modified landscape)? The answer matters for how we evaluate success. A historical baseline is a choice, not a fact.

> "We cannot step into the same river twice. We cannot restore the same ecosystem. We can only make a new one, informed by the memory of what was lost."

**Criticism:** This is an academic volume, and it reads like one. The language is dense, the sentences are long, and the prose lacks the fire of Monbiot's *Feral* or the beauty of Macdonald's *H is for Hawk*. For the general reader, it's a slog. The book also reveals a movement that's still in its infancy — many of the chapters end with "more research is needed," which is scientifically honest but rhetorically unsatisfying. And the volume doesn't resolve the fundamental tension at the heart of rewilding: is it a scientific project (restoring ecological function) or a cultural one (restoring a sense of wildness)? The contributors disagree with each other, and the book lets them disagree without synthesis.

**What stuck with me** is the concept of "rewilding as a spectrum." I had thought of rewilding as a binary — you're either for it or against it, either doing it or not. The book shows that it's a gradient: from full Pleistocene restoration (high intervention, high ambition) to minimal passive rewilding (low intervention, low risk). The question isn't "should we rewild?" but "what kind of rewilding, where, for whom, and at what scale?" Those are much harder questions. But they're the right ones.
