What does it *mean* to have courage? We usually think of it in discrete acts — the soldier charging a machine gun, the whistleblower facing ruin, the cancer patient choosing another round of chemo. Tillich wants none of that. He's after something more elemental: the courage to **be** — to affirm your existence in the face of everything that tells you it's meaningless, guilty, or about to end. Not courage *for* something, but the courage that *is* something. The courage that you don't summon for a moment but inhabit as a way of being. Published in 1952, this is one of those rare books that manages to be a work of serious theology, a piece of existential philosophy, and a lifeline for anyone who has ever lain awake wondering if any of it matters.

Tillich was a German theologian who fled the Nazis, landed in America, and spent his career trying to make Christian theology speak to people who had stopped believing — not just in God, but in the meaningfulness of existence itself. His project was to translate traditional theological language into terms that a secular, anxious, modern person could digest. *The Courage to Be* is arguably his most successful attempt.

Before you can talk about courage, Tillich argues, you need to understand what courage is *against*. He identifies three forms of anxiety, each corresponding to a different kind of threat to existence.

**Anxiety of fate and death.** This is the most basic — the fear that we will cease to be. Not just the intellectual awareness of mortality, but the visceral dread that undercuts every moment of security. You have a good job, but you might lose it. You're healthy, but the tumor could be growing right now. The contingency of existence — the fact that nothing guarantees our continued being — produces a low-grade hum of anxiety that we spend most of our lives trying not to hear.

**Anxiety of guilt and condemnation.** This is the moral dimension. The anxiety of knowing you *ought* to be something you're not. The gap between who you are and who you should be — and the judgment, whether from God or from your own conscience, that you've fallen short. This isn't just guilt about specific actions; it's the anxiety of *self-rejection*, the sense that your existence is somehow illegitimate.

**Anxiety of meaninglessness and emptiness.** For Tillich, this is the uniquely modern form. When ultimate concern — that which matters unconditionally — has been lost, existence becomes hollow. Goals can still be pursued, but nothing feels *worth* pursuing in any final sense. The anxiety of meaninglessness isn't the fear that you'll die; it's the fear that you were never really alive in the first place.

It's easy to treat these as sequential stages or separate problems. "I'll deal with death anxiety later, right now I need to find meaning." But Tillich insists they're always interwoven — you can't solve one without the others, and none of them can be eliminated. They're built into the structure of finite existence.

Tillich's core move is to redefine courage not as the absence of anxiety but as the affirmation of life *despite* it. Courage doesn't remove anxiety — if it did, it wouldn't be courage. Courage is "the self-affirmation of being in spite of the fact of nonbeing."

This is where the book gets genuinely radical. Most of us think of courage as something we do — an act that overcomes fear. Tillich says courage is something we *are*. It's the quality of the self that says "yes" to itself even when there's no guarantee — when death is real, when guilt is deserved, when meaning seems to have withdrawn.

He traces the history of courage through Western philosophy, from Plato's courage of wisdom to Aristotle's courage of the noble man to Thomas Aquinas's courage infused by grace to Nietzsche's courage of the Übermensch. Each era, he shows, elevated a different form of courage because each era was threatened by a different form of nonbeing. The ancients feared death most. The medievals feared moral failure. The moderns fear meaninglessness.

A tempting misreading is to hear Tillich saying "just say yes to life and everything will be fine." He's saying the opposite. Genuine self-affirmation includes the acceptance of one's finitude, one's failures, one's doubts. Courage doesn't *overcome* nonbeing; it incorporates nonbeing into itself.

This is the part of the book that still makes people uncomfortable. Tillich argues that traditional theism — the belief in a Supreme Being who exists *alongside* other beings — is itself a form of anxiety. It's an attempt to find a secure foundation, a cosmic daddy who will guarantee meaning and safety. But this God, Tillich says, must be transcended.

"The courage to be," he writes, "is rooted in the God who appears when God has disappeared in the anxiety of doubt."

What? Tillich is saying that the *experience* of absolute doubt — the collapse of every particular belief, every religious picture, every claim about God's nature — can itself be a form of faith. Not faith *in* something, but faith as the sheer act of being grasped by something unconditional. This is what he calls the **God above God** — not a being, not a person, not a cosmic monarch, but the ground of being itself. The "power of being in which everything that is participates."

When the existentialists proclaimed that God was dead, Tillich says, they were right about the theistic God — the being-upon-whom-we-depend. But they missed the deeper reality: Being Itself doesn't die. And the courage to participate in that reality — even without consolation, even without answers, even without meaning — is the form of faith for the modern age.

This can sound like intellectual gymnastics — "I don't believe in God, but I have faith in the Ground of Being." Tillich is not playing word games. He's describing a genuine psychological and spiritual possibility: that the collapse of every specific belief can reveal not nihilism but a deeper, more fundamental yes. The person who has doubted everything and found no reason to go on — and chooses to go on anyway — has experienced a form of courage that Tillich says is indistinguishable from the courage of the saint.

The most pastorally powerful section of the book deals with the anxiety of guilt. Tillich's move is stunning in its simplicity: the antidote to the anxiety of condemnation is not to try harder to be good. It's to accept that you are accepted *despite* being unacceptable.

This is the heart of the book for many readers. You don't have to earn your acceptability. You don't have to resolve every moral failure. The courage to be, in this dimension, is the courage to accept that you are accepted — by existence itself, by the ground of being, by whatever you want to call the ultimate reality. "Accept that you are accepted," Tillich writes, "in spite of the fact that you are unacceptable."

It sounds too easy, and Tillich knows it. He calls it a "paradox" — something that can only be received, not achieved. But he insists it's real, and that millions of people have actually lived from it.

The image that stayed with me is Tillich's description of the **boundary situation** — the moment when the ordinary structures of security collapse and you're faced with pure nonbeing. Tillich says you can't *prepare* for this moment in the usual sense. But you can become someone who *can* face it. The courage to be isn't a skill you acquire; it's a quality of selfhood you cultivate, or rather, a quality of selfhood you allow to be cultivated in you by life itself.

Tillich's prose can be infuriating. His sentences are often dense, abstract, and repetitive in a way that feels less like depth and more like academic ritual. Whole paragraphs go by where he's essentially saying the same thing in three different ways, as if he doesn't trust you to get it the first time. His use of "nonbeing" as a quasi-substance can also be confusing — is nonbeing a thing? Is it an active force? He assures us it's not, but then he talks as if it is.

The book is also relentlessly Western and Christian in its framework. Tillich engages with Buddhism for precisely one paragraph. Despite his attempts to speak to the secular modern person, his theology is always just beneath the surface, and readers who have no interest in "the God above God" may find the last third of the book irrelevant to their lives.
