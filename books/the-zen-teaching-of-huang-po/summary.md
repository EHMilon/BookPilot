John Blofeld

A Zen master who tells you that everything — every practice, every effort, every thought about enlightenment — is what's blocking you from enlightenment. **The Zen Teaching of Huang Po** is one of the most radical documents in the history of Buddhism. It strips the path away and says: there is no path. You are already what you seek. The only problem is that you're looking. Huang Po (Huangbo Xiyun) was a ninth-century Chinese Chan master, the teacher of Linji (Rinzai), and his teaching is a sustained, relentless, almost violent demolition of the spiritual seeker's most cherished assumptions. If you read this book and feel uncomfortable, confused, or a little attacked — it's working.

Blofeld, the same scholar who brought us *The Chinese Art of Tea*, translated this text with a translator's precision and a practitioner's reverence. The book contains two texts: the *Wanling Record* (dialogues between Huang Po and his student Pei Xiu, a government official) and the *Essential Dharma of the Transmission of Mind* (a more systematic exposition). They present the same teaching from different angles: the One Mind, which is the Buddha, which is what you already are, and which you cannot find because you've never lost it.

This first line of the *Essential Dharma* sets the tone. Huang Po does not invite you to a practice. He attacks the very concept of practice. "The very desire to be free from the cycle of birth and death is itself the thing that binds you." Why? Because the desiring mind is already the problem. The One Mind — which is uncreated, luminous, empty, and aware — doesn't need to be improved, purified, or freed. It's already free. The *feeling* of being bound is a thought, and the thought arises within the One Mind, which means it never actually bound you. You are not a sentient being struggling to become a Buddha. You are a Buddha dreaming that you're a sentient being.

This is where Huang Po becomes dangerous. A novice hears "you're already enlightened" and stops meditating, stops ethical practice, starts acting like a jerk and calling it non-attachment. This is the most famous misapplication of Zen: spiritual bypass, nihilism disguised as awakening. Huang Po doesn't say there's no practice. He says *practice while knowing that practice is futile*. You sit because sitting is what a Buddha does, not because sitting will make you a Buddha. The difference is invisible from the outside and everything from the inside. The trap is thinking "no effort" means "no discipline." It means effort without attachment to the result, not effortlessness.

The teaching uses the language of "one mind" (*yixin*) and "emptiness" (*kong*), borrowing heavily from the Indian Madhyamaka tradition but radicalizing it. In Huang Po's framework, even the concept of "emptiness" is a concept, and you must become empty of emptiness. "If a man, wishing to realize the One Mind, practices the Six Perfections and performs countless good deeds, that will not help him realize the One Mind. He must realize that the One Mind is prior to all such things." This is the "Zen razor" — the teaching so sharp it cuts itself. Every formulation Huang Po offers is designed to be discarded once it's served its purpose.

The *Wanling Record* documents Pei Xiu's increasingly desperate attempts to pin Huang Po down. Pei Xiu was a high-ranking official who genuinely wanted to understand. He asked questions a modern reader would ask: "If there's nothing to attain, why did the Buddhas appear in the world?" "If the One Mind is already perfect, why do we suffer?" "What is the difference between a sage and an ordinary person?" Huang Po's answers are consistent: you're asking the wrong question. The question itself arises from the delusion it's trying to escape. Stop asking. Stop seeking. Stop.

One of the most striking passages describes the relationship between the teacher and the student. Huang Po says: "The Master can do no more than point the way. If the student does not see for himself, it is useless." This is the democratic heart of Chan Buddhism. The teacher is not a guru who transmits a secret substance. The teacher is a *pointer*. The student must do the seeing. Huang Po can't give you enlightenment. He can show you what's blocking your view. That's all.

Huang Po's relentless dismantling of conceptual thought can easily slide into a wholesale rejection of thinking. But Chan masters were among the most carefully educated people in Chinese history — they studied the sutras, memorized the records, and spent decades in debate. The rejection of concepts comes *after* mastering them, not before. The trap is using Huang Po as an excuse to be proudly ignorant. When he says "conceptual thought is the root of delusion," he's speaking to people who had already spent years studying Buddhist philosophy. For a beginner, concepts are scaffolding. They become chains only when you mistake them for the building.

The text also deals directly with death. Huang Po's teaching of the One Mind means that birth and death are both appearances within the mind, not realities that happen to the mind. "The mind is like the void. It has no fixed location, no form, no color. It cannot be born and cannot die." This is not a belief system — it's meant to be *seen* directly in meditation. When you experience that the awareness you are is not located in your body, not bounded by your skin, not born with your birth — the fear of death loses its grip. Huang Po speaks of death with the same radical matter-of-factness as Zhuangzi: it's a change of form within the unchanging.

The idea I couldn't shake was the *quality of rest* in Huang Po's teaching. Most spiritual books make you feel inadequate — "you're not meditating enough, not mindful enough, not compassionate enough." They create a gap between you and the ideal you should be. Huang Po closes the gap by saying: there's no gap. You're not a flawed person seeking perfection. You're the One Mind pretending to be a flawed person seeking perfection. The moment you stop pretending, you're home. This sounds like a cheap trick until you actually *experience* it — the sudden recognition that your search for something better, something higher, something beyond is itself the thought that creates the feeling of separation. The book asks one question that you can't answer conceptually: *Who is seeking?*

This text is almost too advanced to be useful as a first exposure to Zen. Huang Po assumes you've already done the work — years of meditation, a solid grasp of Buddhist doctrine, some degree of self-awareness — and he's talking to you about what to do *after* that. If you read this cold, without context or practice, you'll either feel confused or — worse — you'll feel like you've "gotten it" without actually changing anything. Intellectual understanding of non-duality is not non-duality. The text also has no systematic structure; it circles the same points obsessively, which can feel repetitive.
