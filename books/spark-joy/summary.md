**Spark Joy** is the illustrated practical companion to Marie Kondo's first book — the nuts-and-bolts manual that *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up* deliberately left vague. If the first book was the philosophy, this one is the instruction booklet. And for anyone who tried the KonMari method and got stuck — who couldn't decide if something sparked joy, who didn't know how to fold a fitted sheet, who ended up with a "maybe" pile larger than the keep pile — this book is the fix.

Kondo opens with a confession of sorts: many readers of her first book wrote to her saying they did the tidying festival but their homes slipped back toward chaos. Her response is characteristically direct — you didn't follow the order. The KonMari method's power lies not in the individual techniques but in the sequence. Do clothes before books, books before papers, papers before komono, komono before sentimental items. Break the order, and the emotional buildup you need for the hard decisions never materializes.

> "Tidying is not about what you discard. It is about what you choose to keep."

The book dedicates extensive space to **the six rules of tidying**, now presented with diagrams and step-by-step instructions that the first book lacked. Commit fully. Imagine your ideal lifestyle. Finish discarding first. Tidy by category, not location. Follow the right order. Ask yourself if each item sparks joy.

The most practical section is the folding encyclopedia. Kondo illustrates dozens of folding techniques: how to fold a t-shirt into a compact rectangle that fits standing up, how to fold socks so they don't stretch at the elastic, how to handle the stubborn fitted sheet (it's possible — she swears — and the diagram makes it almost work). The level of detail is almost obsessive, but that's the point. Kondo believes that the *act of folding* is a meditation. The care you put into each fold communicates respect for the object and helps you stay connected to your decision to keep it.

**The Trap** is treating folding as a chore rather than a practice. If you rush through it, you'll revert to the old method within days. The vertical folding system works only if every piece in the drawer is folded correctly. One misfolded shirt collapses the whole visual system, and soon you're stacking again. The system is unforgiving because Kondo believes precision matters.

The book also addresses the most common objection Kondo heard from Western readers: "How do I know if something sparks joy?" Her answer is disarmingly simple: physical sensation. She tells a story of a client who insisted nothing sparked joy — until Kondo asked her to hold a ceramic cup her grandmother had given her. The client's face softened. Her shoulders dropped. She didn't realize she was smiling. That, Kondo says, is what joy feels like: not a thought, but a physical release. When you hold something that genuinely sparks joy, your body relaxes slightly. Your shoulders drop. You smile without meaning to. If you feel tension, guilt, or numbness, it doesn't spark joy. She advises readers to start with items they know they love — a favorite sweater, a cherished book — to calibrate the feeling, then work outward from there.

> "When you touch a piece of clothing, does your heart beat a little faster? Does your face relax into a smile? That is the feeling of joy."

> "The best way to find out what we really need is to get rid of what we don't."

**Spark Joy** introduces a concept that's only hinted at in the first book: **the joy of use.** Some objects spark joy simply by existing — a framed photo, a decorative vase. Others spark joy only when actively used — a well-made kitchen knife, a comfortable pair of shoes. Kondo advises distinguishing between these and ensuring that items in the second category actually get used. If they don't, their joy is theoretical, and the item should probably be let go.

The komono section — miscellaneous items — gets the most practical expansion. Kondo tackles the dreaded junk drawer, the bathroom cabinet, the kitchen utensil drawer, the electronics box full of cables from devices long dead. Her approach is ruthless: empty each space entirely, group by subcategory, and only return items that spark joy. Cables are a special case — she suggests keeping only cables for devices you currently own and discarding the rest. That old USB-mini cable from 2012? Its job is done. Let it go.

> "The purpose of storage is to determine the home of each item. A place for everything and everything in its place."

The sentimental items chapter addresses the hardest part of the process — and, in many ways, the whole point. Kondo tells the story of a client who couldn't let go of love letters from an ex-boyfriend, even years after the relationship ended. She wasn't holding on to the letters because she still loved him; she was holding on because she hadn't forgiven herself for how the relationship ended. The letters represented unfinished emotional business. Kondo's instruction: read each letter, acknowledge the relationship, thank it for what it taught you, and release it. Not because the relationship was meaningless — because its meaning is complete.

**The Trap** with sentimental items is that we keep them as emotional insurance policies. We're afraid that letting go of the object means letting go of the memory. Kondo's counter is firm: the memory lives in you, not in the thing. You don't forget your grandmother when you give away her china. You honor her by keeping the connection alive in your heart and freeing your home of objects you don't actually use.

The book also addresses the practical challenge of shared living spaces. Kondo advises tidying your own things first, completely, before addressing shared areas. Your clarity and example will eventually influence your partner or family — but you can't force the process on someone else. In fact, when you demonstrate joy-based living, others naturally become curious.

The honest criticism: **Spark Joy** is more reference manual than narrative. Nobody is sitting down to read this cover to cover. It's a book you flip through, find your current problem (how to fold socks, how to organize the bathroom, how to handle gifts you don't want), and put back on the shelf. As a read, it's repetitive — Kondo explains the same six rules in slightly different language across multiple chapters. The illustrations, while charming, sometimes confuse more than they clarify — particularly for complex items like the fitted sheet and layered kimono folding. You'll likely need a YouTube video to supplement the diagrams.

The illustrated format is its strength and its limitation. The drawings are charming but sometimes unclear — the fitted sheet folding technique, in particular, takes several attempts to master from the diagrams. Video tutorials are probably more useful for the folding sections.

There's also a cultural specificity gap that widens with this book. Some of the komono categories are distinctly Japanese — specific types of kitchen implements and ceremonial items that don't apply to Western homes. The translation tries to bridge this but sometimes lands in odd places.

The book also assumes you already own the first book — and frankly, you should. **Spark Joy** is a supplement, not a standalone work. If you haven't absorbed the philosophy of the KonMari method, the practical instructions can feel arbitrary. Why fold clothes this way? Because this is the method. But without the deeper "why" from the first book, the folding just looks like an elaborate way to organize a drawer.

The deepest insight that stayed with me: joy is not the same as happiness. Happiness is a reaction to external circumstances. Joy is a quieter signal — a sense of aliveness and rightness that comes from being surrounded by things that genuinely serve and delight you. The KonMari method is not about creating a life of constant happiness. It's about creating a life where the objects around you support rather than drain you.

The idea I couldn't shake: discarding is not about loss. Discarding is about completion. Every object enters your life for a purpose, fulfills that purpose (or doesn't), and then the relationship ends. Kondo's innovation is giving us a ritual for that ending. The "thank you" you say to a discarded item isn't for the object's sake. It's for yours — it closes the loop and frees you from the nagging guilt of unfinished business.

**Fold one drawer the KonMari way.** Pick any drawer with folded clothes. Empty it. Fold each item into a compact rectangle. Stand them vertically. See how much more you can see.

**Empty one komono category.** Pick the smallest miscellaneous category you own — cables, beauty products, spices, whatever. Empty the entire category onto the floor. Handle each item. Discard anything that doesn't spark joy or hasn't been used in a year.

**Identify your most stubborn sentimental item.** Pick one thing you keep out of guilt or obligation rather than genuine joy. Sit with it. Decide: does this serve my present life? If not, thank it and set it free.
