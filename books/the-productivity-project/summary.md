Chris Bailey conducted a year-long personal experiment in productivity. He woke at 5:30 AM, meditated for three hours daily, worked 90-hour weeks, used his smartphone for only one hour per day, and tried almost every productivity system that exists. Then he wrote a book about what actually worked — and more importantly, what didn't. *The Productivity Project* is the diary of that year, and its strength is that it reads like someone who tried everything and came back with a realistic report, not a manifesto.

Bailey was 19 when he started the project, a Carleton University business student who realized that most productivity advice is theoretical. He decided to become the test subject. Over 12 months, he ran structured experiments on himself, measuring before and after, tracking energy, output, satisfaction, and mood. The result is a book that feels more like science journalism with a narrator than a self-help prescription.

Bailey grounds everything in a definition: **productivity** is "accomplishing what you intend to accomplish." That's it. Not doing more. Not being busier. Not optimizing every minute. Accomplishing what *you* intend. This definition does heavy lifting because it immediately removes guilt about "not doing enough" — if what you intended was to rest, and you rested, you were productive.

> "Productivity isn't about doing more or doing it faster. It's about making the best use of your time, energy, and attention."

The book is organized around three pillars: **Time**, **Attention**, and **Energy**. Bailey argues that most productivity systems focus only on time management, which is the least important of the three. You can have all the time in the world, but without attention and energy, you're just filling hours with low-quality output.

**Time** is the easiest to manage, the most democratically distributed (everyone gets 24 hours), and the least flexible. You can't create more of it. You can only choose how to spend it.

**Attention** is harder. It's the quality of your mental focus. A given hour can produce wildly different output depending on how much attention you bring to it. Bailey argues that attention is more valuable than time — an hour of focused work beats three hours of distracted work.

**Energy** is the forgotten factor. Your physical, mental, and emotional energy determine what you're capable of in any given moment. The same two-hour block can produce brilliant work if you're energized and useless output if you're depleted.

**The Trap** of traditional productivity is managing time while ignoring attention and energy. You optimize your calendar but show up to meetings exhausted. You schedule deep work but have the attention span of a gnat. You feel like the system failed, but what failed was your understanding of the problem.

Bailey's experiments yield some surprising results. The **90-minute work block** emerges as the optimal unit of productive time. This aligns with ultradian rhythms — the natural cycles of peak performance that last roughly 90 minutes before your brain needs a break. Bailey's self-tracking confirmed what the research shows: productivity doesn't decline gradually over a long work session. It falls off a cliff after about 90 minutes.

> "Working more hours doesn't result in more productivity. It results in less."

He also discovered the power of **shrinking your projects**. When Bailey reduced his weekly work hours from 60 to 30, his output actually increased. The constraint forced him to prioritize ruthlessly. Nothing focuses the mind like knowing you have half the time you thought you had.

The book's most practical section is on **making your work more difficult** — intentionally. Bailey noticed that easy work (email, calls, light editing) often got done faster while important work languished. His solution: create artificial constraints. Set a timer. Tell someone you'll deliver by noon. Use a smaller space to write. The difficulty creates pressure that forces engagement.

**The Trap** here is that this only works if you're capable of handling pressure. For people prone to anxiety, adding constraints doesn't sharpen focus — it triggers paralysis. Bailey's experiments were run on a motivated, healthy 20-year-old. Your mileage may vary.

Bailey dedicates a chapter to **procrastination** that's worth the price of entry. He argues that procrastination isn't a time management problem — it's an **emotion management problem**. You procrastinate not because you're lazy but because the task triggers negative emotions (anxiety, boredom, confusion, overwhelm). The "solution" isn't more discipline. It's emotional regulation.

His technique: **identify the emotion, then address it specifically.** Anxious about a task? Write down exactly what you're anxious about. Bored? Find a way to make it more interesting (turn it into a game, set a challenge). Overwhelmed? Break it into the smallest possible next action. Confused? Research for exactly 15 minutes to clear the fog.

**Multi-tasking** gets a thorough dismantling. Bailey tested his own ability to do two things at once and found, predictably, that he was terrible at it. The attention residue from each switch cost 20-30 minutes. He notes that the people who think they're good at multitasking are almost always the worst at it — the Dunning-Kruger effect of productivity.

Bailey's **Rule of 3** is simple and sticky: at the start of your day, identify the three most important things you want to accomplish. At the start of your week, identify the three most important things you want to accomplish. Do those three things before you touch anything else. That's it. The elegance is that it's flexible enough for any context and demanding enough to force real prioritization.

**The Trap** is that people expand their Rule of 3 to a Rule of 10. The rule works because of the constraint. Three things. Not the three most urgent. The three most *important*. Important is different from urgent. Important moves your life forward. Urgent just makes noise.

The book covers **energy management** extensively: sleep, exercise, nutrition, and even social interaction as productivity inputs. Bailey's experiment with sleep deprivation is telling — after nights of 5-6 hours of sleep, his productivity dropped by 30-40%, but his *perception* of his productivity stayed high. He felt fine. He just produced less. This is the hidden cost of chronic sleep deprivation: you don't feel the loss, but it's there.

Bailey also experiments with **digital detoxes**: going without his smartphone for a week, then a month. The results are predictable (he felt more present, more focused, less anxious) but the *duration of the effect* is surprising — the benefits kept accumulating for weeks after the detox ended.

> "When you work, work. When you play, play. Don't mix the two."

The book's weaknesses are structural. It's too long for what it says. The experiment format means Bailey includes every dead end and failed attempt, which is honest but bloated. A stronger editor could have cut 100 pages. Some experiments are genuinely boring — we don't need three pages on what happened when Bailey tried drinking more water.

The "I tried everything" framing is also a double-edged sword. It gives the book credibility, but it also means Bailey doesn't have deep expertise in any one area. He's a smart generalist writing about topics that have dedicated experts. His sleep chapter can't compete with Matthew Walker. His focus chapter cites Newport. His willpower chapter cites Baumeister. Bailey is a curator, not a primary source, and the book would have benefited from owning that identity more clearly.

There's also a quiet assumption throughout that the reader has a knowledge-worker job with significant autonomy. The Rule of 3 doesn't work if your day is dictated by a manager or a queue of customers. Bailey doesn't address this.

The idea that productivity is about managing energy and attention, not time. The 90-minute work block as the optimal unit. The Rule of 3. And the redefinition of procrastination as emotional avoidance rather than laziness — that alone has helped more people than any scheduling hack.

People who've tried a dozen productivity systems and none have stuck. It's the book you read after you've read GTD and still feel overwhelmed. Experienced productivity readers who already know the landscape — there's little here that isn't stated better in a more focused book on a specific aspect.

1. List your three most important goals for this week. Not the urgent ones. The important ones. Write them down. Do nothing else until these three are done.
2. Tomorrow, work in 90-minute blocks. Set a timer. After 90 minutes, take a mandatory 15-20 minute break — walk away from your desk, no screens. Repeat.
3. Identify one task you're procrastinating on. Ask yourself: what emotion am I avoiding? (Anxiety? Boredom? Confusion? Overwhelm?) Address the emotion, not the task.
4. Track your energy levels for two days. Mark every hour from 1-10. Schedule your hardest work for your peak energy periods. Schedule low-cognition tasks (email, admin) for your low-energy times.
