In 1925, the most famous British explorer of his generation, Colonel Percy Harrison Fawcett, walked into the Amazon rainforest with his eldest son and a young friend named Raleigh Rimell. They were looking for a lost civilization — an ancient city Fawcett had named simply "Z," hidden somewhere in the Mato Grosso region of Brazil. They never came out. The search for Fawcett became one of the great mysteries of the twentieth century, spawning rescue expeditions, conspiracy theories, and the deaths of at least a hundred would-be rescuers who vanished trying to find the man who had vanished. David Grann, a staff writer at The New Yorker, became obsessed with the story and decided to retrace Fawcett's last journey, armed with historical records, satellite technology, and a healthy dose of skepticism about whether the lost city existed at all.

The Lost City of Z is that rare work of nonfiction that works as a biography of an obsession, a detective story, an adventure narrative, and a revisionist history of Amazonian archaeology. It reads like a thriller because it is one, but the most thrilling thing about it is Grann's willingness to discover that the hero he started out admiring was something more complicated — and that the lost city might actually have been found.

Fawcett was the real Indiana Jones — and I don't use that comparison lightly. He was a decorated military officer, a skilled cartographer, and a man of almost supernatural endurance. In his twenties, he spent years surveying jungles and deserts. He mapped borders for the Bolivian government. He survived attacks by piranhas, electric eels, and indigenous tribes. He once trekked for weeks with a broken rib. He was tall, gaunt, and utterly fearless, with a gaze that made men uncomfortable and women swoon. He was also, as Grann reveals with careful restraint, a man whose judgment was clouded by a mystical belief system that bordered on the irrational.

Fawcett had been fascinated by the Amazon since reading the accounts of early Spanish conquistadors who claimed to have seen vast cities of gold in the interior. He refused to believe that the Amazon was a "green desert" — the prevailing theory among archaeologists, who argued that the soil was too poor to support complex civilizations. Fawcett was certain that a great civilization had once existed there, and that evidence of it was still waiting to be discovered. He was right about that, as Grann eventually proves. But he was also wrong about almost everything else, including his own survival.

*"Fawcett had a quality that is essential for any explorer: the ability to believe in something that everyone else dismisses as impossible."*

**The Trap:** It's tempting to read Fawcett as a tragic genius, a man ahead of his time who died for his vision. But Grann shows the underside: Fawcett was arrogant, dogmatic, and willing to risk not only his own life but his son's life for a theory he couldn't prove. The line between visionary and fool is thinner than we'd like.

Grann's accounts of the Amazon itself are as gripping as any thriller I've read. He describes the conditions Fawcett faced with the precision of a reporter who has done his research and the vividness of a novelist. The heat is relentless. The insects are a constant, maddening assault. Every river hides something venomous. The forest presses in from all sides, dense and green and utterly indifferent to human will.

But the real horror is what the jungle does to the mind. Grann documents the experiences of explorers who lost their grip on reality after weeks in the green darkness. Hallucinations were common. Paranoia was inevitable. Several search parties for Fawcett reported hearing gunshots, seeing signals, and following trails that led nowhere. The jungle doesn't just hide things. It creates illusions.

Grann's own journey into the Amazon is the book's climax, and it's brilliantly structured. He hires a guide, hires porters, and hires a local expert. He carries modern equipment — GPS, satellite phones, medical supplies. And he still nearly dies. His feet rot from the constant moisture. He runs out of food. He loses his sense of direction. At one point, he falls into a creek and feels something brush against his leg. He doesn't find out what it was. He's grateful for that.

*"The jungle is not a place you visit. It is a place that enters you — through your pores, your lungs, your dreams. And it never leaves."*

**The Trap:** Grann's own journey narrative is compelling, but it's worth asking: what does it mean for a Western journalist to "conquer" the same terrain that defeated Fawcett? The book doesn't fully interrogate the ethics of adventure tourism in places where people still die from lack of basic healthcare.

The mystery of what happened to Fawcett has spawned more theories than the Kennedy assassination. He was killed by indigenous tribes. He was killed by animals. He went mad and wandered into the swamp. He found Z and stayed there voluntarily. He was captured by German agents. He was eaten by cannibals. Grann methodically examines each theory, weighing evidence, testing timelines, and interviewing experts. The process is the intellectual backbone of the book, and it's a masterclass in investigative journalism.

The most convincing theory — supported by new evidence Grann uncovered — is that Fawcett was killed by the Kalapalo tribe, not out of hostility but out of a cultural logic that the outside world couldn't understand. The Kalapalo had a practice of killing outsiders who violated their protocols, but they also had a tradition of honoring the dead by incorporating them into their oral histories. Grann's interviews with the Kalapalo elders, conducted through translators and requiring immense patience, are the book's most moving passages. The tribe didn't hate Fawcett. They remembered him. They remembered killing him. And they remembered it as a necessary act of self-preservation, not a crime.

*"The Kalapalo do not see death as we do. For them, the boundary between the living and the dead is porous. Fawcett is not gone. He is simply elsewhere."*

**The Trap:** The Kalapalo explanation is satisfying on a narrative level, but Grann acknowledges it's incomplete. No physical evidence of Fawcett's remains has ever been found. The oral history is compelling but unverifiable. The mystery may never be fully solved, and Grann's willingness to leave it open is the mark of a good journalist.

And this is where the book delivers its biggest surprise. Fawcett was right. Not about the specific city he was looking for, but about the Amazon's capacity to sustain complex civilizations. In the decades since Fawcett's disappearance, archaeologists using LIDAR and satellite imagery have discovered extensive earthworks, roads, and settlements across the Amazon basin. The "green desert" theory has been overturned. The Amazon was home to millions of people before European contact, living in walled cities, practicing agriculture, and engineering the landscape on a massive scale.

Fawcett's Z wasn't one city. It was an entire civilization that Western science had refused to see. The irony is devastating: Fawcett died looking for something that was real, but he died because his methods were too primitive and his worldview too romantic to find it properly. His intuition was right. His execution was fatal.

What stayed with me was the double story: a man who was right about the big thing and wrong about the small one, and a journalist who risked his own safety to complete a research assignment. Grann doesn't pretend to be an explorer. He's a writer who followed a thread to its end, and the thread led him into the jungle. The humility of that stance — the willingness to be afraid, to be lost, to be wrong — is what elevates the book above the adventure genre.

The book's weakness is Grann's own presence. He's a fine reporter but a less compelling character than Fawcett, and the sections where he's the protagonist occasionally feel like the book wants to be two things at once. The historical chapters are stronger than the contemporary ones. And the ending — Grann's return from the Amazon, his reflections on what he's learned — is too brief, as if he was eager to be done.
