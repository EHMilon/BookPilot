There's a story James C. Humes tells about Winston Churchill that gets at the heart of this book. Churchill, the giant of 20th-century oratory, spent hours every morning in bed — not sleeping, but *working*. Propped up with pillows, a cigar in hand, he would dictate, revise, dictate again. He wrote every speech with the same care a poet gives a sonnet. And then, when he delivered those speeches, people thought he was improvising.

That's the real art. The art of making the prepared feel spontaneous. The art of making the crafted feel natural. **Speak Like Churchill, Stand Like Lincoln** is James C. Humes's attempt to pass on the specific, repeatable techniques that history's greatest speakers used — not the theory of rhetoric, but the *moves*.

Humes is uniquely positioned to write this book. He was a presidential speechwriter (Eisenhower, Nixon, Ford, Reagan), a professor of rhetoric, and a student of oratorical history. He doesn't just know the theory. He's watched these techniques work in the highest-stakes rooms in the world.

> "The great speakers are not born. They are made. And they are made by using the same time-tested tools."

Humes identifies one pattern that appears in almost every great speech in history: the rule of three. Three points. Three examples. Three clauses. The brain processes triads more naturally than any other number. "Government of the people, by the people, for the people." "Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness." "*Veni, vidi, vici.*"

The rule of three works because it creates a sense of completeness without closure. Two feels short. Four feels like a list. Three feels *just right* — enough to make a point, not enough to exhaust attention.

Humes advises speakers to structure every major point as a triad. Three arguments for a proposal. Three stories that illustrate a theme. Three characteristics of a leader. Not only does the audience remember triads, but they *feel* satisfied by them.

> "Three is the smallest number the mind can perceive as a pattern."

**The Trap:** Speakers try to cover everything. They make five points, then a sixth, then an aside. The audience remembers none of them. Humes's rule: find your three best points. Use them. Throw the rest away.

The shift: for your next speech, identify exactly three things you want the audience to remember. Structure everything around those three.

Humes says that most speakers waste their first thirty seconds on pleasantries. "Thank you for having me. It's a pleasure to be here." Meanwhile, the audience is deciding whether to pay attention or check their phone. The first thirty seconds are the *most valuable real estate* in a speech.

Humes catalogs the great openers from history. Lincoln's Gettysburg Address: "Four score and seven years ago..." — a single sentence that transported the audience to 1776. Churchill's "We shall fight on the beaches" — a list of specific images that painted a nation's resolve. John F. Kennedy's "Ask not what your country can do for you" — a direct challenge that flipped the expected script.

The common thread: great openers don't warm up. They *strike*.

> "The first sentence of your speech should be your most carefully crafted sentence."

**The Trap:** We feel the need to "ease in." We thank the introducer, acknowledge the organization, tell a joke. By the time we get to the point, the audience has already checked out. Humes's counter: start with your strongest story, your most provocative question, or your most startling fact. You can thank people *after* you've earned their attention.

The shift: write your opening sentence before anything else. Make it impossible to ignore.

Churchill's greatest speeches are not abstract. They are *pictures*. "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills." He didn't say "we will resist vigorously." He showed you *where*. Beaches. Streets. Hills. You can see them.

Humes calls this the principle of *the specific over the general*. The human brain is not designed to process abstractions. It's designed to process images. When you say "freedom," the brain searches for an image. When you say "a man standing in his own doorway, unafraid," the brain *sees* it.

> "Use words that summon pictures. The picture-making word is the shortest path to the heart."

**The Trap:** We use the language of business — "optimize," "leverage," "synergize." These words have no images attached. They bounce off the brain like rubber off concrete. The audience may *understand* them, but they won't *feel* them.

The shift: before you deliver any abstract word, ask yourself: *what does this look like?* Say that instead.

Lincoln's most famous speeches are masterclasses in brevity. The Gettysburg Address is 272 words. The Second Inaugural is 703 words. But Humes points out that the real genius isn't what Lincoln said — it's what he *didn't* say. Lincoln used silence as a weapon.

At the climax of the Second Inaugural — "with malice toward none, with charity for all" — Lincoln paused before saying those words. The pause told the audience: *what comes next is not an afterthought. It is the point of everything.*

Humes's advice: when you have a line that matters, say it, then *stop*. Let the silence do the work. The audience will fill the pause with their own thought, their own emotion, their own commitment. Rushing to the next sentence steals that moment.

> "The pause is the most powerful punctuation mark in public speaking."

**The Trap:** We hate silence. A one-second pause feels like an eternity to the speaker. To the audience, it feels like *emphasis*. The speaker who hurries through their best line is the speaker who buries their own treasure.

The shift: identify your most important line in your next talk. Say it. Then count to three before continuing.

Humes worked with Ronald Reagan and devotes significant space to his approach. Reagan was called "The Great Communicator," but Humes reveals it wasn't a gift — it was a *system*. Reagan never spoke without a story.

The story of the boy who forgot his lines at a school play. The story of the Marine who carried his wounded comrade. The story of the grandmother who prayed for peace. These stories weren't digressions — they were the *argument*. Reagan's policy positions were often weak in detail, but his stories made people *feel* that his positions were right.

Humes distills this into a principle: *never make a point you can't illustrate with a story.* If you can't find a story for it, you probably haven't thought about it deeply enough.

> "A story is a fact wrapped in an emotion that can make a president — or break one."

**The Trap:** We separate logic from narrative. "First I'll give the facts, then a story to illustrate." But the best speakers weave story and argument together so tightly they're inseparable. The story *is* the argument.

The shift: for every major point in your next speech, find a specific story that proves it. Don't make the point without the story.

Humes shows that one of Kennedy's favorite devices was *antithesis* — setting two opposing ideas side by side. "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country." The contrast creates energy. It surprises. It makes both sides sharper.

The structure is simple: "Not this, but that." "Not for the few, but for the many." "Not the end of a journey, but the beginning." The opposition creates a *click* in the listener's mind — a moment of recognition that makes the point unforgettable.

> "Antithesis is the figure of speech that makes a phrase memorable."

**The Trap:** We state our positions directly. "We need to work together." Kennedy's version: "Let us not negotiate out of fear, but let us never fear to negotiate." Same meaning. Different impact. The direct statement is forgotten. The antithesis is quoted for generations.

The shift: take one of your key messages and rephrase it as an antithesis. "Not [thing A], but [thing B]."

**The core takeaway** of **Speak Like Churchill, Stand Like Lincoln** is that great public speaking is not a mystery. It's a set of techniques — devices that have been used by the best speakers for centuries. The rule of three. The specific image. The strategic pause. The story. The antithesis. You don't need to be born with a silver tongue. You need to learn the moves and practice them.

**What will actually stick** is the *specificity* of the advice. Most public speaking books tell you to "be authentic" or "connect with your audience." Humes tells you to use three points, open with a story, and pause before your climax. It's not poetic advice. It's *usable* advice. You can test it tomorrow.

**The honest criticism** is that the book is a collection of examples in search of a theory. Humes is brilliant at identifying what great speakers did, but weaker at explaining *why* the techniques work. The book lacks a unifying framework — it's more of a toolbox than a system. Some chapters feel thin, as though Humes ran out of material and stretched a single example into a chapter. The book also assumes a specific kind of speaking: the formal, podium-based, political or business address. It doesn't address digital speaking, panel discussions, or conversational influence well.
