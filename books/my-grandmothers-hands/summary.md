Here's a question you've probably never been asked: where, in your body, do you carry the trauma of your ancestors? Not your personal pain. The pain that was passed down through blood and bone, through habits of survival, through the way your grandmother held her shoulders or clenched her jaw when she didn't feel safe. And here's the harder question: what if that trauma is not just *in* you — but is actively shaping the politics of an entire country, and has been for four hundred years?

Resmaa Menakem is a trauma therapist who spent decades working with the most violent and broken bodies in America — gang members, veterans, abused children — before he realized that he was treating symptoms, not causes. The cause, he argues, is a collective wound that lives not in our heads but in our **nervous systems**: the trauma of white-body supremacy, passed down generationally in Black, white, and police bodies alike. *My Grandmother's Hands* is not a book about racism as an idea or a system. It's a book about racism as a *physiological* reality — something you can feel in your gut, your chest, your clenched teeth, and your fight-or-flight response.

---

Menakem's central claim is radically simple: the racism that shapes America is not primarily ideological. It's **somatic** — stored in the body's nervous system as a survival response. Black bodies learned to constrict, to brace, to hyper-vigilance to survive centuries of violence. White bodies learned a different constriction — the numbing, the disassociation, the "I don't see color" that is actually a refusal to feel the truth of what is happening. For four hundred years, these responses have been passed down not just through culture, but through actual biological inheritance — epigenetic changes, stress-response patterns, the way a mother's cortisol shapes her child's developing nervous system before birth.

*Trauma is not a flaw or a weakness. It is a highly effective tool of protection. The problem is that it also restricts us, narrows our lives, and reduces our ability to function.*

This is the book's most important reframe. Menakem isn't pathologizing the trauma response. He's honoring its intelligence. The body's hyper-vigilance kept Black ancestors alive through lynchings and whippings and daily humiliations. The white body's disassociation (what he calls **"the fragmented white body"**) protected white ancestors from having to fully feel the horror of what they were participating in. These responses worked. They saved lives. But they also froze the nervous system in a state of chronic activation that gets passed down like a family heirloom nobody asked for.

The book introduces a concept called **"clean pain"** vs. **"dirty pain."** Clean pain is the discomfort of facing a hard truth — it hurts, but it heals. Dirty pain is the familiar, addictive loop of avoidance, blame, and retraumatization. Most of our collective conversations about race, Menakem argues, are dirty pain rituals. We perform outrage, we tweet our condemnations, we feel righteous — and nothing changes. The nervous system stays locked in its old patterns. Clean pain is quieter. It means sitting with your own body's response without numbing, without blaming, without performing. It's harder. It's also the only way out.

*Clean pain is the pain that leads to growth and healing. Dirty pain is the pain of avoidance, blame, and the refusal to look at what is.*

The book is structured around Menakem's **Soulnercising** — body-based practices designed to settle the nervous system. These are not metaphors. He gives specific, concrete instructions: grounding exercises (feet on the floor, hands on your thighs, feeling your weight), breathing patterns (long exhales to activate the vagus nerve), movement sequences (shaking, stretching, yawning). His argument is that you cannot think your way out of trauma. You have to *feel* your way out — in the literal, physical sense of learning to inhabit your body again instead of living in your head. He argues that the obsession with "understanding" racism through books and lectures is itself a white-body habit — a way of staying in the intellect to avoid the discomfort of the body.

One of the most powerful sections is Menakem's analysis of **police bodies**. He argues that police violence is not primarily about individual racism or bad training — though both exist. It's about the physiological collision between a hyper-vigilant Black body and a hyper-aroused white police body, both operating from trauma responses they don't know they have. The cop's nervous system reads a Black man's body language (shaped by generations of survival hyper-vigilance) as a threat. The Black man's nervous system reads the cop's approach (shaped by generations of white-body dominance) as a threat. Two traumatized nervous systems meet, and the result is predictable.

*When two nervous systems meet, one of two things happens: they create a groove of comfort and safety, or they bounce off each other in escalating distress.*

This reframe is both liberating and deeply uncomfortable. It's liberating because it de-escalates moral judgment — we're not talking about "good people" versus "bad people," but about bodies responding to danger signals they can't consciously control. It's uncomfortable because it means that no amount of diversity training or consciousness-raising lectures will fix the problem. The solution is *somatic*: we have to learn to settle our nervous systems, individually and collectively, before we can have the conversations that need to happen.

Menakem devotes a significant section to what he calls **"the white body's work."** White Americans, he argues, have a particular obligation to heal their own trauma — not because Black people need them to, but because the white body's disassociation from its own history is what maintains the system. The white body that flinches from talking about race, that goes numb when confronted with its own complicity, that reaches for guilt as a way to avoid action — that body is the engine of white-body supremacy. He doesn't ask white readers to feel guilty. He asks them to *feel*, period. To stop numbing, stop performing, stop hiding in intellectual analysis. To learn to inhabit their own bodies so fully that they can finally be present for the truth of what happened and is still happening.

*The white body has been trained for centuries to believe that it is separate from the Black body, when in fact every white body in America is deeply entangled with Black bodies — through history, through violence, through care, through proximity.*

The book has genuine weaknesses. The writing can be repetitive — Menakem circles back to the same concepts many times, sometimes in almost identical language. The Soulnercising exercises, while valuable in practice, can feel simplistic when reduced to writing. "Breathe. Feel your feet. Notice your heartbeat" reads like basic mindfulness, and readers familiar with somatic therapy will find little new here. The book also lacks the historical breadth it gestures toward — it's strong on the present moment and the therapeutic frame, but thin on the actual four hundred years of history it invokes. It occasionally overcorrects from intellectualism into anti-intellectualism, as if reading and thinking were themselves the problem rather than just incomplete solutions.

But the core thesis is one of the most important contributions to racial justice thinking in the last decade. Menakem is asking us to stop debating racism as an abstraction and start *feeling* it as a lived, bodily reality. This is not a comfortable ask. It's meant not to be.

**The Trap** is the "both sides" misinterpretation. Menakem is careful: "both sides have trauma" does not mean "both sides are equally responsible." The Black body's trauma is a response to being hunted. The white body's trauma is a response to being the hunter (or the bystander to the hunt) and not wanting to feel it. Menakem is not moralizing, but neither is he equivocating. The work white bodies have to do is different from the work Black bodies have to do, and pretending otherwise is itself an act of clean-pain avoidance.
