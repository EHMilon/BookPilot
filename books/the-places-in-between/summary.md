In January 2002, just three months after the fall of the Taliban, a 28-year-old Scottish former diplomat named Rory Stewart walked across Afghanistan. Not drove. Not traveled with a convoy. Walked. From Herat to Kabul, through the heart of the central highlands, across snow-covered passes at 12,000 feet, through villages that had never seen a foreigner, through territory controlled by warlords who had been fighting for twenty years and didn't particularly care about the new government. He had no security, no backup, no satellite phone. He had a sleeping bag, a wool hat, a set of local clothes, and the willingness to rely entirely on the hospitality of strangers in a country that had just emerged from a generation of war.

The Places in Between is the account of that walk, and it's one of the most extraordinary travel books of the twenty-first century. Stewart is writing about something that barely exists anymore: a kind of travel that is so physically demanding, so culturally immersive, and so politically naive that it could only be attempted by someone young enough to believe he was invincible and smart enough to know he wasn't. The book is a journey narrative, a study of Afghan society, and a meditation on what it means to be a foreigner in a place that has every reason to distrust you.

Stewart's explanation for why he walked across Afghanistan is frustratingly vague, which is the first honest thing about the book. He says he wanted to see the country. He says he was drawn to the mountains. He says he wanted to follow in the footsteps of earlier travelers, particularly the Mughal emperor Babur, who walked through these same passes five hundred years ago. None of these explanations are fully satisfying, and Stewart knows it. The walk is an act of pure, stubborn curiosity — the kind that can't be justified in practical terms because it doesn't arise from practical motives.

The absurdity of the undertaking becomes clear immediately. He's walking alone through a country where bandits, Taliban remnants, and local militias all operate with impunity. The snow is waist-deep in places. He doesn't speak the local dialects fluently. He's dependent on strangers for food, shelter, and directions. Every night he has to find a village and ask for a place to sleep, never knowing whether he'll be welcomed or turned away into the cold.

*"I had no right to be in Afghanistan. I had no real reason to be there. I was walking through the heart of the country because I wanted to, because I was curious, because I was young, and because I didn't know any better."*

**The Trap:** The book has been criticized for romanticizing a kind of colonial-era exploration — the lone Westerner testing himself against a "savage" landscape. Stewart is aware of this and addresses it directly, but the framing is still there. The walk is an adventure for him. For the Afghans he meets, it's just survival.

The book's most important contribution is its documentation of what Stewart calls "the Afghan code" — the elaborate system of hospitality that governs social interaction in rural Afghanistan. Every village he enters offers him food and shelter, not because they trust him but because the code demands it. The host is obligated to provide for the guest, and the guest is obligated to accept without complaint. The system is beautiful and exhausting: beautiful because it creates bonds across impossible divides, exhausting because it never stops.

Stewart's descriptions of these encounters are the book's heart. He's welcomed by a widow who has lost her husband to the war and shares her last piece of bread with him. He's hosted by a village elder who interrogates him for three hours about British politics before allowing him to sleep. He's given shelter by a man who has just lost his son to a landmine and insists on feeding him anyway because turning away a traveler would dishonor his family. The hospitality is never simple. It's always weighted with grief, suspicion, and the thousand small calculations of a society that has survived by knowing exactly who to trust.

*"The Afghan guest is not a customer. He is a responsibility. You feed him, you protect him, and you ask nothing in return. To do less would be to break the deepest law of the land."*

**The Trap:** Stewart is honest about the dark side of hospitality: the same villages that welcomed him would kill a stranger who broke the code. The system works because it's enforced with violence. The warmth of Afghan hospitality exists alongside a brutal capacity for punishment, and Stewart doesn't flinch from showing both sides.

The most recurring and visceral threat in the book isn't the Taliban, the warlords, or the snow. It's the dogs. Afghan village dogs are semi-wild, aggressive, and trained to attack strangers. Stewart is attacked repeatedly, and his descriptions of these encounters are terrifying. He carries a stick and learns to fight them off, but he's bitten, cornered, and humiliated more times than he can count. The dogs become a symbol of the country itself: unpredictable, hostile to outsiders, but capable of being calmed if you know the right approach.

Stewart's relationship with the dogs evolves over the course of the walk. Early on, he's afraid. Middle of the journey, he's resigned. By the end, he's developed a kind of perverse respect for them. They're part of the landscape, as real and as indifferent as the mountains.

*"I dreamed of war and the dead, but I was kept awake by dogs. They were the real rulers of Afghanistan, not the warlords."*

**The Trap:** The dog passages, as vivid as they are, can overshadow the human stories. Stewart spends more pages on dogs than on some of the people he meets. It's a choice that works as narrative but can feel like a displacement of attention from the real costs of the war.

Stewart is walking through Afghanistan in the immediate aftermath of the US invasion. The Taliban has been overthrown but hasn't been replaced by anything stable. Villages are still armed. Warlords are still in control of their territories. The new government in Kabul barely extends beyond the city limits. Into this walks a young Westerner, a former diplomat, asking to be fed.

The political dimension of the walk is inescapable. Stewart is constantly asked why the West invaded, what the new government will do, and whether the Americans are going to stay. He has no answers that satisfy anyone. His presence is a political statement regardless of his intentions. He's a representative of the world that has decided to intervene in Afghanistan, and he has to carry the weight of that intervention even when he disagrees with it.

The most politically acute moment comes when Stewart realizes that his walk is being interpreted as a form of diplomacy. Village elders assume he's a spy or an envoy, and they treat him accordingly. He tries to explain that he's just a traveler, but the distinction is meaningless in a country where every foreigner is a potential agent of power. The book is, among other things, a meditation on the impossibility of apolitical travel in a political world.

*"They couldn't understand why anyone would walk through their country without a political purpose. And perhaps they were right. Perhaps there is no such thing as a walk without politics."*

**The Trap:** Stewart's later political career — he served as a Conservative MP and a UK government minister — has been used to reframe the book as the origin story of a politician. Stewart resists this reading, and the book deserves to be read on its own terms, but the connection is real. The walk gave him a kind of authority he wouldn't otherwise have had.

The idea I couldn't shake was the weight of presence — what it means to show up somewhere not as a tourist, not as a journalist, not as a representative of power, but as a person who has walked. Stewart's method of travel — slow, vulnerable, dependent — forced him into relationships that no faster form of travel could have produced. He couldn't leave when things got uncomfortable. He couldn't skip the difficult conversations. He had to be present in every village, every encounter, every moment of hospitality and hostility. The book is a case for the unexpected power of moving slowly through a place, particularly one you don't understand.

The book's weakness is its structure. The chronology is linear — day by day on the walk — and while this creates momentum, it can also feel repetitive. Stewart's encounters blur together by the middle of the book. The political reflections, while sharp, are unevenly distributed. And the ending — Stewart's arrival in Kabul — is strangely anticlimactic, as if he didn't know how to close a journey that never had a clear purpose.
